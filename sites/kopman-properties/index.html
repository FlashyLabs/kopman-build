01 — The Thesis

A generation of stock
that stopped working.

The Greater Toronto Area built almost no small industrial for thirty years. What exists is old, and much of it was designed for a way of moving goods that no longer exists.

More than four fifths of the region's shallow-bay industrial — buildings under 50,000 square feet with 14 to 28 feet of clear height — was built before 2000. Close to half of it predates 1980. Only about one part in twenty has been delivered since 2010, because almost every square foot of new supply went to large distribution boxes instead.

Meanwhile the buildings themselves aged out of usefulness. A modern distribution occupier expects roughly one dock door for every 5,000 to 10,000 square feet. Buildings at 18 to 24 feet clear already command materially lower rents. Below 18 feet, the industry treats a building as effectively obsolete for logistics.

And a building with no truck-level door at all is in a category of its own. It is not merely worth less per square foot. For most of the market it is not a candidate at any price — because the tenant's freight arrives on a trailer whose floor sits four and a half feet above the ground, and there is nothing to back it up to.

That is the gap. Sound structure, good location, employment zoning, serviced land — and a configuration that excludes almost every occupier who would otherwise want it. The building is not tired. It is locked.

What we acquire

  • Grade-level drive-in doors only, or one or two truck-level positions on a building that needs six
  • 16–24 ft clear, pre-2000 construction, often a former manufacturing or service occupier
  • Fitted out for a use nobody else has — overhead cranes, wash bays, pits, heavy power in the wrong place
  • Tired roof, dated lighting, single service, no sub-metering
  • Yard used for parking and storage rather than for trucks
  • One tenant, or empty, with no way to divide it

What we deliver

  • A ganged bank of truck-level docks with hydraulic levellers, shelters and bumpers
  • A truck court built to take a 53-foot trailer — 130 ft minimum, concrete at the apron
  • De-fitted, cleaned and returned to a general-purpose warehouse envelope
  • LED throughout, upgraded service, sub-metering per unit
  • Demised into small-bay units, each with its own dock, service and washroom
  • A building six occupiers can use instead of none
02 — The Refinement

What we got wrong
the first time.

The opening version of this thesis was: buy warehouses without docks, add docks, charge more rent. We costed it properly against Ontario conditions. The premise held. The mechanism did not.

A six-dock conversion on a 30,000 square foot building carries roughly $2.06 million of dock and truck-court scope once contingency and professional fees are loaded — about $68.80 per square foot of building, or some $344,000 per dock position all in. At a 6.5 per cent capitalisation rate, that spend needs a $4.47 per square foot rent lift simply to break even.

The plausible rent premium for dock-loaded over grade-only small-bay space in this market is somewhere between $1.50 and $3.50 — and no published figure for it exists anywhere we could find. Judged as a rent play, the trade is under water before it starts.

So we stopped judging it as a rent play.

Refinement 01

Docks are not a rent story. They are a leasability story.

The value is not created by charging a premium on space you could already lease. It is created by converting a building that almost nobody can occupy into one that almost anybody can.

That reframes the whole underwriting. We are not modelling rent-to-higher-rent on a building with a tenant. We are modelling vacancy-to-occupancy on a building whose addressable market today is close to zero. The dock is not an upgrade. It is the key.

Refinement 02 — buy large, sell small

Once we started reading asking prices rather than assuming them, a second correction appeared, and it inverted our sourcing brief. Small buildings already carry the small-bay premium in the ask. Buying small destroys the trade before a shovel moves.

Refinement 03

We are a merchant developer, not an income landlord.

Underwritten as an income hold, every candidate we tested failed — by $178 to $320 per square foot. Asking prices have been broadly flat since early 2024 while rents fell for eleven consecutive quarters, so today's asks imply roughly a 4 per cent capitalisation rate against a Class B market range nearer 5.25–6.00. On income, the numbers simply do not meet.

They meet on a sale. The buyer who pays $460 to $900 per square foot for small-bay industrial in this region is an owner-occupier — a trades business, a distributor, an importer — comparing the price against building new or against the rent they are already paying, not against a yield.

So the product is not a leased building. It is six individually saleable small-bay units, each with its own dock, its own service and its own washroom. Build for the owner-occupier, sell to the owner-occupier, recycle the capital.

04 — The Screen

Four gates,
in this order.

Physical feasibility is screened before price. A building that fails gate one or two is not repriced — it is dropped, because no discount fixes a lot that cannot hold a truck court.

GATE 01

Truck court depth

Measured from the proposed dock wall to the property line or the nearest obstruction. Under 100 feet a 53-foot trailer cannot manoeuvre at all. 100 to 120 feet means single-trailer working and discounted rents. 130 feet is the minimum we will underwrite. Beyond 180 feet the yard earns money on its own as trailer storage.

Under 100 ft — kill
GATE 02

Site coverage

Building footprint divided by lot area. Above about 45 per cent there is rarely room for a court once parking, landscape strip and setbacks are honoured. This runs before any financial analysis, because a cheap building on a tight lot cannot be fixed at any price.

Target under 45%
GATE 03

Wall structure

In precast panel buildings, panels are frequently load-bearing individually — and cutting a nine-foot opening can mean replacing an entire panel rather than framing an opening. A structural walk-through before we go firm turns a known line item into a known number, or into a walk-away.

Walk-through before firm
GATE 04

Soil at the dock well

A dock well means excavating several hundred cubic metres directly beside a building that may have held solvents, hydraulic fluid or a fuel tank. We bore the dock-well footprint specifically during due diligence, not the site generally — because a failed excess-soil characterisation is a six-figure surprise and eight to twelve weeks of schedule, and it is entirely avoidable.

Bore before closing

Two Ontario provisions materially help this strategy and are frequently mis-budgeted. Development charges generally do not apply — the Development Charges Act exempts enlargement of an existing industrial building's floor area by 50 per cent or less, and a dock conversion adds none. A Record of Site Condition is not normally triggered either, because that applies to a change toward a more sensitive use, not industrial to industrial. Both should be confirmed for the specific parcel; neither is a general guarantee.

05 — Economics

What it costs,
honestly.

Most repositioning budgets we have seen under-price two things: the foundation work at the dock wall, and the yard. Here is the shape of a real one.

Gang the docks

An old building's footings sit at frost depth. A dock well is excavated below the bearing elevation of an existing foundation, which means underpinning or a new frost wall at every opening — the line most broker-supplied budgets omit entirely.

An isolated dock needs about 130 linear feet of retaining wall to serve one position. A bank of four sharing one well needs about 45 feet per position. Cost per dock falls by 40 to 50 per cent the moment you stop building them one at a time.

Then price the yard

On a 30,000 square foot building with six docks and a 130-foot court, the site works alone — concrete apron, heavy-duty asphalt, excavation, granular, catch basins, storm and an oil-grit separator — run to roughly $620,000, about $20.70 per square foot of building.

There is also a capital-light answer worth modelling on every deal: a portable steel yard ramp at $18,000–$35,000, with no permit and no site plan approval. Where a building needs one or two truck positions rather than six, it frequently beats a $130,000 dock well.

A retrofit carries a 12–20 per cent contingency, not a new-build 5–10 — the difference is what you find behind the wall.

Schedule

Close to occupancy runs 14 to 24 months, with 18 as the base case. Site plan control is the critical path — not construction.

The seasonal trap

Ontario asphalt plants close from about mid-November to mid-April. Approval landing in September means no paving until May — a six-month dead carry that appears in no cost table. We time acquisitions so site works run in a single season.

Who does the work

Kopman Build — the group's construction company, operating since 1999. The retrofit enters our model at internal cost rather than at a tendered price, which is the entire reason we can compete for these buildings.
