2026 Reference Guide

Toronto Building Permit Timeline

Processing times, fees, inspection stages, and what to expect at every step — from a contractor who has pulled hundreds of Toronto permits since 1999.

Permit Type Reference

Processing times & fee ranges

The table below reflects 2026 conditions based on current City of Toronto Building Division processing. All fees are estimates calculated on declared construction value — the City's exact fee formula is published in the Building By-Law and updated periodically. Processing times assume a complete, compliant first submission; revisions add time.

Permit Type Typical Processing Fee Range Notes
Minor Variance 3–6 months $900–$2,500 Committee of Adjustment hearing required; public notice must be posted minimum 10 days before hearing date. Neighbour objections can extend timeline significantly. Applies when your project does not conform to zoning by-laws (setbacks, lot coverage, height, etc.).
Building Permit — Small Renovation 5–10 business days $500–$2,000 Projects under $50,000 declared value with no structural changes, no change of use, and no HVAC scope. Typically applies to interior cosmetic work involving plumbing or electrical rough-in only. ePermit submission recommended to avoid intake delays.
Building Permit — Major Renovation 2–6 weeks $2,000–$8,000 Structural work, HVAC scope changes, drain relocations, or projects over $50,000 value. Requires BCIN-registered drawings. Multiple trade disciplines trigger parallel plan review queues which can extend timelines.
Addition Permit 4–8 weeks $3,500–$12,000 Rear or side additions with foundation work. Complexity scales with footprint, basement configuration, and whether underpinning is involved. Zoning compliance and grading plans typically required at submission.
Second Storey Addition 6–12 weeks $5,000–$15,000 Full zoning and structural review required. Existing foundation and floor structure must be assessed for load capacity. Heritage-adjacent properties may trigger Heritage Preservation Services referral, extending timelines by 4–8 weeks.
New Custom Home 8–16 weeks $8,000–$25,000 BCIN drawings required at minimum; most lenders and insurers require a licensed architect or engineer. Full site plan, grading, and drainage required. Demolition of existing structure requires a separate prior permit. Tarion enrolment required for new homes.
Laneway House 4–10 weeks $4,000–$12,000 As-of-right in most Toronto residential zones since 2018 policy changes, but a permit is still required. Must confirm lane access, zoning eligibility, and servicing. Lot coverage and height rules apply. Structural drawings required for any two-storey laneway unit.
Garden Suite 5–10 weeks $4,500–$13,000 As-of-right across Toronto since 2022 Official Plan Amendment. Similar complexity to laneway house permit; servicing and grading review required. Maximum permitted size varies by lot; owner-occupancy requirement was eliminated in 2023.
Legal Basement Suite 3–6 weeks $2,500–$6,000 Requires egress window or door compliance, mechanical separation (fire-rated assemblies), independent HVAC, and ESA electrical inspection. Existing basement suites built without permits carry ongoing liability and must be retroactively permitted before sale or rental.
Heritage Alteration Permit 2–6 months $1,500–$5,000+ Required for designated properties (Part IV or Part V of the Ontario Heritage Act). Heritage Preservation Services must review any proposed exterior alteration before a building permit can be issued. Scope of review depends on heritage designation type and the character-defining elements involved.
Demolition Permit 2–4 weeks $500–$2,500 Required before issuing any new home or major addition permit where existing structure must be removed. An asbestos survey (Designated Substances Report) may be required for structures built before 1985. Utility disconnections must be confirmed before inspection sign-off.

All fees are estimates. The City of Toronto's current fee schedule is published at toronto.ca/building. Declared construction value must be accurate — undervaluing is common cause of permit revision requests and can create liability at project close-out.

Site Inspections

The nine inspection stages

The City of Toronto Building Division conducts inspections at mandatory stages defined under the Ontario Building Code. Each inspection must be booked through the City's Building Portal (ePermit system) at minimum 2 business days in advance; actual wait is typically 3–5 business days. Work must not proceed past an inspection stage until the previous inspection is passed and recorded in the system. Inspectors are assigned by district and arrive within a 4-hour window.

Stage 01

Pre-Construction

Required on complex projects before any ground disturbance. Inspector confirms lot lines, grade, and that the approved drawings are on site. Not required for all permit types — check your permit conditions.

Stage 02

Foundation / Excavation

Inspector verifies that footings and foundation walls conform to approved drawings before backfill. Includes inspection of waterproofing membrane and drainage layer. Underpinning projects have additional inspection requirements at each pit sequence.

Stage 03

Rough Framing

All structural framing must be complete before this inspection — walls, floors, roof structure, beams, and connections. Inspector verifies dimensions, header sizes, bearing points, and any engineered wood product installation against stamped drawings.

Stage 04

Rough Plumbing

All drain, waste, and vent (DWV) piping and supply lines must be installed and pressure-tested before concealment. Inspector verifies pipe sizing, slope, venting configuration, and proper cleanout placement per OBC Part 7.

Stage 05

Rough Mechanical (HVAC)

Ductwork, air handlers, HRV installation, and combustion air provisions are inspected in their rough-in state. Inspector checks equipment sizing against approved mechanical drawings and confirms fire damper installation at assemblies where required.

Stage 06

Rough Electrical (ESA)

This inspection is conducted by the Electrical Safety Authority (ESA), not the City Building Division — it is a separate booking and a separate certificate. All rough wiring, panel work, and electrical rough-in must be complete. ESA issues a Certificate of Inspection, which is required before City final sign-off.

Stage 07

Insulation & Air Barrier

Inspector verifies that insulation type and R-values conform to OBC requirements and that the air barrier and vapour retarder are correctly installed before any drywall is applied. This is a sequencing-critical stage — drywall cannot go up until passed.

Stage 08

Drywall / Fire Separation

Required specifically where fire-rated assemblies are involved — basement suites, attached garages, party walls, floor/ceiling assemblies between occupancies. Inspector verifies type-X drywall installation, taping, and joint tape requirements before finishing. May be combined with insulation inspection on simpler projects.

Stage 09

Final Inspection

Inspector confirms project completion against all permit conditions. ESA final certificate must be in hand. All mechanical equipment must be operational and accessible. Grading, drainage, and exterior works are verified. Occupancy is not permitted until the Final inspection is passed and recorded.

Building District Offices

Processing times by district

The City of Toronto operates four Building District offices, each responsible for a geographic area of the city. Processing times vary meaningfully between districts — not because of different standards, but because of application volume relative to staff capacity. Planning your submission district can be relevant for projects near district boundaries.

Downtown & Midtown

Slowest — Highest Volume

Covers the City's core and densest residential neighbourhoods including Yorkville, Rosedale, Forest Hill, The Annex, and the downtown condo corridor. Application volume is consistently highest in this district. Plan for the top end of any processing range for complex applications.

North York

Mid-Range

Covers North York including Don Mills, Lawrence Park, and Willowdale. Generally 10–15% faster than the Downtown district for equivalent application types. A reliable district for additions and custom home permits in the mid-to-upper residential market.

Etobicoke

Faster — 20–30% Below Average

Consistently among the faster districts for residential permit processing. Projects in Etobicoke, Mimico, New Toronto, and Islington Village typically see permits issued in the lower half of the applicable time range. Good district for addition and new home timelines.

Scarborough

Faster — 20–30% Below Average

Similar to Etobicoke in processing speed relative to application volume. Covers all areas east of Victoria Park Avenue. Heritage applications are less common in Scarborough, which removes a frequent source of delay seen in the Downtown district.

District processing speed estimates are based on operational experience and are not published guarantees. Times fluctuate with staffing levels, seasonal application volume, and City-wide initiatives. ePermit submissions may process faster regardless of district.

Contractor Strategies

How to expedite your permit application

There is no paid fast-track lane for residential permits in Toronto. The only reliable ways to reduce wait time are to reduce the number of times your application cycles through review. Incomplete or non-compliant submissions are the single largest source of delay.

01

Use the pre-application consultation

For additions, second-storey work, laneway houses, and anything with zoning uncertainty, a pre-application consultation (PAC) with Building and Planning staff before drawing completion is time well spent. Issues identified at a PAC are resolved before they become formal revision requests, which eat 2–4 weeks per cycle.

02

Submit a complete application the first time

The most common cause of delay is an incomplete submission — missing site plan, absent grading information, or drawings that don't address all applicable OBC divisions. Review the City's checklist for your permit type before submission and confirm every box is checked. One revision cycle is normal; two or more is preventable.

03

Use BCIN-registered drawings

Provincial BCIN registration is the benchmark for drawing quality the City expects. Professionally prepared drawings stamped by a BCIN designer are less likely to generate revision requests than homeowner-prepared drawings. For anything beyond simple interior work, the cost of professional drawings is recovered in time saved during review.

04

Resolve zoning compliance before applying

If your project doesn't conform to the zoning by-law, a building permit cannot be issued until a Committee of Adjustment minor variance is approved — and that process takes 3–6 months on its own. Identify any zoning conflicts at the design stage and either redesign within compliance or begin the COA process in parallel with drawing preparation.

05

Submit through ePermit online

The City's ePermit system processes electronic submissions directly into the review queue without a physical intake step, which avoids 3–5 business days of scanning and data entry. ePermit also allows real-time status tracking and online inspection booking. Paper submissions are still accepted but have no time advantage.

06

Engage your structural engineer early

Structural engineering is on the critical path for additions, second-storey work, and custom homes. If your structural drawings are not ready when your architectural and mechanical drawings are complete, you hold up the entire submission. Engage a structural engineer at the same time you engage your designer — not after.

Revision Requests

Common reasons permits come back

When the City's plan examiner finds an issue with a submission, the application is returned with a Deficiency Notice. Responding to and resubmitting for a single revision cycle typically adds 2–4 weeks to your overall timeline. The following are the six most common reasons for revision requests on residential applications in Toronto.

Missing or incomplete site plan

The site plan must show the property boundary, all existing and proposed structures, setbacks from all lot lines, driveway location, and the location of any accessory structures. A dimensioned survey is required for most applications. Sketch plans drawn from memory are returned at high rates.

Setback or lot coverage non-compliance

If the proposed work places a structure closer to a lot line than the zoning by-law permits, or pushes total lot coverage over the maximum, the application is returned. This is not a drawing quality issue — it requires either a design revision or a concurrent Committee of Adjustment application.

Grading and drainage documentation absent

Any addition that changes the footprint of the house or alters the lot grade requires a grading plan prepared by a civil engineer or licensed grading designer. This is frequently missing from first submissions because it falls between architectural and structural scope. The City requires confirmation that stormwater will not flow toward neighbouring properties.

Heritage details missing or inadequate

For properties within a Heritage Conservation District or individually designated under the Ontario Heritage Act, drawings must include existing and proposed elevation comparisons showing how character-defining elements will be treated. Applications that do not address heritage requirements are sent to Heritage Preservation Services, which adds weeks before a substantive response is issued.

Incomplete mechanical drawings

For permits involving HVAC scope — new forced-air systems, HRV installation, additions requiring duct extension — the mechanical drawings must show equipment location, duct routing, supply and return registers, combustion air provisions, and fuel line routing where applicable. Partial or schematic mechanical drawings are a frequent deficiency.

HVAC load calculations absent

The Ontario Building Code requires that heating and cooling equipment be sized based on calculated loads for the dwelling. A Manual J or equivalent load calculation report prepared by a mechanical engineer or qualified HVAC designer must accompany the permit for new homes, additions, and substantial HVAC replacements. Missing this document is among the most common single deficiency on residential permit applications.

Regulatory Changes

What has changed 2024–2026

Several legislative and policy changes have affected residential building permits in Ontario and Toronto over the past two years. Understanding these changes matters both for project feasibility and for budget accuracy.

Bill 23 · 2023–2024

Development charge reductions and fee impacts

Bill 23 (More Homes Built Faster Act) reduced development charges (DCs) for certain residential project types — particularly purpose-built rentals, affordable housing, and missing-middle housing forms. However, these reductions have affected municipal capital budgets, and some municipalities offset by adjusting other fee categories. Building permit fees themselves are largely separate from development charges and were not directly reduced by Bill 23. Confirm current fee schedules with the City before budgeting.

MTSA Zoning · 2024–2025

As-of-right density around transit stations

Provincial MTSA (Major Transit Station Area) policies require municipalities to permit significant density increases around transit stations without requiring rezoning or official plan amendments. For Toronto, this means certain residential areas near subway and GO Transit stops have expanded as-of-right permissions. For homeowners, this is most relevant to those considering additions or new construction near station areas — what previously required a rezoning application may now proceed as of right.

Garden Suites · 2022

As-of-right across Toronto residential zones

Garden suites (detached accessory dwelling units built in the rear yard, not on a laneway) became as-of-right across all Toronto residential zones in 2022. Prior to this, each garden suite application required individual zoning approval. Permit processing times of 5–10 weeks reflect the standard building permit review now applicable — the zoning uncertainty that previously added months has been eliminated. The owner-occupancy requirement was also removed in 2023, making garden suites viable as investor-held rental properties.

ePermit · 2023–2026

Expanded online permit and inspection services

The City of Toronto has continued expanding the ePermit platform, which now supports permit applications for most residential project types, online payment, real-time status tracking, and inspection booking. Inspections booked through the Building Portal are now the standard path; phone-in inspection bookings are being phased out. Applicants who do not use the online system may experience longer intake processing times as staffing is allocated increasingly toward digital queue management.

Frequently asked questions about Toronto permits

How long does a Toronto building permit take in 2026?

It depends on permit type. Small renovation permits (under $50K, no structural) are typically issued in 5–10 business days through the City's ePermit system. Major renovation permits take 2–6 weeks. Additions and second-storey additions run 4–12 weeks. New custom homes take 8–16 weeks. Heritage applications can take 2–6 months. All timelines assume a complete, compliant first submission — revision cycles add 2–4 weeks each.

What does a Toronto building permit cost?

Toronto permit fees are calculated based on the declared construction value of the project. Fees typically range from roughly $500–$2,000 for small renovation permits to $8,000–$25,000 for new custom homes. The 2024 Bill 23 changes reduced development charges for some project types but did not eliminate building permit fees. Always check the current fee schedule at toronto.ca/building before budgeting.

Do I need a permit to renovate my kitchen or bathroom in Toronto?

Cosmetic kitchen and bathroom renovations — new tile, paint, fixture swaps without moving drains — typically do not require a permit. Any work that moves plumbing drain or supply lines, changes the electrical panel load, modifies load-bearing walls, or changes the layout of a wet area does require a permit. When in doubt, apply for one: working without a required permit creates serious liability at resale.

What is an ePermit and how does it speed up the process?

Toronto's ePermit is the City's online building permit application and tracking portal. Permits submitted electronically are entered into the City's queue without a physical intake step, which typically saves 3–5 business days compared to paper submission. Inspection bookings can also be made online, and status updates are available in real time. ePermit is the standard path for all new permit applications as of 2025.

What is a pre-application consultation and when should I use it?

A pre-application consultation (PAC) is a meeting with City of Toronto planning and building staff before formal submission. It is most useful for complex projects — additions, second-storey additions, laneway houses, heritage properties, or anything with potential zoning conflicts. A PAC can identify issues before you spend money on full working drawings, potentially saving weeks of revision cycles. PACs are free and bookable through the City's planning department.

What is a BCIN and when is it required?

A BCIN (Building Code Identification Number) is a provincial registration number for designers who prepare drawings under the Ontario Building Code. Any permit application for a new home, addition, or complex renovation must include drawings stamped by a BCIN-registered designer or a licensed professional engineer or architect. For residential projects under the Part 9 threshold, homeowner-prepared drawings are technically permitted in some cases, but professional drawings are almost always the faster path to permit issuance. See our full permit guide for more on BCIN requirements.
