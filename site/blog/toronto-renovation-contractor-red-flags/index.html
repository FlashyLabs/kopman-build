12 renovation contractor red flags in Toronto

What 25 years in the Toronto market teaches you about which warning signs to take seriously — and what each one actually costs you when you ignore it.

Why red flags are worth learning

The Toronto renovation market is large, competitive, and — for homeowners who don't know what to look for — genuinely hazardous. Every year, clients come to us after a project with another contractor went wrong. The stories are remarkably similar: a quote that seemed reasonable, a contract that was vague, an initial phase that moved quickly, then a slowdown, then communication problems, then money gone and work unfinished or done poorly.

None of these clients were foolish or careless. They simply didn't know what to watch for before signing. The warning signs that precede a bad renovation experience are almost always visible before a dollar changes hands — you just need to know where to look and what they mean.

This list is drawn from 25 years of operating in the Toronto market: what we see when we walk into projects that have gone sideways, what the paper trail looks like, and which early signals consistently predict a bad outcome. We have organized it roughly in the order you would encounter these signals during the hiring process.

Red flags at the first contact stage

1. No verifiable business history

A contractor operating in Toronto should have a verifiable trail: a registered business name you can look up through the Ontario Business Registry, a physical office address (even a small one), a portfolio of identifiable completed projects, and some form of online presence with independent reviews. None of these need to be elaborate. What matters is that they exist.

A contractor with no business registration, no fixed address, a portfolio of stock photos or images without project details, and reviews only on platforms they control themselves is not a contractor with a low marketing budget — it is a contractor with something to hide. In the Toronto market specifically, where renovation fraud is a documented problem, this combination should end the conversation immediately.

One simple check: search the company name on the City of Toronto's permit records. A legitimate contractor doing regular work in the city will show up in permit history. One who never appears may be doing work without permits — which matters for reasons we will get to shortly.

2. An unusually low quote

Pricing varies between contractors, and there are legitimate reasons for quotes to differ: different assumptions about scope, different overhead structures, different trade relationships. But when one quote is dramatically lower than all others — typically more than 25–30% below the midpoint of the other bids — the gap is almost never explained by efficiency.

In the Toronto market, extremely low quotes usually mean one or more of the following:

  • Scope items have been omitted that the other contractors included, setting up change orders once work is underway
  • The contractor plans to use materials of significantly lower specification than what was discussed
  • Labour costs reflect workers who are not properly insured or who are being paid under the table, creating liability for you as the homeowner
  • The quote was deliberately low to win the project, with the intention of recovering margin through disputes and extras once you are committed

For reference: in 2026, a full main-floor kitchen renovation in a Toronto semi-detached typically runs $80,000–$140,000 depending on specification. A primary bathroom renovation runs $35,000–$75,000. A finished basement runs $90,000–$170,000 for a full project with permitting. Quotes that land well below these ranges for comparable scope deserve hard scrutiny.

3. Pressure to decide quickly

Urgency tactics are among the oldest tools in low-quality sales. "I have another client interested in this start date," "material prices are going up next week," "I can only hold this price until Friday" — these are not accurate descriptions of market conditions. They are designed to prevent you from doing the due diligence that would reveal the problems.

A contractor who has done good work and built a real reputation in Toronto does not need to pressure anyone into a decision. Their schedule fills because of referrals and repeat clients. If the pressure to commit is intense before you have even reviewed a contract, that pressure will only intensify once your money is in and the work has begun.

Red flags in the contract and paperwork

4. No proof of insurance or WSIB coverage

This is not a bureaucratic checkbox. It is a direct liability issue for you as the homeowner. Any general contractor operating in Ontario should carry general liability insurance — typically a minimum of $2 million for residential projects, with $5 million being standard for larger renovations — and either carry WSIB (Workplace Safety and Insurance Board) coverage for their own workers or provide proof that all subcontractors are individually covered.

If a worker is injured on your property and the contractor does not have WSIB coverage, the WSIB can pursue you for the costs. If a contractor's work causes property damage to your home or a neighbour's and there is no liability insurance, you are exposed. Ask for certificates of insurance as a matter of course, not as an accusation. A legitimate contractor will have these ready and will hand them over without hesitation. Reluctance, delay, or vague assurances are a clear signal to walk away.

5. A vague or one-page contract

A renovation contract that is one or two pages long cannot possibly contain the detail needed to protect either party on a project of any complexity. It will leave scope items undefined, change order processes unspecified, and payment terms open to interpretation — all of which work against you once disputes arise.

A proper residential renovation contract for a Toronto project should include, at minimum:

  • A detailed scope of work with specific materials called out by brand, model, and specification where relevant
  • A project schedule with a start date, key milestone dates, and a substantial completion target
  • A milestone-based payment schedule tied to defined project phases, not arbitrary dates
  • A written change order process specifying that no additional work proceeds without documented approval and agreed pricing
  • Explicit exclusions from scope, so there is no ambiguity about what is and is not included
  • The contractor's WSIB account number and liability insurance details
  • Warranty terms for labour and materials, including how warranty claims are handled
  • A dispute resolution process

If what you are handed does not cover these items, ask for a revised version. If one is not forthcoming, treat that as your answer about how the project will be managed when things get complicated.

6. A large upfront deposit

A deposit at contract signing is standard and reasonable — it covers mobilization costs and demonstrates commitment from both parties. But the size of that deposit matters. In Ontario, a deposit of 10–15% of the total project value is normal. Demands for 30%, 40%, or 50% upfront before work begins are not.

Established contractors have trade accounts with suppliers and do not need your money to float materials. A large upfront deposit requirement most often indicates a contractor who is using new project funds to pay for previous projects, or one who plans to collect and disappear. Once that money is gone, your leverage disappears with it. Keep meaningful funds tied to project milestones — and keep a 10% holdback until all deficiencies are resolved to your satisfaction.

Ontario Construction Act note: On projects valued at $250,000 or more, Ontario's Construction Act requires a statutory holdback of 10% of each payment until the project's lien period expires (typically 45 days after substantial completion). Even on smaller projects, maintaining a voluntary holdback until deficiencies are cleared is strongly advisable. Make sure your contract explicitly references holdback terms.

7. Suggesting you skip the permit

Any contractor who recommends avoiding the permit process — framed as saving you time, money, or hassle — is telling you something important about their operating standards. In Toronto, building permits are required for structural changes, additions, new plumbing, electrical upgrades, HVAC modifications, and most basement finishing projects. These are not bureaucratic inconveniences; they are the mechanism by which your work is independently inspected to confirm it was done correctly.

Unpermitted work creates compounding problems:

  • Your home insurance may be voided or claims denied for damage related to unpermitted work
  • When you sell, you are required to disclose unpermitted renovations — and buyers and their lawyers will find them
  • If an inspector identifies the work after the fact, you may be required to open walls to verify the rough-in, at significant cost
  • The work may not meet current code, creating genuine safety risks

Your contractor should pull permits in their own name. If they suggest you pull them as the homeowner — which is technically permitted under Ontario's Building Code Act — be aware that doing so transfers responsibility for compliance to you and can complicate insurance and sale disclosures down the line.

Red flags during the project

8. The site goes quiet without explanation

Renovation projects have natural rhythms: periods of intensive activity followed by waiting time as inspections clear, materials arrive, or trades are scheduled. A site that is quiet for a day or two while a permit inspection is pending is not concerning. A site that goes silent for a week or more without explanation or communication from the contractor is a serious warning sign.

In most cases, an unexplained site shutdown means one of three things: the contractor has taken on more work than they can manage and is rotating crews between jobs, they are having cash flow problems and cannot pay subcontractors to continue, or something has gone wrong that they are not prepared to tell you about. None of these scenarios resolve themselves. If communication goes dark for more than three or four business days without a clear explanation, escalate the conversation in writing immediately.

9. Change orders presented verbally or after the fact

Change orders are a normal and expected part of renovation — particularly in older Toronto homes where what is behind the walls is genuinely unknown until demolition. A 1960s Etobicoke bungalow may reveal knob-and-tube wiring. A 1940s Rosedale semi may have galvanized plumbing. These discoveries are not contractor failures; they are just the reality of old construction.

What matters is how changes are handled. A contractor who informs you of a necessary change, explains the implications clearly, provides a written change order with documented additional cost, and waits for your written approval before proceeding is managing the project professionally. A contractor who has the work done first and presents a bill for extras later — or who describes changes verbally with a rough number and keeps moving — is creating the conditions for a significant dispute at the end of the project.

Insist on written change orders before any out-of-scope work proceeds. This protects the contractor as much as it protects you. Any professional contractor will be comfortable with this process.

10. The site is consistently disorganized or hazardous

A job site does not need to be immaculate during active demolition. But the baseline level of organization on a contractor's site is a reliable proxy for the quality of their work. Contractors who run orderly, well-managed sites tend to produce better outcomes than those who do not — not because tidiness is the measure, but because the same attention to detail and professional habits that keep a site organized show up in how carefully the work itself is done.

Warning signs include: materials left outdoors without weather protection, tools and equipment scattered without organization, inadequate dust containment in areas of your home that should be protected, no clear staging area for waste and debris, and safety hazards left unaddressed — open floor penetrations, unsecured ladders, no temporary weatherproofing on open areas of the building envelope. These are not aesthetic concerns. They indicate how the contractor runs their operation.

On subcontractors: Most general contractors in Toronto use subcontractors for specialized trades — electricians, plumbers, HVAC, tile setters. This is normal and does not itself indicate a problem. What matters is whether the GC is actually managing those subcontractors: scheduling them properly, inspecting their work before it gets covered up, and taking responsibility for the overall result. A GC who treats subcontractors as independent from their own accountability is abdicating the core of their job.

Red flags in communication and conduct

11. Slow or evasive communication

How a contractor communicates before you sign tells you exactly how they will communicate once you have signed. This is one of the most reliable predictors of project experience that we have observed over 25 years. A contractor who takes three days to return your initial inquiry, who gives vague answers to direct questions about insurance or schedule, or who becomes noticeably less responsive once the contract is executed is showing you who they are.

You should be able to reach your contractor or project manager within one business day for non-urgent matters and same-day for active issues on site. If the communication standard during the sales process does not meet that bar, negotiate it explicitly before signing or find someone else. Once a project is underway and problems arise — and problems always arise — slow communication compounds every other issue.

12. Reluctance to provide references from similar projects

A contractor with genuine experience doing work similar to yours should be able to provide at minimum two or three references from comparable projects completed in the past two years. Note the qualifiers: comparable scope, recently completed. A contractor who offers to connect you with a reference from a deck they built in 2019 is not giving you useful information about their current team, their current subcontractors, or their current management practices.

Reluctance to provide references almost always means one of three things: the recent project history is not one they want you to hear about, they have done so few projects that comparable references don't exist, or the relationships with past clients did not end well enough to make a reference call productive. Any of these scenarios is telling. Call every reference you are given. Ask specifically about communication, schedule adherence, how change orders were handled, and — most importantly — whether they would hire that contractor again.

What a strong contractor looks like instead

The flip side of a red flag list is worth stating explicitly. The contractors who consistently deliver excellent renovation outcomes in Toronto share a common profile: they are selective about the projects they take on, they communicate proactively and in writing, they pull permits as a matter of course, and they treat their trade and supplier relationships as long-term assets rather than one-time transactions. Their contracts are detailed because they have learned — as we have — that ambiguity always costs someone something.

They also tend to be busier than the average contractor, because good work generates referrals. Which means the timeline to start a project with a genuinely good contractor in Toronto is typically longer than with one who always has immediate availability. If a contractor can start next week on a project of any real scope, ask yourself why their schedule is that open.

None of the flags on this list are subtle. They are visible during the normal process of getting quotes, reviewing contracts, and having initial conversations. The difficulty is not in seeing them once you know what you are looking for — it is in taking them seriously rather than rationalizing them away when you are eager to get your project started.
