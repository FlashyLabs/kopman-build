10 renovation mistakes Toronto homeowners keep making

Twenty-five years of Toronto renovation experience distilled into the ten mistakes we see most often — and how to avoid them.

Patterns repeat across every renovation

After 25 years building and renovating homes in Toronto, we have seen a lot of projects go wrong in a lot of different ways. But the causes are rarely random. The same ten mistakes appear over and over, across different neighbourhoods, different project types, and different budgets. Some cost homeowners thousands of extra dollars. Others create legal problems that follow a property for years. A few cause projects to stall completely.

None of them are inevitable. All of them are avoidable if you know what to look for before you start. What follows is what 25 years in this industry has taught us about where Toronto renovations go off the rails — and what to do instead.

1. Choosing the lowest quote

The instinct to take the lowest bid is understandable, but it is one of the most reliable predictors of a difficult renovation. When three contractors quote the same project and one comes in 30 or 40 percent below the others, the gap almost never reflects superior efficiency. It reflects missing scope, underpriced labour, planned reliance on change orders to recover margin, or some combination of all three. The cheapest job almost always ends up costing more than the middle bid would have, once the change orders accumulate or the rework begins. Compare quotes line by line rather than as totals, and get clarity on anything that differs before you make a decision based on price.

2. Skipping permits

Permits feel like an unnecessary hurdle, especially when a contractor suggests they are not really required or that "everyone skips them." They are required, and the consequences of not getting them are real: unpermitted work must be disclosed to buyers in Ontario, and undisclosed unpermitted work discovered after closing is grounds for a lawsuit. Beyond resale, skipping permits can void your home insurance if an unpermitted installation causes a loss, and retroactive permits — when they are even possible — are more expensive and more complicated than pulling the permit correctly the first time. Any contractor who recommends skipping a permit as a cost-saving measure is telling you something important about how they operate.

3. Starting construction before materials are ordered

Demolition is satisfying. It feels like progress. But starting demo before your materials are ordered and confirmed is one of the most common causes of project delays and cost overruns we see in Toronto. Cabinetry from most quality manufacturers takes six to twelve weeks to produce. Tile patterns can be discontinued between the time you select them and the time you need them. Custom items of any kind — windows, doors, millwork, light fixtures — have lead times that are difficult to compress no matter how urgent your situation is. Once you have pulled out a kitchen, you are living without it until the cabinets arrive, full stop. Confirm every material lead time before demolition begins.

4. No contingency budget

Renovation budgets without contingency are not budgets — they are best-case scenarios. In a Toronto home built before 1970, you should assume that opening walls will reveal something that was not in the original scope: knob-and-tube wiring, asbestos-containing materials in old pipe insulation or floor tiles, undersized structural members, water damage from a leak nobody knew about, or plumbing that is older and less compliant than expected. The standard contingency for a straightforward project in a relatively modern home is fifteen percent. For older homes, twenty to twenty-five percent is more appropriate. If you find you do not need it, that money stays with you. If you do need it and it is not there, you are making difficult decisions mid-construction.

5. Scope creep mid-project

Every "while you're at it" conversation that happens after construction has started costs significantly more than it would have if it had been part of the original plan. Trades are scheduled, materials are ordered, and the project has a logic to it that changes disrupt. A decision made at the planning stage to add a heated floor in the bathroom costs the price of the materials and the labour to install them. The same decision made after the subfloor is down, the plumber has roughed in, and the tile has been delivered costs all of that plus the cost of reversing completed work and rescheduling trades whose time is already committed. Changes during construction cost two to three times what they would have cost in the design phase. Make your decisions before the work starts and stick to them.

6. No detailed written contract

A renovation contract that protects you is specific, line-by-line, and covers what happens when things do not go according to plan. At minimum, it should define the scope of work in enough detail to resolve any reasonable dispute about what was included, specify materials with brands and models where they matter, tie payment milestones to project completion stages rather than arbitrary dates, describe how changes are documented and priced before they proceed, and confirm the contractor's insurance and WSIB coverage. A two-paragraph agreement written on a letterhead is not a contract; it is a handshake with extra paper. If a contractor is reluctant to put details in writing, that reluctance will persist when there is a disagreement about what was promised.

7. Underestimating disruption

Living through a renovation is harder than most homeowners expect, and failing to plan for it in advance makes it harder still. A kitchen renovation that takes eight weeks means eight weeks without a functional kitchen — where are you eating, how are you managing with a microwave and a hot plate, where are the children doing homework if the dining room is inaccessible? A bathroom renovation in a one-bathroom home means coordinating shower access with neighbours or a gym membership for the duration. Dust in a renovation travels further than you expect and settles on things further away than seems logical. Before construction begins, make a realistic plan for how your household will function during it. The disruption is manageable; being unprepared for it is not.

8. Not verifying WSIB and insurance

Ontario's Workplace Safety and Insurance Board (WSIB) provides coverage for workers injured on the job. If a contractor or their subcontractor is injured on your property and is not covered by WSIB, you can be held personally liable for costs that can be substantial. The same applies to general liability insurance: if something goes wrong — a fire, a flood, structural damage — and the contractor's insurance is not in order, you may have limited recourse. Ask every contractor for their WSIB clearance certificate and their certificate of liability insurance before anyone steps foot in your home. A legitimate contractor produces these documents without hesitation; any resistance to providing them is a significant warning sign.

9. Over-improving for the street

Renovation returns are determined by the neighbourhood, not by what you build. A $300,000 kitchen renovation in a neighbourhood where houses sell for $800,000 will not add $300,000 of value — buyers in that market have a ceiling for what they will pay for a kitchen regardless of its quality, and the comparable sales in the neighbourhood constrain what anyone will offer. This is not a reason to avoid renovation; it is a reason to calibrate the scope of your renovation to the realistic value uplift in your specific context. Improvements that improve livability and function are almost always worthwhile. Improvements that push the quality of your home far beyond the neighbourhood average rarely recover at resale. Your contractor or a knowledgeable real estate agent can help you find that line.

10. Not calling references

Every contractor will tell you their past clients were happy. The only way to verify that is to speak with those clients directly. Ask for three to five references from projects completed in the past two years that are similar in scope to yours, then actually call them. The questions that tell you the most: Did the project come in on or near the original budget? Did it finish on schedule? Was the site kept reasonably clean during construction? How did the contractor handle problems when they came up? Would you hire them again? A genuine "yes, absolutely" on that last question is a meaningful endorsement. A hesitation, a qualification, or a "yes, but you should know..." tells you something just as valuable. No contractor has a perfect record across every project, but how they handle difficulties when they arise is a reliable predictor of what your experience will be.

The pattern behind all ten mistakes is the same: decisions made in haste, under pressure, or without enough information at the start of a project. The planning phase is the cheapest time to change anything. Time spent getting it right before construction begins is almost always recovered many times over.
