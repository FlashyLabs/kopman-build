How long do Toronto renovations really take?

The honest contractor's guide — real timelines, the phases most homeowners don't account for, and what you can actually do to keep a project on schedule.

The timeline question nobody answers honestly

Every homeowner planning a renovation in Toronto eventually asks the same question: how long will this take? And most contractors, honestly, fudge the answer. They give you the construction duration — the weeks the crew is on site — without accounting for the months of design, permitting, procurement, and scheduling that precede the first swing of a hammer.

That gap between what homeowners expect and what projects actually take is one of the most reliable sources of frustration in this industry. A kitchen renovation that takes eight weeks to build can take five months from the day you decide to do it to the day you cook your first meal in a finished kitchen. A basement that takes six weeks of active construction can take four months by the time permits are issued and materials arrive. Those timelines are not failures — they are the normal reality of doing renovation work properly in Toronto.

This guide is an attempt to give you an honest picture of what each phase of a renovation project actually takes, why delays happen, and how to set up a project so that the avoidable delays don't happen to you.

The phases most homeowners miss

When people think about renovation timelines, they think about construction. But a well-run project has at least four distinct phases before a single wall comes down, and each one takes time.

Phase 1: Design and planning

The design phase is where the scope, layout, materials, and specifications for your project get determined. On a modest renovation — a bathroom refresh or a straightforward basement finish — this can be a relatively quick process: a few meetings, some drawings, a finalized material schedule. On a complex project like a full kitchen gut, a whole-home renovation, or an addition, it can take two to four months to get design documents to a state where they are ready for permit submission and construction.

Homeowners routinely underestimate this phase. The instinct is to call a contractor, get a quote, and start work. But a quote built on undefined scope is not really a quote — it's an approximation that will be revised repeatedly once the details get worked out. The contractors who build realistic schedules and budgets are the ones who insist on doing the design work properly before committing to either.

Phase 2: Permits

Permit timelines in Toronto are one of the most common sources of project delays, and they are largely outside your control once a submission is made. The City of Toronto Building Division processes permit applications at varying speeds depending on project type, reviewer workload, and whether the first submission is complete and compliant.

For a straightforward renovation that requires a permit — structural modifications, basement underpinning, a new bathroom rough-in, any electrical panel upgrade — you should budget four to eight weeks for permit issuance after submission. More complex projects, additions in particular, can take three to six months. These are not theoretical worst cases; they are the current reality of working with the City of Toronto's Building Division as of 2026.

There are things you can do to minimize permit delays. Submitting a complete, well-drawn set of documents the first time avoids the back-and-forth of deficiency letters. Using a contractor who has established relationships with the Building Division and understands submission requirements matters. But even under the best conditions, build permit lead time into your schedule from the beginning.

Phase 3: Material procurement

Lead times on materials and fixtures vary enormously, and they have become more variable in recent years. Standard items — dimensional lumber, drywall, common plumbing fixtures — are typically available within one to two weeks. But custom cabinetry, imported tile, specialty windows, engineered wood flooring, and certain appliance lines routinely run eight to sixteen weeks from order to delivery.

The mistake most homeowners make is assuming materials will be ordered once construction starts. By that point, you may be waiting two months for your cabinets to arrive after your kitchen has already been gutted. The right approach is to finalize all material selections during the design phase, place long-lead orders before construction begins, and sequence the build around confirmed delivery dates rather than assumed ones.

Phase 4: Trade scheduling

Good tradespeople in Toronto — licensed electricians, plumbers, tile setters, custom millwork installers — are in demand. A reputable general contractor maintains established relationships with a roster of reliable subcontractors and can sequence their work on your project efficiently. A contractor who is assembling a new crew for each job, or who does not have standing relationships with key trades, will struggle to hold a schedule when conflicts arise.

This is one of the underappreciated reasons why contractor selection affects your timeline: a well-connected contractor can often accelerate the trade coordination that would otherwise add weeks to a project.

These ranges are broad because the variables are significant. The age and condition of your home matters enormously: a 1920s Annex semi-detached with knob-and-tube wiring and undersized drain lines will require more remediation work than a 1980s North York bungalow with updated mechanicals. Scope changes mid-project extend timelines. Material delays extend timelines. Unforeseen conditions — asbestos in the floor tiles, water damage behind the shower wall, a structural beam in a place the drawings didn't anticipate — extend timelines.

A word on Toronto's older housing stock: A significant portion of the detached homes in high-demand Toronto neighbourhoods — Rosedale, Forest Hill, the Annex, Leslieville, Riverdale, Leaside — were built before 1960. These homes have character, but they also have surprises. Lead paint, asbestos-containing materials, undersized electrical services, inadequate insulation, and unconventional framing are common. Budget both time and money for discovery work. A contractor who doesn't build contingency into the schedule for a pre-1960 home is not being realistic with you.

What drives delays in Toronto specifically

Some delay factors are universal to renovation work anywhere. Others are specific to Toronto's market and regulatory environment.

The permit queue

Toronto has one of the more demanding permit processes among major Canadian cities. The Building Division reviews applications for full code compliance, and any deficiency in the submission triggers a correction cycle that can add four to six weeks to the permit timeline. Projects near ravines or involving heritage designations require additional approvals from other city departments, sometimes adding months.

The practical implication: treat your permit submission as a milestone that needs as much preparation as the construction itself. Incomplete or non-compliant drawings are the single most preventable cause of permit delays.

Trade availability

Toronto is an active construction market. Residential renovation activity has remained high throughout the mid-2020s, partly driven by high home prices making move-up purchases less accessible and renovation a more attractive option. The result is that skilled tradespeople are busy. A reputable plumber or electrician who is already booked four to six weeks out will not rearrange their schedule to accommodate a contractor they have no relationship with. Plan around this reality, not against it.

Inspection sequencing

Toronto's Building Division conducts inspections at required stages of construction: framing, rough-in, insulation, and final, at minimum. Inspection scheduling typically requires two to five business days' notice, and inspectors are not always available the day you request them. A project that requires multiple sequential inspections will have several embedded waits that add up. A good project manager accounts for these in the schedule; a bad one treats them as surprises.

Decision delays

The delay category contractors are least likely to mention but that accounts for a meaningful share of project overruns: homeowner decisions that take longer than planned. Changing a tile selection after it has been ordered. Taking two weeks to decide on a hardware finish. Reconsidering the cabinet layout after the rough-in is done. These are human and understandable, but they have real schedule consequences. The best way to avoid them is to make all material and design decisions before construction begins, not during it.

How to keep your project on schedule

Not all delays are avoidable, but most of the common ones are. Here is what we have found after 25 years of running renovation projects in Toronto:

  • Finalize your design before starting construction. Every unresolved detail at construction start is a decision that will need to be made under pressure, on the fly, while trades are standing by. The weeks you invest in design upfront are paid back many times over in reduced construction delays.
  • Order long-lead materials the moment selections are confirmed. Custom cabinetry, specialty tile, imported fixtures, and custom millwork should be ordered as soon as your design is finalized — ideally before the permit is even submitted. You will often need them by the time the permit is issued and the site is ready.
  • Give your contractor complete information at each decision point. If your contractor asks you to confirm a specification and you take two weeks to respond, that delay flows through to the schedule. Keep your own timeline for decisions and treat it as seriously as the construction timeline.
  • Build contingency into your schedule, not just your budget. A reasonable contingency on a complex renovation is 10–20% of the construction timeline, on top of planned duration. If your contractor says the construction will take twelve weeks, plan for fourteen to sixteen.
  • Choose a contractor with strong trade relationships. This is not something you can easily verify from the outside, but you can ask: who are your key subcontractors for electrical and plumbing, how long have you worked with them, and are they available for your project start date? A contractor who can answer these questions specifically is better positioned to hold a schedule than one who will be calling around once you sign.
  • Resist scope additions once construction starts. Adding a wet bar to the basement after framing has begun, or deciding mid-kitchen-renovation to also renovate the adjacent powder room, almost always extends the timeline more than the additional scope alone would suggest. Every addition creates new coordination, new material orders, and potentially new permit requirements.

The schedule and the budget are connected: Extended timelines cost money, both in direct costs (extended equipment rental, additional site management time) and in indirect costs (alternative accommodation if you have moved out, carrying costs on a project that is taking longer than planned). A contractor who is honest about schedule is also helping you protect your budget.

Seasonal considerations in Toronto

Renovation work in Toronto is year-round, but some project types are meaningfully affected by season.

Exterior work and additions

Foundation work, exterior additions, roofing, and any project that requires prolonged open excavation or exposure of the building envelope is best scheduled to avoid the period from late November through mid-March. Frozen ground limits excavation. Concrete cannot be poured below 5°C without special measures. Waterproofing membrane products have temperature minimums. This does not mean exterior work cannot happen in winter — with proper tent structures and heating, it can — but it adds cost and complexity. If you are planning an addition or significant exterior project, starting the design and permit process in late summer or fall so you can begin construction in spring is often the most efficient path.

Interior renovations

Interior work is largely season-independent, but timing matters for occupancy. If you are planning a kitchen renovation, the worst time to be without a functional kitchen is over the December holidays. If your home will be uninhabitable during construction and you need to arrange alternative accommodation, school calendars may constrain when you can move the family out. These are planning variables worth thinking through early.

Contractor availability

Spring is the busiest season for renovation starts in Toronto. Homeowners who spent the winter planning want to start work as soon as the weather permits, and demand for contractor time peaks from April through June. If you want to start a project in the spring, your planning conversation with a contractor should begin in the fall — not in February. Quality contractors book out months in advance, and the ones with spring availability in March are often the ones the busier contractors couldn't accommodate.

A note on living through a renovation

One of the practical questions that affects timeline planning is whether you will be living in the home during the renovation. For targeted projects — a single bathroom, a basement that doesn't affect living areas — it is usually workable. For a full kitchen renovation, most homeowners are surprised by how disruptive three months without a functional kitchen actually is. For a whole-home renovation, living in a construction site is generally not viable.

If you need to move out, factor that into your timeline planning. Finding rental accommodation in Toronto's current market takes time. The duration of your alternative accommodation arrangement needs to account for construction plus a buffer for punch-list and final inspection. Coordinate your lease start and end dates with your construction schedule, not just your projected substantial completion date.
