Kitchen renovation cost by size in Toronto

Real 2026 cost ranges for every kitchen configuration — from the narrow galley in a downtown semi to the sprawling open-plan in a North York centre-hall. What drives the number, and what doesn't.

Why kitchen size is only part of the answer

When homeowners ask us what a kitchen renovation costs in Toronto, the first thing they want is a number. That is understandable. But the honest answer is that kitchen size sets the floor, not the ceiling. A 120-square-foot kitchen with a complete layout change, plumbing relocation, load-bearing wall removal, and high-end cabinetry can cost more than twice what a 200-square-foot kitchen costs if that larger kitchen keeps its footprint intact and uses mid-range materials.

That said, size matters enormously in practical terms. More square footage means more cabinetry, more countertop, more flooring, more lighting circuits, and more labour hours. Understanding what your kitchen size implies in cost terms is a useful starting point for planning your budget — as long as you treat it as a baseline, not a quote.

We have completed kitchen renovations across Toronto and the GTA for 27 years. The ranges below reflect what projects are actually costing in 2026 — accounting for current labour rates, material pricing, and permit fees. They are not based on national averages, American data, or renovations from five years ago.

The cost drivers that cut across every size

Before we get into size tiers, it is worth understanding the variables that push costs up or down regardless of square footage. These factors can shift a budget by 30% to 60% within the same kitchen footprint.

Scope of structural work

Keeping your kitchen in exactly the same footprint — same door and window locations, same island position, same plumbing locations — is the single biggest lever on cost. The moment you start moving walls, especially load-bearing ones, you add engineering, permits, temporary support structures, and significant labour. In older Toronto homes, removing a wall between the kitchen and dining room is one of the most common requests we see, and one of the most variable in cost. A non-load-bearing wall in a relatively modern home might add $8,000–$15,000 to a project. A load-bearing wall in a 1940s centre-hall with an unexpected steel beam requirement can add $25,000–$40,000.

Plumbing and electrical relocation

Moving the sink from one wall to another requires new drain lines, possibly a new vent stack, and new supply lines — and in many Toronto homes, that means cutting into finished basement ceilings below. Moving the gas range adds a licensed gas fitter to the team. Adding a new island with a sink means all of the above plus a longer drain run. Electrical upgrades for a modern kitchen — dedicated circuits for the dishwasher, refrigerator, microwave, and island outlets — are required by code regardless of what you had before. Budget $4,000–$10,000 for a meaningful electrical upgrade in a mid-sized kitchen.

Cabinetry tier

Cabinetry is typically the largest single line item in a kitchen renovation, often 35–45% of total project cost. The difference between a stock cabinet from a big-box retailer and a semi-custom cabinet from a reputable Ontario millwork shop can be $15,000–$40,000 on the same kitchen layout. Custom cabinetry — fully bespoke, built to specific dimensions and specifications — can cost two to three times more than semi-custom. For the purposes of the ranges in this article, we use three tiers: entry-level (stock or IKEA-style flat-pack), mid-range (semi-custom from a local supplier), and premium (custom millwork).

Countertop material

Laminate countertops start around $40–$60 per linear foot installed. Quartz runs $120–$200 per linear foot installed for mid-grade slabs. Premium quartzite, marble, and thick-profile waterfall edges can push $250–$400 per linear foot. On a kitchen with 25 linear feet of counter, that difference is $2,000 vs. $10,000 for the countertop line alone.

Permits

Most complete Toronto kitchen renovations require at least a building permit if structural work is involved, and an electrical permit for panel or circuit work. City of Toronto permit fees for a typical kitchen renovation run $1,000–$3,500 depending on scope. Some contractors suggest skipping permits to save money. We never do this — unpermitted work creates real problems when you sell and can void your home insurance if something goes wrong.

Cost by kitchen size: the 2026 Toronto ranges

The table below shows realistic all-in budget ranges for complete kitchen renovations in Toronto and the inner GTA by square footage. "Complete renovation" means new cabinetry, countertops, appliances, flooring, backsplash, lighting, plumbing fixtures, and paint — the full scope. It does not include layout changes or structural work, which are addressed in the notes below each tier.

Kitchen Size Typical Square Footage Entry-Level Finish Mid-Range Finish Premium Finish
Small galley / condo 60–100 sq ft $35,000–$55,000 $55,000–$90,000 $90,000–$150,000+
Small semi-detached 100–140 sq ft $45,000–$65,000 $65,000–$110,000 $110,000–$190,000+
Medium detached / townhouse 140–200 sq ft $55,000–$85,000 $85,000–$150,000 $150,000–$250,000+
Large open-plan kitchen 200–300 sq ft $75,000–$110,000 $110,000–$200,000 $200,000–$350,000+
Grand / full custom build 300+ sq ft N/A $175,000–$275,000 $275,000–$500,000+

Why these numbers may look high: Toronto renovation costs have risen significantly since 2020. Skilled trades are in short supply relative to demand, lumber and cabinetry materials remain elevated, and HST applies to all labour and materials. The floor in these ranges assumes licensed trades, proper permits, written contracts, and warranty-backed work. You can find lower quotes — but lower quotes in this market almost always mean unlicensed labour, skipped permits, or scope that disappears on paper but reappears as change orders.

Small kitchen renovations (60–140 sq ft)

The majority of kitchen renovations we do in the City of Toronto proper fall into this category: narrow galleys in condo units, tight kitchens in the post-war semis and detached homes that line the streets of East York, Leslieville, Roncesvalles, and the Annex. These spaces are small, but that does not make them inexpensive.

The fixed costs of a renovation — design, permits, project management, trade mobilization, site protection — do not scale down proportionally with kitchen size. A licensed electrician costs the same per hour whether your kitchen is 80 square feet or 180 square feet. A plumber's minimum call-out plus the work of roughing in a dishwasher connection and updating the shut-off valves takes most of a day regardless of footprint.

What small kitchens do save you on is cabinetry and countertop material. A 60-square-foot galley might have 15–18 linear feet of cabinet run. A 140-square-foot kitchen might have 22–28 linear feet. That difference in cabinet quantity translates to real dollars — but it rarely offsets the fixed cost of the project entirely.

Common scope items in small kitchen renovations

  • Full cabinet replacement: Even in entry-level projects, we rarely recommend refacing or repainting existing cabinets if the boxes are more than 20 years old. The carcasses are typically not worth preserving, and modern flat-pack or semi-custom cabinetry offers meaningfully better functionality at reasonable cost.
  • Countertop replacement: Almost always included. Quartz is the most common choice in this market segment — durable, consistent in appearance, and reasonably priced at mid-grade.
  • Backsplash tile: Labour-intensive relative to material cost, particularly in a galley where the tile work involves intricate cuts around outlets and windows. Budget $3,500–$8,000 for a complete backsplash in this size range.
  • Flooring: Typically done at the same time to avoid a seam where old meets new in an adjacent space. Engineered hardwood is common; luxury vinyl plank is gaining ground for its moisture resistance.
  • Appliances: Not always included in the renovation contract but a major budget item. A complete mid-range appliance package (refrigerator, range, dishwasher, over-range microwave) runs $6,000–$12,000. Premium integrated appliances can add $20,000–$50,000 on their own.
  • Lighting: Pot light installation, under-cabinet LED strips, and a new pendant or semi-flush fixture over the sink or peninsula are standard. Budget $3,000–$6,000 for a complete lighting upgrade including electrical work.

Medium kitchen renovations (140–200 sq ft)

This is the range we see most often in Toronto's mature suburban neighbourhoods: North York bungalows from the 1950s and 1960s, centre-hall colonials in Leaside and Don Mills, and newer townhouses in the inner suburbs. These kitchens often have original layouts that made sense for a different era of cooking and entertaining — closed off from the main living space, with minimal natural light and limited counter space relative to modern expectations.

The most common upgrade request in this category is opening the kitchen to an adjacent dining room or family room. That work — structural assessment, temporary support, beam specification, wall removal, patching of floors and ceilings at the opening — adds $12,000–$35,000 to a project budget depending on what is in the wall and what beam is required. It is transformative and almost always worth doing if you are already committing to a full renovation, because the cost of disruption is largely fixed. Doing the structural work now while the trades are already on site costs far less than doing it later as a standalone project.

Layout changes and their true cost

In a medium kitchen, layout changes are financially significant but manageable within a reasonable total budget. The questions to ask before committing to them:

  • Is the wall load-bearing? A structural engineer's assessment ($600–$1,200) is worth every dollar before any demolition starts. Non-load-bearing walls are far simpler and less expensive to remove.
  • Where does the plumbing drain? If your sink sits on an exterior wall with a drain that runs to the basement directly below, moving it even a few feet means a longer drain run and potential conflict with the floor joist layout. Get a plumber involved in the planning stage.
  • What is above? In a two-storey home, a load-bearing wall removal in the kitchen may require a beam that runs to the exterior foundation wall and supports the floor above. The structural solution for a simple-looking opening can be surprisingly involved.
  • Will the island work? Islands are extremely popular and can be excellent functional additions, but they require adequate clearance on all sides (minimum 42 inches from cabinet face to island face for a work aisle), a dedicated electrical circuit, and in most cases a plumbing rough-in if you want a sink. Measure twice before committing to an island in a space that does not comfortably accommodate one.

Large and open-plan kitchen renovations (200+ sq ft)

Large kitchens in Toronto tend to fall into two categories: homes where a wall between the original kitchen and dining room was removed in a previous renovation, creating a combined open space; and newer construction or additions where an open-plan kitchen-dining-family room was built from the start. Both present their own considerations.

For an established open-plan kitchen in the 200–300-square-foot range, the renovation scope closely mirrors a medium kitchen but with larger material quantities and more complex lighting requirements. A kitchen of this size will typically have 35–50 linear feet of cabinetry, a large island, and significant countertop coverage. Material costs scale accordingly, and the premium for well-designed storage and millwork detail becomes more pronounced at this size.

Premium kitchens at this scale are where custom cabinetry, integrated appliances, and architectural details — fluted panels, exposed shelf sections, built-in coffee stations, butler's pantries — come into play. These are not extravagances for their own sake; at this scale, the kitchen is often the architectural centrepiece of the main floor, and the design quality shows in a way that matters both for daily use and for resale value in Toronto's competitive market.

Kitchen renovations and Toronto resale value: A well-executed kitchen renovation consistently delivers strong returns in the Toronto market. Our experience — confirmed by appraisers and real estate agents we work with across Rosedale, Forest Hill, the Annex, and Leaside — is that a high-quality kitchen renovation in a home priced in the $1.5M–$3M range returns 70–90 cents on the dollar in immediate market value, and often more in days-on-market and multiple-offer positioning. The return is lower in entry-level price points, where buyers have less headroom to price in renovation quality.

Typical renovation timeline by kitchen size

Timeline is one of the most underestimated planning factors in a kitchen renovation. Most Toronto homeowners significantly underestimate how long the pre-construction phase takes relative to the actual build.

Phase Small Kitchen Medium Kitchen Large / Open-Plan
Design and selections 4–8 weeks 6–12 weeks 10–16 weeks
Permits (if required) 2–6 weeks 3–8 weeks 4–10 weeks
Cabinet lead time 6–10 weeks 8–14 weeks 12–20 weeks
Active construction 4–6 weeks 6–10 weeks 10–16 weeks
Total project duration 4–6 months 5–8 months 7–12 months

The single biggest driver of schedule extension is decisions that are not made during the design phase. Cabinet lead times from reputable manufacturers are not compressible — they are what they are. If cabinet orders are delayed because countertop material selection changed the required depth, or because a plumbing location moved after the order was placed, the entire construction phase waits. Getting all material selections locked before construction begins is not a luxury; it is the only way to run a kitchen renovation efficiently.

What the estimate should include

A complete estimate for a Toronto kitchen renovation should break down costs at a level of detail that lets you understand what you are paying for and make informed trade-offs. Be cautious of lump-sum estimates that do not itemize by trade or phase. The line items you should expect to see:

  • Demolition and disposal: Removal of existing cabinetry, countertops, flooring, and any structural elements. Dumpster or bin rental. Typically $2,500–$6,000 for a medium kitchen.
  • Structural work: Itemized separately if any wall removals, beam installations, or floor reinforcement is required. Should include the engineer's fee if applicable.
  • Rough carpentry: Framing for new openings, blocking for upper cabinets, subfloor repairs. Often bundled with structural but should be visible.
  • Plumbing rough-in and finish: Any pipe relocation, new supply/drain lines, dishwasher connection, faucet installation. These should be quoted by a licensed plumber as a separate line, not hidden in a general labour total.
  • Electrical rough-in and finish: Panel assessment and any required upgrades, new circuits for dedicated appliances, pot light rough-in, switch and outlet placement, fixture installation. Also a licensed trade that should be quoted separately.
  • Cabinetry supply and installation: The largest line item. Should specify manufacturer, door style, finish, and hardware. Installation separate from supply.
  • Countertops: Material, edge profile, cutouts, sink undermount, and installation. Quoted by the slab shop or fabricator.
  • Backsplash tile and installation: Material and labour, including grout, caulking, and any specialty cuts.
  • Flooring: Material and installation, including transitions to adjacent spaces.
  • Painting: Walls, ceiling, and trim.
  • Permits and inspection fees: Always itemized as a pass-through, not buried in overhead.
  • Project management and general conditions: Site protection, cleanup, coordination, and the contractor's overhead and margin. In a properly priced project, this is transparent, not hidden in individual trade markups.

How to use this information

The best way to use these ranges is to identify your kitchen's size tier, decide on a realistic finish level, and then sketch out which scope items apply to your project. If you are keeping the layout intact in a 160-square-foot kitchen with mid-range finishes, you are looking at a realistic starting point of $85,000–$115,000 in the current Toronto market. If that same kitchen involves removing a load-bearing wall, you add $20,000–$35,000. If you upgrade to custom cabinetry, add another $20,000–$40,000. The math is not complicated once the variables are clear.

What we caution against is bringing a number you found online — from a home improvement blog, a national average, or a renovation show — and trying to make a Toronto project fit it. The Toronto market has its own labour costs, permit requirements, and material supply chain. Projects priced to a national average inevitably come in over budget, not because the estimate was dishonest, but because the starting point was wrong.
