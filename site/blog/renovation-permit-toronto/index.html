Do you need a permit for your Toronto renovation?

A contractor's guide to building permits in Toronto — what requires one, how to get one, and what happens if you don't.

The question every homeowner asks

Almost every client we meet asks some version of the same question: do we actually need a permit for this? The honest answer is: it depends on what you are doing, but the category of work that requires a permit is broader than most people assume. Getting it wrong — in either direction — has real consequences. This guide will walk you through what the City of Toronto requires, what the process looks like, and why skipping a permit is almost never the shortcut it appears to be.

The rules that govern when permits are required in Toronto come from the Ontario Building Code and the City of Toronto's own zoning bylaws. The City's Building Division administers them, and compliance is not optional — even if the work is done inside your own home, even if your neighbours never find out, and even if the contractor suggests otherwise.

What always needs a permit

The following categories of work require a building permit in Toronto in virtually all cases. If your renovation involves any of these, assume a permit is required until you have confirmed otherwise with the City or your contractor:

  • Structural changes. Removing or altering load-bearing walls, changing the structural configuration of floors or the roof, adding a beam to open up a space — all structural work requires a permit. The permit process includes engineering review, and inspections will confirm that the work meets code before the walls are closed.
  • Additions. Any addition to the building envelope — a rear addition, a second storey, a new garage — requires both a building permit and, depending on the scope, zoning approval. Additions in Toronto almost always trigger a full review of the existing structure as well.
  • Basement suites and secondary units. Converting an unfinished basement into a legal dwelling unit requires a permit. The City has specific requirements for ceiling height, egress windows, fire separation, and ventilation that can only be satisfied through the permit and inspection process.
  • Plumbing relocation. Moving a toilet, sink, or tub to a different location — as opposed to replacing it in the same location — requires a plumbing permit. Adding a bathroom where none existed before always requires one.
  • Electrical panel upgrades. Upgrading your electrical panel from 100-amp to 200-amp service requires an electrical permit through the Electrical Safety Authority (ESA), which operates separately from the City's building permit process but is equally mandatory.
  • New window or door openings. Cutting a new opening in an exterior wall requires a permit because it affects the structure, the waterproofing, and sometimes the fire rating of the wall assembly.
  • Decks over 600mm above grade. Any deck more than 600 millimetres above the adjacent grade requires a building permit in Toronto.
  • HVAC changes. Installing a new forced-air system, adding ductwork, or significantly modifying existing mechanical systems requires a permit.

What does not need a permit

Not everything requires going through the City. The following types of work are generally exempt from permit requirements in Toronto, which means you can proceed without an application:

  • Painting, wallpaper, and other purely cosmetic surface treatments
  • New flooring — hardwood, tile, carpet, or vinyl — where no structural changes are involved
  • New countertops, backsplash, or cabinet replacements where the layout and structure do not change
  • Like-for-like fixture replacements: swapping a toilet for a new toilet in the same location, replacing a bathtub without moving drains, installing a new light fixture on an existing circuit
  • Replacing windows or doors in existing openings without enlarging the opening
  • Interior insulation improvements that do not involve changes to the structure

The practical test is this: if the work affects the structure, the plumbing rough-in, the electrical supply, or the fire separation of your home, assume a permit is required. If the work is entirely cosmetic and reversible, it likely is not.

The City of Toronto permit process

Toronto processes building permits through its Building Division, and applications are submitted online through the city's portal. What you need to submit depends on the complexity of the project, but for anything beyond the most straightforward scope, you will need drawings. On many projects, those drawings must be prepared by a licensed architect or engineer.

What the application involves

For a typical mid-size renovation — say, a rear addition or a basement suite — the application package will include architectural drawings showing the existing conditions and the proposed work, structural drawings if there are any changes to the building's structure, a site plan, and a list of how the project complies with applicable zoning bylaws. For larger or more complex projects, additional reports — arborist, heritage, planning — may be required before a permit can be issued.

Review timelines

This is where Toronto's permit process requires the most planning. The City targets a first review within a set number of business days depending on complexity, but real-world timelines for renovation permits range from three to eight months for complex projects. Projects that require zoning variances or Committee of Adjustment approval add further time. The fundamental mistake homeowners make is assuming they can start construction and get the permit later. That is not how it works, and the risks of proceeding without a permit are substantial.

Inspection stages

Once the permit is issued and construction begins, the permit process includes mandatory inspection stages. The inspector must see certain stages of the work before the next phase can proceed. Typical inspection points include:

  • Framing inspection: Before insulation or drywall is installed, an inspector must approve the rough framing, structural elements, and rough-in mechanical, plumbing, and electrical work.
  • Insulation inspection: Before drywall is hung, insulation and any required air barriers must be inspected and approved.
  • Final / occupancy inspection: Once the work is substantially complete, a final inspection confirms that the project matches the approved drawings and that all systems are functioning as required.

Failing to call for inspections — or proceeding past an inspection stage before it has been approved — puts the permit at risk and can result in orders to expose completed work for inspection, which means opening up walls you have already finished.

What permits cost

Toronto building permit fees are calculated as a percentage of the estimated value of construction. For most residential renovation projects, permit fees will fall in the range of $1,500 to $8,000, with larger or more complex projects at the high end of that range. The permit fee does not include the cost of drawing preparation, which for a project requiring an architect or engineer can add several thousand dollars. These costs are real, and they should be part of your renovation budget from the beginning — not a surprise at the application stage.

What happens if you skip the permit

The consequences of doing permitted work without a permit range from inconvenient to genuinely serious. They are not hypothetical: they happen to Toronto homeowners regularly, and they are almost always more costly than getting the permit would have been.

  • Stop-work orders. If the City becomes aware of unpermitted construction — through a neighbour complaint, a routine inspection of adjacent work, or a utility connection request — they can issue a stop-work order immediately. The project stops until the permit situation is resolved.
  • Demolition orders. In some cases, the City can order completed work torn out so that it can be inspected. If the framing was never inspected before drywall was hung, the remedy may be to open the walls. This is expensive and disruptive in proportion to how much work was done.
  • Retroactive permits. It is sometimes possible to get a permit after the fact, but the process is more complicated and more expensive than getting one upfront. The City may require destructive investigation to verify what was actually built, and they may require changes to bring the work into compliance before they will accept it.
  • Insurance voids. Most home insurance policies require that work affecting the structure, plumbing, or electrical be done with the required permits and inspections. If unpermitted work causes a loss — a fire from an unpermitted electrical installation, a flood from unpermitted plumbing — the insurer may deny the claim.

Unpermitted work must be disclosed to buyers in Ontario. Undisclosed unpermitted work discovered after closing is grounds for a lawsuit. Buyers' home inspectors and real estate lawyers are increasingly familiar with what to look for, and this issue surfaces regularly in Toronto real estate transactions.

Who pulls the permit

In Ontario, permits can be pulled either by the contractor or by the homeowner as an owner-builder. Each approach has implications worth understanding.

Contractor-pulled permits

When a licensed contractor pulls the permit, they take on responsibility for ensuring the work meets code and passes inspections. This is the standard arrangement for most professional renovation firms. The contractor's name and licence number appear on the permit, and they manage the inspection process. For the homeowner, this means you have a clear line of accountability: the person responsible for the work is also responsible for the permit.

Owner-builder permits

Homeowners can pull their own permits, and some do when they are doing significant work themselves or coordinating trades directly. An owner-builder permit means you are responsible for code compliance and for scheduling inspections. If you pull the permit but a trade does substandard work and it passes inspection, the accountability is still yours as the permit holder. Owner-builder permits also affect the availability of the Tarion new home warranty in certain situations, so this is worth understanding carefully before choosing this route.
