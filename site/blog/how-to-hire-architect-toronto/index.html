How to hire an architect in Toronto for your renovation

A contractor's perspective on when you actually need an architect, what to expect from the process, and how to avoid the mistakes that cost clients time and money.

Do you actually need an architect?

The first question worth answering honestly is whether your project requires an architect at all. In Ontario, an architect's stamp is legally required for any building exceeding 600 square metres or three storeys, and for most commercial construction. For a typical Toronto residential renovation, the legal requirement is narrower than most homeowners assume.

What most residential renovation projects in Toronto actually require is not a registered architect but a set of drawings sufficient to obtain a building permit. In many cases — a basement underpinning, a rear addition under a certain size, a second-storey addition on a straightforward semidetached — those drawings can be prepared by a qualified designer. Ontario's Building Code allows "designers" (a regulated category separate from architects) to prepare and sign off on permit drawings for most residential work. The distinction matters because designer fees are typically 30–50% lower than architect fees for comparable residential scope.

That said, there are good reasons to hire an architect even when the law does not require it. If your project is architecturally complex — a custom home, a significant addition that alters the character of the house, a heritage property with Conservation Review Board requirements, or a site with unusual constraints — a registered architect brings both technical expertise and regulated accountability that a designer cannot match. Architects carry mandatory errors and omissions insurance, are licensed through the Ontario Association of Architects (OAA), and can be held to a professional standard of care. On a project where the drawings really matter, that accountability has real value.

Our honest advice after building in Toronto for 25 years: match the professional to the project. A full rear addition and second-storey addition on a Victorian semi in Leslieville benefits from an architect. A bathroom gut and reno in a postwar bungalow in Scarborough does not.

When to hire an architect versus a designer

Understanding the difference helps you spend your budget where it actually matters.

Projects that typically benefit from a registered architect

  • Custom new builds: A custom home requires coordinated structural, mechanical, and architectural drawings. An architect manages the design intent across all of these in a way that a designer typically cannot.
  • Significant additions (above grade, 500 sq ft or more): The larger and more architecturally ambitious the addition, the more value an architect's spatial and structural thinking adds — particularly if the addition must integrate with an existing home in a way that reads as intentional rather than bolted on.
  • Heritage properties: The City of Toronto's Heritage Register covers thousands of properties. If your home is individually listed or within a Heritage Conservation District, you will need drawings that satisfy the Toronto Preservation Office, and an architect with heritage experience will navigate that process more effectively than a residential designer.
  • Complex sites: Ravine lot setbacks, Committee of Adjustment variances, situations requiring a Toronto and Region Conservation Authority permit — projects with multiple regulatory layers benefit from an architect who has navigated those agencies before.
  • Projects with real design ambition: If the appearance of your finished home genuinely matters to you — not just functionally but architecturally — a good architect will deliver something a set of contractor drawings will not.

Projects where a qualified designer is usually sufficient

  • Basement renovations, including underpinning and lowering
  • Straightforward rear additions under 500 sq ft
  • Kitchen and bathroom renovations involving layout changes
  • Interior structural modifications (removing load-bearing walls)
  • Garage conversions and laneway suite drawings

A note on laneway suites and garden suites: Toronto's laneway suite program has specific design requirements, and the City's approval process has particular drawings requirements. Some designers specialize specifically in laneway and garden suite applications. An architect is not required, but the designer's experience with Toronto's specific process matters more than credentials alone.

What architects actually do — and what they don't

A lot of client frustration with architects (and with contractors who work alongside architects) comes from mismatched expectations about what the architect is actually responsible for. Here is a clear breakdown of the typical scope.

What an architect does

Architects provide design services, not construction management. The standard OAA services are organized into phases, and many residential architects offer only some of them. Understanding which phases you are hiring for prevents expensive misunderstandings.

  • Schematic design: Develops the overall concept for the project — massing, layout, relationship between spaces. This is where the big decisions get made.
  • Design development: Refines the schematic into specific dimensions, materials, and systems. This is where you choose windows, cladding, and structural approaches.
  • Construction documents: Produces the permit drawings and detailed construction drawings that contractors price from and build to. The quality of these documents has a direct effect on the accuracy of your contractor quotes and on what happens during construction.
  • Permit coordination: Submits to and coordinates with the City of Toronto Building Division, responding to reviewer comments and resubmitting as needed.
  • Construction review: Site visits during construction to review conformance with the drawings. This is often where homeowners cut scope to save money — and often where they regret it.

What an architect typically does not do

  • Manage the contractor or the day-to-day construction process
  • Make binding decisions about construction means and methods
  • Supervise trades or enforce subcontractor work quality
  • Source or specify every finish (interior designers handle this)
  • Guarantee that construction will be built exactly to the drawings

This last point trips up homeowners more than any other. An architect who provides construction review services visits the site periodically and flags deviations from the drawings — but the contractor remains responsible for the quality and execution of the work. A good working relationship between your architect and your contractor is not a luxury; it is a prerequisite for a successful project.

What does an architect cost in Toronto?

Architect fees in Toronto for residential work are typically structured as a percentage of construction cost or as a fixed fee agreed at the outset. Percentage fees for residential work generally run between 8% and 15% of construction cost, depending on the complexity of the project and the scope of services included.

Service Scope Typical Fee Structure Estimated Cost (CAD)
Permit drawings only (small addition / basement) Fixed fee $4,000 – $10,000
Full design + permit drawings (rear addition) Fixed fee or % of construction $15,000 – $35,000
Full design + permit + construction review (addition) % of construction (10–12%) $25,000 – $60,000
Custom home — full OAA services % of construction (8–12%) $80,000 – $200,000+
Heritage application support Fixed fee (additive) $5,000 – $20,000
Committee of Adjustment report / drawings Fixed fee $3,500 – $8,000

These figures reflect the Toronto market in 2026 and apply to registered architects. A qualified residential designer working on the same permit drawings will typically charge 30–50% less for equivalent scope. The premium you pay for an OAA architect buys professional liability insurance, regulated accountability, and — on the right project — a level of design sophistication that a designer is not trained to provide.

On large projects, the architect fee often saves money downstream. Detailed, coordinated construction documents reduce contractor uncertainty, which reduces contingency pricing. Vague drawings produce high quotes; precise drawings produce competitive ones. We have seen $2 million custom homes where tight architectural documents saved the client more in competitive contractor pricing than the architect fee cost in the first place.

How to find and vet the right architect

Finding an architect in Toronto is not difficult — the OAA's public directory lists all licensed practitioners in Ontario. Finding the right architect for your specific project and working style takes more effort.

Start with referrals and portfolio review

The same principle that applies to finding a contractor applies here: the best source is a referral from someone whose finished project you have actually seen. If you've admired a neighbour's addition, ask who designed it. If a friend went through a similar process, find out whether they'd hire the same architect again — and why or why not.

Architectural portfolios are more useful than contractor portfolios because design authorship is clearer. Look for projects that are similar in scale, typology, and neighbourhood context to yours. An architect who specializes in high-budget modern custom homes may not be the right fit for a sensitive addition on a heritage property in Cabbagetown. Look for evidence that the architect understands and has worked within Toronto's regulatory environment — not just produced beautiful images.

Questions to ask before you hire

When you meet with a prospective architect, go beyond the portfolio. These are the questions that predict whether the working relationship will be effective:

  • What does your permit approval track record look like for projects like mine? Ask how many revisions are typically required and what the average permit timeline has been for comparable projects. Toronto Building Division review times have been a major source of project delays; an experienced architect knows how to submit complete applications that minimize revision cycles.
  • How do you work with contractors during construction? Some architects are highly collaborative with contractors and respond quickly to site questions and RFIs (requests for information). Others are difficult to reach and slow to issue drawings revisions. Ask for a reference from a contractor they have worked with, not just client references.
  • Will you be the primary contact throughout, or will a junior staff member run the project? In larger firms, the principal who presents the portfolio may hand off day-to-day contact to a project architect or intern architect. Know who you are actually working with before you sign.
  • What happens when the design needs to change during construction? Cost and schedule impacts from design changes during construction are real. Understand how the architect handles change requests, how quickly they turn around revised drawings, and how they charge for changes.
  • Are you familiar with the specific permit requirements for my project type? Toronto's zoning bylaws, heritage policies, and Building Division review processes are complex and change regularly. An architect who last did a rear addition in your neighbourhood five years ago may be less useful than one who submitted four last year.

Verify their registration

Every registered architect in Ontario must be licensed by the OAA. You can verify registration through the OAA's public directory at oaa.on.ca. It takes two minutes and confirms that the person you're hiring is actually licensed to practice — not just calling themselves an architect, which is a protected title in Ontario. Anyone representing themselves as an architect without OAA registration is committing an offence under the Architects Act.

Design-build as an alternative: If the architect/contractor coordination feels like a lot to manage, a design-build firm integrates both under one contract. The advantage is single-point accountability and faster decisions during construction. The trade-off is that you don't have an independent design professional whose interests are separate from the contractor's. For projects where the design itself is the primary driver, a traditional architect-led process is usually preferable. For projects where schedule, cost certainty, and integration matter most, design-build often performs better.

The architect-contractor relationship: why it matters on your project

As a contractor, we work with architects on probably a third of the projects we build each year. The quality of that relationship — between the design team and the construction team — has more effect on project outcomes than most homeowners realize.

A good architect produces construction documents that are complete, coordinated, and buildable. They respond to RFIs quickly (within 24–48 hours on an active project), issue supplemental instructions clearly, and visit the site at meaningful intervals rather than just at the beginning and end. They treat the contractor as a partner in delivering the project, not an adversary trying to cut corners.

A difficult architect produces vague documents full of "by contractor" notes that shift design decisions onto trades who aren't qualified to make them, takes weeks to answer questions, and visits the site only when there's a dispute. The result is slower construction, more change orders, and a finished product that doesn't quite match the design intent.

You can assess this before you hire by asking the architect for a contractor reference — not just client references. Call that contractor and ask: how complete were the drawings at the time of tender? How quickly did the architect respond to site questions? Would you work with them again? The answers will tell you a great deal.

When Kopman Build is involved early in the process — before the architect has finalized the design — we can flag constructability issues that save time and money later. An architect who welcomes that input early produces better projects than one who treats contractor involvement as an intrusion on the design process. If the architect you're considering is resistant to early contractor collaboration, that's worth noting.

The typical process, step by step

For a major renovation or addition in Toronto, here is how the architect engagement typically unfolds from initial meeting to permit issuance. Timelines are realistic estimates based on current Toronto Building Division review volumes.

Phase What Happens Typical Duration
Discovery & schematic design Site visit, program development, concept design options 3 – 6 weeks
Design development Refined layouts, structural coordination, material selections 4 – 8 weeks
Construction documents Full permit drawing set, specifications, details 4 – 10 weeks
Permit submission & review City of Toronto Building Division review; revisions if required 8 – 20 weeks
Contractor tender Drawings issued to contractors for pricing 3 – 5 weeks
Construction review Periodic site visits, RFI responses, supplemental instructions Duration of construction

The permit review timeline is the wildcard in Toronto. The Building Division has made progress in reducing review times for certain project types, but a complex addition requiring zoning review, heritage input, or a variance can add months. An experienced architect who submits complete applications and responds to reviewer comments quickly can materially reduce this time. Hiring someone who is new to Toronto's specific processes can add unnecessary weeks.

Putting it together

Hiring an architect for your Toronto renovation is a significant decision — both in terms of cost and in terms of the professional relationship you are entering into for the duration of your project. The clients we see get the most value from their architects are the ones who were clear about their project goals from the start, selected someone whose relevant experience matched their project type, and stayed actively engaged throughout the design process rather than delegating and then reappearing at permit stage.

The clients who have the most difficulty are typically those who hired based on a beautiful portfolio without checking whether the architect had experience with Toronto's specific regulatory environment, or who cut the construction review phase to save money and then had no design professional on site when deviations from the drawings occurred.
