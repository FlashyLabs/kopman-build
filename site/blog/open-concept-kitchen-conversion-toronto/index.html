Open concept kitchen conversion in Toronto

What it costs, what the process looks like, and what your 1950s Toronto semi-detached is actually going to throw at you — from a contractor who has done hundreds of them.

What an open concept conversion actually involves

When most Toronto homeowners talk about an open concept kitchen conversion, they mean one thing: removing the wall or walls that separate the kitchen from an adjacent dining room, living room, or both. The end result is a combined, flowing space where cooking, dining, and living happen in one continuous room. It is one of the most consistently requested renovations we do, and it transforms how a house feels — particularly in the older semi-detached and detached homes that make up a large portion of Toronto's housing stock.

But "remove a wall" is a description, not a scope of work. Depending on your home's construction, what looks like a simple demolition job can involve structural engineering, a steel or laminated veneer lumber (LVL) beam, temporary shoring, relocated mechanical systems, new electrical circuits, matched flooring, new ductwork, and a full kitchen renovation to take advantage of the reconfigured space. The wall removal itself might take two days. The complete project typically takes ten to sixteen weeks.

This guide walks through every component of that process: the structural realities, the permit requirements, the real cost ranges in today's Toronto market, and the sequence of work so you know what to expect before the first hammer swings.

The structural question: load-bearing or not?

This is the question that determines more about your budget and timeline than anything else. Every wall in a house is either load-bearing — meaning it carries structural weight from above and transfers it to the foundation — or non-load-bearing, meaning it is purely a partition with no structural role. In Toronto homes built before 1980, and particularly in the post-war brick semis and detached houses that dominate neighbourhoods like The Junction, Danforth Village, Leslieville, and Etobicoke, the wall between the kitchen and the dining room is more often than not load-bearing. That is the default assumption we bring to any site visit until proven otherwise.

How to tell if a wall is load-bearing

A few general indicators are useful, though none is definitive without opening up the structure. Walls that run perpendicular to the floor joists are frequently load-bearing. Walls located near the centre of the house, stacked above one another on multiple floors, or located directly below a ridge beam or floor system are also suspect. In a typical Toronto two-storey semi-detached home, the wall between the kitchen and the rear dining area is often the one point where the second floor's weight is transferred down to the basement structure.

The only way to know for certain is to have a structural engineer review your plans and, in most cases, inspect the framing in your basement and attic. This is not optional. We do not remove walls in Toronto homes without a structural engineer's involvement — not because it is legally required in every case, but because the consequences of getting it wrong are serious and permanent.

When the wall is load-bearing

Removing a load-bearing wall requires replacing its structural function with a beam — typically either a steel I-beam (W-beam) or an engineered wood beam such as LVL, PSL, or LSL — supported by posts or columns that carry the load down to a proper foundation point. The beam must be sized by a structural engineer based on the span, the tributary load above, and the specific framing configuration of your home. A beam that spans a 12-foot opening in a bungalow carrying roof load is a very different engineering problem than one spanning the same distance in a two-storey home with a second floor and a roof above.

Installation involves temporary shoring to support the floor and structure above while the wall is removed and the beam is set. Depending on the size of the beam, it may need to be brought in through a window opening or assembled in place. The posts carrying each end of the beam need to bear on something solid below — often this means adding a new beam or post in the basement, which adds to both cost and complexity. None of this is unusual or alarming; it is simply the work that the job requires.

Toronto homes and hidden complexity: Older Toronto homes frequently have surprises inside their walls — knob-and-tube wiring, cast iron drain stacks, older gas lines, or galvanized water supply pipes that have been waiting for an opportunity to fail. Budget a contingency of 10–15% for unforeseen conditions; in homes built before 1960, make it 20%. These are not contractor excuses; they are realities of working with century-old and mid-century construction.

Cost breakdown for a Toronto open concept kitchen conversion

The numbers below reflect what we actually see on projects in Toronto and the inner GTA in 2026. They are ranges because every home is different, and the single most significant variable — whether the wall is load-bearing — creates a major fork in the cost tree. We have presented them as two scenarios: a straightforward non-load-bearing removal with basic finishing, and a load-bearing removal with beam work and a concurrent kitchen renovation.

A note on those kitchen renovation figures: $45,000 gets you a well-executed mid-range kitchen with semi-custom cabinets, stone countertops, and standard appliances. At $90,000 to $120,000, you are looking at fully custom cabinetry, integrated appliances, a statement island, and high-end stone or sintered surfaces. Many of our Toronto clients undertaking a full open concept conversion and kitchen renovation land in the $110,000 to $160,000 range all-in, which reflects the full scope of structural work, trades, and a properly executed kitchen.

What the permit process looks like in Toronto

Any structural change to a Toronto home requires a building permit from the City of Toronto's Building Division. This includes the removal of any load-bearing wall, and in practice it includes most non-load-bearing wall removals that involve changes to mechanical, electrical, or plumbing systems as well. Do not let any contractor tell you a wall removal does not need a permit until they have reviewed the specific scope with a professional. The cost of getting it wrong — unpermitted structural work, insurance exposure, mandatory remediation at time of sale — is far higher than the cost of the permit itself.

The permit process in Toronto generally works as follows:

  • Pre-application: Your contractor or designer prepares drawings showing the existing and proposed conditions, the beam and post design (stamped by a structural engineer for load-bearing work), and any affected mechanical systems. For straightforward conversions this is typically a set of simple as-built and proposed floor plans with structural notes.
  • Application submission: The permit is applied for through the City's ePLAN system. As of 2026, the City's target review timeline for residential permits of this type is 10 to 20 business days, though actual timelines vary. Complex applications or those with incomplete submissions take longer.
  • Inspections: Once the permit is issued and work begins, inspections are required at specific stages: framing inspection before walls close, and final inspection upon completion. Your contractor coordinates these. Skipping inspections creates problems when you sell the home.
  • Permit closure: The permit is closed when the final inspection is passed. Keep the final inspection report — it is documentation that the work was done to code, which buyers and their lawyers may ask for.

Realistically, factor four to eight weeks for permit approval into your project schedule before construction begins. If you are doing design work and permit preparation in parallel, that time can overlap with the planning phase rather than delaying it.

Project timeline: what to expect week by week

Open concept kitchen conversions in Toronto follow a predictable sequence, though the total calendar duration depends heavily on how long the permit process takes and whether the kitchen renovation is concurrent. Here is a typical timeline for a load-bearing wall removal combined with a full kitchen renovation:

  • Weeks 1–3 — Design and documentation: Measurements, as-built drawings, structural engineering review, material selections. Kitchen design is finalized so cabinet lead times can be placed immediately; custom cabinets in Toronto take 8 to 14 weeks from order to delivery.
  • Weeks 2–6 — Permit application and review: Running concurrently with cabinet fabrication. This is the waiting period; the project is being planned and materials are on order while the permit is processed.
  • Week 7 — Mobilization and demolition: Site protection goes up, kitchen is disconnected, wall demolition proceeds. For a typical Toronto kitchen this takes one to three days. Surprises discovered in the wall (wiring, plumbing, ductwork) are assessed and the plan adjusted if necessary.
  • Weeks 7–8 — Structural work: Temporary shoring, beam installation, post setting, and foundation reinforcement if required. The framing inspection is called at the end of this phase.
  • Weeks 8–10 — Rough-in trades: Electrical rough-in, new circuits, HVAC ductwork relocation, and any plumbing rough-in for relocated sink or island. Rough-in inspections as required.
  • Week 10 — Insulation, drywall, and boarding: Walls close up, the space starts to look like a room again.
  • Weeks 11–12 — Cabinets and flooring: Cabinets are installed, flooring goes in across the unified space. This is when the transformation becomes visible.
  • Weeks 12–14 — Countertops, appliances, and finishes: Countertop templating after cabinets are set (allow 7–14 days for fabrication), appliance installation, backsplash, trim, lighting, and hardware.
  • Week 14–16 — Deficiency list and final inspection: Walk-through, deficiency corrections, final City inspection, permit closure.

The cabinet lead time trap: The most common cause of schedule delay on kitchen conversions in Toronto is not structural work or permits — it is cabinetry ordered too late. Semi-custom cabinets from local Toronto suppliers typically run 8–12 weeks. Fully custom cabinetry from small Toronto shops can be 14–20 weeks. Order before your permit is in hand, not after. A reputable contractor will coordinate this timing deliberately.

Factors that significantly affect your final cost

Beyond the load-bearing question, several other variables push open concept conversion costs up or down materially. The ones that most consistently surprise homeowners are:

  • Matching existing hardwood floors. Many Toronto homes have original strip hardwood in the living and dining areas that was never extended into the kitchen, which had vinyl, linoleum, or tile instead. Matching and extending the hardwood into the unified kitchen space — or replacing everything to match — adds $5,000 to $18,000 depending on the floor area and whether the original species and stain can be matched. In some cases, refinishing all floors in the open space after patching is the most consistent result.
  • Electrical panel capacity. Kitchen renovations typically add circuits for under-cabinet lighting, a dedicated island outlet circuit, upgraded appliances, and sometimes in-floor heating. If your existing panel is at capacity or is a Federal Pacific or Zinsco panel (common in Toronto homes from the 1960s and 70s), a panel upgrade adds $3,500 to $7,000 and is non-negotiable from both a code and safety standpoint.
  • Chimney breast or soffit removal. Many Toronto homes have a chimney that passes through the kitchen wall being removed, or a large ceiling soffit built around ductwork that will look out of place in an open space. Removing or integrating these elements adds scope and cost that is easy to underestimate in early planning.
  • Island plumbing. Running a drain and supply to a kitchen island in an older Toronto home often means cutting the basement ceiling and rerouting drain lines to maintain proper slope. Expect $3,500 to $7,000 for island plumbing depending on your basement configuration.
  • Permit and engineering complexity. Projects that require more extensive structural documentation, heritage review (applicable in some Toronto neighbourhoods), or multiple permit revisions cost more in both professional fees and calendar time.

Is it worth doing the kitchen renovation at the same time?

In almost every case, yes. The economics are straightforward: the trades are already mobilized, the walls are already open, and the kitchen is already gutted to make the structural work accessible. Doing the kitchen renovation six months later means a second mobilization, a second disruption to your household, and higher overall cost because you are paying for setup, protection, and management twice. We have seen homeowners defer the kitchen to "save money" and then spend $15,000 to $25,000 more than they would have by combining the scopes.

There is also the design argument. An open concept conversion done without a kitchen renovation leaves you with a modern, open layout and a kitchen that was designed to be a closed, galley-style room. The cabinet layout, the island placement, the range hood position — all of these things change when the wall comes down and the space is reimagined. Doing the design properly means treating the kitchen and the conversion as one project, not two.

What to look for in a contractor for this work

Open concept conversions involve more trades coordination than most homeowners expect: structural, framing, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, flooring, cabinetry, countertop fabrication, and painting, all sequenced in a specific order. A general contractor who manages all of these in-house or through established subcontractor relationships is significantly lower-risk than a kitchen renovation company that subcontracts the structural work to whoever is available. Specifically, look for:

  • Experience with structural work in Toronto's older housing stock, not just cosmetic kitchen renovations
  • An established relationship with a structural engineer they have worked with before, not a cold referral
  • A clear process for how change orders are handled when surprises show up inside the wall
  • References from projects that involved load-bearing wall removal, specifically — not just general kitchen renovations
  • Proof of WSIB coverage and general liability insurance in writing, before you sign anything

The result is worth it

Toronto homeowners who go through an open concept kitchen conversion consistently report that it is the single most transformative renovation they have done — more than a finished basement, more than a bathroom addition, more than new windows. The reason is simple: it changes how the house is used every single day. The kitchen stops being a place you disappear to and becomes the centre of the home. Meals are cooked in the same room where family and guests gather. Natural light that was blocked by the wall reaches further into the space. The home feels larger without gaining a single square foot.

Done properly — with the right structural work, the right design, and the right kitchen to take advantage of the new layout — it also adds measurable value to your property. In Toronto's market, an open, functional main floor with a well-executed kitchen is a significant competitive advantage when it comes time to sell.
