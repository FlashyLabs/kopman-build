Renovating in The Beaches: cottage character meets modern living

The east end's most distinctive neighbourhood demands a renovation approach that is equal parts preservation and practicality. Here is what 25 years of working in these homes has taught us.

What makes The Beaches different

The Beaches — or The Beach, depending on who you ask and how local they are — is unlike most Toronto neighbourhoods from a renovation standpoint. The housing stock is overwhelmingly pre-war: bungalows, two-storey cottages, and narrow-lot semis built between the 1900s and 1940s, most of them originally constructed as summer retreats for families who wanted to be close to Lake Ontario without leaving the city. That origin story shapes everything about renovating here.

These homes were built for a different way of living. Rooms are smaller than what a modern family expects. Ceilings are low by contemporary standards. Kitchens were designed as working rooms, not social ones. Basement ceilings often clear 6′4″ only with aggressive underpinning. And the construction methods of the era — balloon framing, knob-and-tube wiring, cast-iron plumbing, plaster walls over wood lath — present real complications the moment you open up a wall or pull a permit.

At the same time, these homes have something that almost no post-war Toronto house has: genuine character. Original fir floors. Wood casings with back bands and plinth blocks. Deep window sills. Brick chimneys with corbelled caps. The exterior massing of a well-proportioned cottage, on a street lined with mature trees that were themselves planted a century ago. Owners renovate these homes because they love them, and the best renovations honour that.

The challenge — and the reason this guide exists — is that renovating a Beaches home requires balancing what you want to add with what you cannot afford to lose. That balance is harder than it sounds, and it is made more complicated by the neighbourhood's permit environment, its zoning constraints, and the genuine physical complexity of working in 80- to 120-year-old construction.

The most common renovations in The Beaches

After working in this neighbourhood since the early 2000s, we see the same project types come up repeatedly. Each has its own set of constraints that are specific to Beaches housing stock.

Kitchen renovations

The original kitchens in these homes were galley-style, tucked against the back wall, and separated from the dining room by a wall that felt load-bearing even when it was not. The most common ask is opening the kitchen to the rear living area to create the open-plan layout that modern families expect. This is almost always achievable, but the devil is in the details.

In balloon-framed construction, removing even a seemingly minor partition can have structural implications that are not obvious until you open the ceiling. We always engage a structural engineer before removing any wall in a pre-war Beaches home — the fee is $1,500 to $3,000 and it eliminates surprises that could cost ten times that to address mid-project.

Kitchen renovations in The Beaches typically range from $65,000 to $150,000 depending on scope. A targeted refresh — new cabinets, counters, appliances, and flooring, with no structural changes — sits at the lower end. A full reconfiguration with wall removal, plumbing relocation, and high-end finishes sits at the upper end and beyond.

Basement underpinning

This is the single most transformative renovation available to a Beaches homeowner, and also the most structurally demanding. The typical original basement has a 6′0″ to 6′4″ ceiling height — usable as storage, but not as a finished living space that a family will actually enjoy. Underpinning lowers the floor slab to achieve a finished ceiling height of 7′6″ to 8′6″, turning dead square footage into a functional bedroom, family room, or income suite.

Underpinning costs in Toronto — and The Beaches specifically, where lot access can be constrained — run between $350 and $500 per linear foot of perimeter wall. A typical 800-square-foot Beaches basement might have 120 to 140 linear feet of exterior wall, putting the underpinning alone at $42,000 to $70,000. The finished basement build-out on top of that typically adds another $60,000 to $100,000 depending on finishes and whether plumbing rough-ins for a full bathroom are included.

Second-storey additions

Many Beaches bungalows have owners who want more space but cannot or will not sell. A second-storey addition is the answer, but it is one of the most complex projects in residential construction. The existing structure must be assessed for its capacity to carry additional load, the foundation may need reinforcement, and the roof is entirely rebuilt. In The Beaches, the additional consideration is the neighbourhood's heritage character guidelines and the city's review process for additions that alter a home's massing or roofline on certain streets.

A full second-storey addition in The Beaches runs from $400,000 to $650,000 for a typical bungalow, including design, engineering, permits, structural work, and finishes. This is not a project to approach without a thorough pre-design feasibility assessment and a realistic conversation about timelines: from initial design to occupancy, 18 to 24 months is a realistic expectation.

Bathroom renovations

The bathrooms in pre-war Beaches homes are characteristically small — a 5′×7′ footprint was standard — and the plumbing behind the walls is often cast iron or galvanized steel that is well past its service life. A targeted bathroom renovation that replaces fixtures and finishes but keeps plumbing in place runs $25,000 to $45,000. A renovation that relocates plumbing, upgrades the drain stack, or reconfigures the room footprint runs $50,000 to $85,000 and up.

Permits in The Beaches: what you need to know

The Beaches sits within the City of Toronto's standard building permit jurisdiction, with most renovation projects falling under the Toronto Building Division's review process. But the neighbourhood has additional layers of complexity that homeowners should understand before they start.

Heritage designations and character guidelines

Several streets in The Beaches fall within the Neighbourhood Character Study area that the City of Toronto has used to assess and restrict certain types of alterations. Specifically, additions that significantly alter a home's front-facing massing, roofline, or the relationship between the building and the street can trigger a heritage planning review even on properties that are not individually designated under the Ontario Heritage Act.

The practical implication is that a rear addition is almost always simpler to approve than a front-facing change. Second-storey additions on certain Beaches streets may require a Zoning By-law Amendment or Committee of Adjustment variance before a building permit can be issued. Your contractor should identify this at the design stage, not after drawings are complete.

What typically requires a permit

  • Any structural work, including wall removal, beam installations, and foundation alterations (underpinning always requires a permit)
  • Additions of any size, including decks over 600mm above grade
  • New plumbing fixtures or drain relocations, including adding a bathroom in a basement
  • Electrical panel upgrades and any work that adds new circuits or changes the service size
  • HVAC modifications, including adding forced-air ducts to previously unserviced areas
  • Changes to exterior windows or doors that alter size or location (in some cases)

The unpermitted work problem: We see unpermitted work in nearly every Beaches home we open up — an electrical panel that was upsized without a permit, a load-bearing wall removed and replaced with an undersized beam, plumbing work done without inspection. This creates real problems: your insurance may not cover a claim related to that work, you will need to disclose it on sale, and the new work often has to be remediated before we can close walls on the current project. A permit pulled in the contractor's name protects you.

Preserving character while modernizing: the balancing act

The most common mistake we see in Beaches renovations is a design that treats the home's existing character as an obstacle rather than an asset. Strip out the original trim, replace the fir floors with wide-plank engineered hardwood, and box in the original staircase with drywall — and you have created a house that is neither old nor new, and that has lost the qualities that justified its purchase price.

The better approach is to be explicit about what you are preserving before the design is finalized. We typically walk through a Beaches home with a client before scoping begins and identify three categories:

  • Preserve as-is: Original fir floors in good condition, original trim profiles, wood casings and plinth blocks, masonry chimneys, original exterior siding where intact. These elements are typically worth the cost of restoration rather than replacement.
  • Restore or match: Where original elements are damaged or missing, match the existing profile rather than substituting a modern equivalent. A missing piece of back-band casing is a millwork order, not a reason to strip all the trim in the room.
  • Complement, don't copy: New additions and new rooms should be designed to read as contemporary spaces that are clearly in dialogue with the original home, not imitations of it. A rear addition with clean lines and large glazing reads honestly as new construction and makes the original cottage feel more deliberate by contrast.

This discipline applies to mechanical systems too. When we replace the heating system in a Beaches home, we typically route new HVAC in ways that avoid lowering ceilings in original rooms — even when that adds cost. Dropping a beam pocket into a 9-foot ceiling to run ductwork to the back of the house saves $3,000 at rough-in and reduces the room's character permanently. It is rarely the right trade.

Original fir floors

Most pre-war Beaches homes have original Douglas fir floors under whatever flooring was installed in subsequent decades. We find them under carpet, under 1970s vinyl tile, under laminate. When they are in recoverable condition — which they usually are — refinishing original fir floors is one of the highest-value interventions in a Beaches renovation. The cost runs $8 to $14 per square foot for sanding, staining, and finishing, compared to $18 to $35 per square foot to supply and install new hardwood. And the original floor, with its patina and tight grain, is almost always more beautiful than anything new.

Kitchens that feel like they belong

The Beaches kitchen that works best is not the one that could have come from a Vaughan new-build. It is the one where the cabinet profiles, hardware, and countertop material feel continuous with the house's era while functioning at a completely modern standard. Shaker-profile painted cabinets, unlacquered brass hardware, stone counters with a simple eased edge, and subway tile in a traditional running bond pattern: these choices cost no more than their trend-forward equivalents and they look correct in a 1920s cottage in a way that flat-front cabinets and quartz with dramatic veining simply do not.

On rear additions specifically: The most successful rear additions we have built in The Beaches share a common trait: they are clearly subordinate to the original house. A single-storey addition set back from the rear wall of the original structure, with a flat or shed roof and large south-facing glazing, reads as a considered intervention. A two-storey addition that matches the original roofline but extends the full width of the lot reads as an attempt to expand without acknowledging what was there. The former adds value and character; the latter tends to feel awkward from every angle.

Working in narrow-lot Beaches homes: logistics matter

The typical Beaches lot runs 25 to 30 feet wide, with a detached garage at the rear accessed from a lane. This creates real logistical constraints that affect project cost and schedule in ways that homeowners rarely anticipate.

Site access and material staging

On a 25-foot-wide lot with the house set close to both side property lines, there is often no safe route to bring equipment or materials to the rear of the property without going through the house or over the neighbour's property. We manage this by staging materials on the boulevard with proper City of Toronto encroachment permits — which add cost and require lead time — and by planning demolition and rough-in sequencing carefully to minimize the number of trips through finished spaces.

For basement underpinning specifically, the soil excavation is labour-intensive in a way that it would not be on a larger property where a small excavator can access the rear yard. Hand-digging underpinning sections in a space that machine equipment cannot reach is slower and more expensive per linear foot. We factor this into our pricing explicitly; contractors who do not are setting up a change order conversation mid-project.

Living through the renovation

Many Beaches homeowners plan to live in the house during renovation. This is possible for targeted projects, but for larger scopes, it creates real costs: dust control, temporary kitchens, maintaining access to at least one functioning bathroom, and the scheduling complexity of working around a family. Our rule of thumb: if the project touches more than two rooms simultaneously, plan for temporary accommodation for at least the rough-in phase. The cost of a furnished short-term rental for six to eight weeks is real money, but it is significantly less than the cost of doing rough-in work around people trying to live in the space.

Timeline expectations for Beaches renovation projects

The following timeline estimates assume a normal permit approval process. The City of Toronto's Building Division typically takes 10 to 20 business days to issue a permit for a straightforward residential renovation, and 6 to 12 weeks for projects that require zoning review or go through Committee of Adjustment. Plan for the longer end when scheduling trades.

  • Kitchen renovation (targeted): 6 to 10 weeks on site after permit (if required) or material lead time
  • Bathroom renovation: 4 to 8 weeks on site
  • Basement underpinning + finish: 16 to 24 weeks on site; add 8 to 12 weeks for permit and engineering
  • Rear addition (one storey): 20 to 28 weeks on site; add 12 to 20 weeks for design, permit, and zoning
  • Second-storey addition: 10 to 14 months on site; allow 18 to 24 months total from design start to occupancy
  • Full home renovation: 12 to 18 months on site depending on scope

These are honest timelines, not marketing timelines. Anyone who tells you a second-storey addition in Toronto takes six months is either describing a miracle project or setting you up for disappointment. The permit process alone, particularly if it involves a Committee of Adjustment hearing, can take four to six months before a shovel enters the ground.

Choosing the right contractor for a Beaches project

Not every contractor who does good work in Etobicoke or North York is the right fit for a pre-war Beaches cottage. The specific skills and experience that matter here are different from what you need on a new-build suburban project, and it is worth asking directly about them.

When you are evaluating contractors for a Beaches renovation, the questions that matter most include:

  • Have you worked on balloon-framed homes specifically? The framing systems in pre-war Beaches houses behave differently than platform-framed construction. A contractor who has only worked on post-war homes may not recognize the structural implications of a seemingly routine wall removal.
  • Do you have experience with knob-and-tube wiring remediation? Most Beaches homes still have some original electrical, and any renovation that involves opening walls will expose it. The decision about how to address it — full replacement, partial remediation, or working around it with an electrical engineer's sign-off — requires experience and judgment, not just a licence.
  • Can you show me a Beaches project or comparable east-end project that you have completed? The specific challenges of these homes are visible in finished projects. Ask to see not just photos but the project file: the original condition, the decisions made, the result.
  • How do you handle the discovery of asbestos-containing materials? Pre-war homes frequently have asbestos in floor tiles, pipe insulation, and roofing materials. A contractor who does not have a clear protocol for abatement — including engagement of a licensed abatement firm and proper disposal documentation — is not equipped for this work.

What a well-executed Beaches renovation produces

The Beaches sits at the intersection of genuine housing scarcity and consistent demand. Detached homes on the better streets routinely trade above $2.5 million, and there are relatively few of them. A well-executed renovation in this neighbourhood does not just make your life better while you live in the house — it compounds the asset value over time in a market that rewards quality and authenticity.

The renovations that hold their value best in The Beaches are the ones that read as investments in the house rather than trendy overlays on top of it. Structural improvements — underpinning, structural reinforcement, systems upgrades — are invisible but meaningful. Finished spaces that feel continuous with the home's character attract buyers who are looking for exactly that combination of old and new. And the homes that have been carefully maintained and thoughtfully improved over time are the ones that attract multiple offers even in a flat market.
