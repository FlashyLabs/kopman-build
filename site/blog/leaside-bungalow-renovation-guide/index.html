The Leaside bungalow renovation guide: second storeys and full renos

A practical breakdown of Leaside's most common renovation projects — costs, timelines, zoning considerations, and what you'll find behind the walls of a 1940s–60s Toronto bungalow.

Why Leaside bungalows are a renovation contractor's most familiar project

Leaside is one of the most consistently renovated neighbourhoods in Toronto, and it has been for the better part of two decades. The housing stock is the reason. The neighbourhood was developed primarily between the late 1930s and the early 1960s, which means a large proportion of its detached homes are one-and-a-half or two-storey houses built on generous lots — along with a significant number of bungalows that were built to accommodate returning veterans and young families in the postwar years. Those bungalows, typically 950 to 1,300 square feet on the main floor, are sitting on lots that regularly run 30 to 40 feet wide and 120 feet deep, in a neighbourhood where detached homes now trade north of $2 million.

The math drives the decision. Buying a larger home in Leaside at current prices means competing in one of the tightest resale markets in the city. Renovating or adding to what you already own — particularly if you purchased more than eight or ten years ago — is often the more financially rational path. For many Leaside homeowners, a second storey addition or a comprehensive full renovation is not a luxury project; it is a practical response to needing more space in a neighbourhood they do not want to leave.

We have completed projects throughout Leaside, from targeted kitchen and bathroom renovations to full gut rebuilds and complete second storey additions. This guide covers the options honestly: what each scope of work actually costs, how long it takes, what the permit process looks like, and what surprises tend to appear in houses of this era and construction type.

Understanding Leaside's housing stock before you renovate

Leaside bungalows were built to the construction standards of their time, which means they have specific characteristics that any renovation has to account for. These are not defects — they are simply the reality of working in houses that are seventy to eighty years old. Understanding them upfront prevents surprises mid-project.

Structure and framing

Most Leaside bungalows use platform or balloon framing with dimensional lumber that is often larger in actual cross-section than modern nominal equivalents. The framing is generally sound, but it was not designed with a second storey in mind. Before any addition project, a structural engineer must assess whether the existing foundation, load-bearing walls, and connection points can support the added load — and in most cases, some reinforcement is required. This is not optional and is not something to negotiate out of a budget. A proper structural assessment typically costs $1,500 to $3,000 and is money that protects the entire project.

Electrical

Houses built before 1960 were typically wired with 60-amp service and knob-and-tube wiring. Most Leaside homes have had their electrical updated at some point, but the nature and completeness of that work varies considerably. On a full renovation or second storey addition, the electrical will be brought up to current code regardless. On more targeted projects, a licensed electrician needs to inspect what exists. Knob-and-tube wiring that is active and in good condition is not automatically condemned by the Ontario Electrical Safety Code, but most insurers will not cover a home that still has it, and any home insurance claim involving fire in a renovated area with grandfathered wiring can become complicated. Plan to upgrade.

Plumbing

Original plumbing in Leaside bungalows is typically galvanized steel supply lines and cast iron drain lines. Galvanized steel has a service life of roughly fifty to seventy years and often shows reduced flow and rust at this age. When walls are opened for a renovation, replacing the supply lines with copper or PEX is strongly advisable. The cast iron drain stacks, by contrast, are often still in serviceable condition and can be kept if they pass a camera inspection. Budget for the contingency of drain replacement if you are doing any work near the stack.

Insulation

Leaside bungalows were built with minimal insulation by today's standards. Original exterior walls typically have no insulation, or were later packed with blown-in cellulose or fibreglass during the 1970s and 1980s. When the exterior is opened for a second storey addition, it is the right time to improve the thermal envelope of the existing main floor as well. Attic insulation is usually the highest-priority upgrade for homes that have not had it addressed recently; the payback in heating and cooling costs is meaningful, and the City of Toronto has periodically offered programs that subsidize a portion of the cost.

What to expect behind the walls: In our experience, roughly 60–70% of Leaside bungalow renovations encounter at least one condition not visible during the pre-construction walkthrough — most commonly inadequate electrical, evidence of previous water infiltration around windows or roof penetrations, or framing that was modified at some point without a permit. This is not a reason to avoid renovating; it is a reason to budget a contingency of 10–15% of the construction budget and to choose a contractor who documents and prices discoveries transparently rather than absorbing them silently and cutting corners elsewhere.

The second storey addition: Leaside's most common major project

A second storey addition converts a single-storey bungalow into a full two-storey home, roughly doubling the living area on the same footprint. It is the most significant renovation a Leaside homeowner can undertake, and for many households, it transforms what would otherwise be an undersized house into a home they can comfortably raise a family in without relocating.

What a second storey addition actually involves

The process is more disruptive than most homeowners initially anticipate. The roof comes off entirely. The existing ceiling structure is removed or modified to become the floor of the new upper level. New bearing walls and beams are engineered and installed. The entire second floor is framed, insulated, sheathed, roofed, and finished from scratch. The project typically requires the homeowners to vacate the main floor for at least four to six weeks during the structural phase, and many families choose to be out for the full duration. Plan for this in your budget: temporary accommodation, storage, and the cost of eating out for four to six months adds up.

A standard Leaside second storey addition adds between 900 and 1,200 square feet of new finished living space, typically comprising three bedrooms and two bathrooms. The main floor layout is often modified at the same time, particularly the kitchen and the staircase location, which moves from wherever it was tucked to a more central position appropriate for a two-storey home.

Zoning and permits for second storey additions in Leaside

Leaside is zoned primarily under the RD (Residential Detached) zone categories, most commonly RD1 and RD2. For a straight vertical addition that does not increase the building's footprint, the project typically falls within as-of-right permissions and does not require a minor variance from the Committee of Adjustment. However, several conditions apply:

  • Maximum building height is capped at 8.5 metres for most RD zones in this area, measured to the midpoint of the roof. A second storey addition on a bungalow with a modest pitch will typically come in under this limit, but it must be confirmed in the design phase.
  • Lot coverage must remain within the applicable limit (typically 33–35% of the lot area for detached homes in RD zones). If the existing bungalow is already at or near that limit, the addition must be kept strictly vertical.
  • Floor space index (FSI) limits the total gross floor area relative to the lot size. Adding a full second floor on a lot of average Leaside dimensions usually pushes a project close to or into the FSI limit, which needs to be modelled early.
  • A building permit is mandatory. This project involves structural work, new mechanical runs, new electrical circuits, and significant changes to the building envelope. The permit triggers inspections at framing, rough-in, and completion that protect you as the owner.

If the proposed design exceeds any as-of-right permission, the application goes to the Committee of Adjustment for a minor variance. In Leaside, minor variances for second storey additions are routinely approved, but the process typically adds 12 to 16 weeks to the pre-construction timeline and several thousand dollars in application fees and drawings. Factor this into your schedule from day one.

The full gut renovation: transforming the existing house

Not every Leaside homeowner needs or wants a second storey. For some households — empty nesters, couples, or families whose children are nearly grown — the priority is not more square footage but a fundamentally better version of the space that already exists. A full gut renovation of a Leaside bungalow takes everything down to the studs, replaces or upgrades all mechanical systems, and rebuilds the interior to a modern standard.

Done well, a full gut reno of a 1,100-square-foot Leaside bungalow can produce a home that functions as well as anything built today, with the benefit of the existing structure, lot, and neighbourhood character. Done poorly — cosmetically renovated but with original mechanical and electrical left in place — it is a house that looks updated but is not.

What a full gut renovation includes

  • Complete demolition of all interior finishes down to the studs, subfloor, and ceiling joists. This exposes everything and eliminates guesswork about condition.
  • Full electrical upgrade to 200-amp service, new panel, and all new wiring throughout. ESA inspection at rough-in and completion.
  • Full plumbing replacement of supply lines (PEX or copper) and inspection or replacement of drain lines. Rough-in for bathrooms, kitchen, and laundry relocated as needed for the new layout.
  • HVAC replacement including a new forced-air furnace and A/C unit or a heat pump system, new ductwork, and HRV (heat recovery ventilator) as now required by the Ontario Building Code for significant renovations.
  • Insulation upgrade throughout: spray foam on rim joists and foundation walls, batt or blown-in in exterior wall cavities, and increased R-value in the attic.
  • New drywall, millwork, and finishes throughout, including new windows and exterior doors if not recently replaced.
  • Kitchen and bathroom(s) fully designed and installed as part of the project.

On finishes and cost: The cost difference between a standard and a high-end finish package on a Leaside bungalow renovation can easily be $100,000 to $200,000. The largest line items are kitchen cabinetry and appliances, primary bathroom fixtures and tile, and flooring. Establishing a realistic finishes budget before the contractor begins estimating — not after — produces quotes that reflect what you actually want to build, rather than a number that looks appealing on paper and expands through change orders.

Project timelines: what to plan for

One of the most consistent sources of frustration in major Leaside renovations is underestimating how long the process takes from the decision to start to the day you move back in. The construction phase is only one part of the timeline.

Phase Second Storey Addition Full Gut Renovation
Design and drawings 3 – 5 months 2 – 3 months
Permit approval (City of Toronto) 3 – 6 months 2 – 4 months
Committee of Adjustment (if required) Add 3 – 4 months Rarely required
Construction 6 – 9 months 4 – 6 months
Total elapsed time (typical) 12 – 20 months 8 – 13 months

These timelines assume the permit application is complete and accurate when submitted, that the design is finalized before construction begins, and that no major discoveries in the field require significant redesign. Projects where the design evolves during construction, or where permit comments require multiple rounds of revision, take longer. Start the process earlier than feels necessary.

Choosing the right approach for your specific house

The right renovation scope for a Leaside bungalow depends on several factors that are specific to each property. Before committing to a scope, it is worth working through the following:

  • Foundation condition. A bungalow with a solid poured-concrete foundation and adequate headroom in the basement is a better candidate for a full renovation and second storey than one with a rubble stone foundation that needs underpinning. Have the foundation assessed early — it is the one condition that can change the cost calculus significantly.
  • Lot coverage and FSI headroom. Confirm with your designer or contractor how much room you have under the applicable zoning rules before assuming a particular scope is achievable as-of-right. A few minutes of math in the pre-design phase can prevent weeks of planning for a project that requires a variance you were not counting on.
  • Your timeline and disruption tolerance. A second storey addition is a longer, more disruptive project than a full main-floor renovation. If you have young children or a household that cannot easily relocate for six to nine months of construction, the more targeted scope may be the right answer even if the addition is the better long-term investment.
  • Resale horizon. If you are renovating primarily for your own use and plan to be in the house for fifteen or more years, invest in the systems and finishes you actually want. If you are planning to sell within three to five years, focus on the projects with the clearest resale return: kitchen, primary bathroom, and finished basement typically rank highest in this neighbourhood.
  • Phasing. A second storey addition and a main floor renovation do not have to happen simultaneously, but combining them is almost always more cost-efficient than doing them in separate projects. Opening the house once, relocating once, and managing one permit and one contractor team produces better results and lower overall cost than two sequential projects.

Working with the City of Toronto permit process

The City of Toronto's building permit process has improved measurably in recent years with the introduction of online submissions through the Toronto Building portal, but it remains a process that rewards patience and thorough preparation. Several points are worth knowing before you start:

  • Permit applications for second storey additions must include complete drawings from a registered architect or designer, a structural engineer's drawings and schedule, a site plan showing existing and proposed conditions, and energy efficiency compliance documentation under the Ontario Building Code's SB-12 supplement. Incomplete submissions are returned without review, resetting the clock.
  • Zoning review and building code review run on separate tracks within the City. A project that passes zoning may still require revisions to satisfy the building inspector's comments, and vice versa. Expect at least one round of comments and revisions on any project of this scale.
  • Inspections are mandatory at defined stages: demolition, foundation (if applicable), framing and structural, rough-in mechanical and electrical, insulation, and final. The contractor is responsible for calling inspections and ensuring the work is ready before the inspector arrives. Failed inspections add time and cost, which is why choosing a contractor with a consistent track record of passing inspections matters.
  • The permit must remain open until the project passes final inspection. A project that was built but never closed out with a final inspection creates a cloud on the property's title and can complicate a future sale or refinancing. Make sure your contractor closes the permit properly.

What Leaside bungalow projects actually produce

We have completed enough projects in this neighbourhood to speak to the outcomes with reasonable confidence. A well-executed second storey addition on a Leaside bungalow typically produces a home worth $1.8 to $2.4 million at current market values, depending on the specific street, lot size, and finish level. The cost of the renovation combined with the original purchase price (for homeowners who have owned for a decade or more) still leaves meaningful equity relative to the finished value — which is why this project type continues to make financial sense in this neighbourhood even at current construction costs.

A full gut renovation of the main floor, without an addition, typically does not add as much absolute value as a second storey, but it meaningfully reduces deferred maintenance risk, lowers operating costs (heating, cooling, water), and produces a materially better quality of life for the years you remain in the house. For homeowners who are not planning to sell, the value calculation is different than a simple before-and-after comparison.

The condition that produces the worst financial outcome — and the one we try hardest to help clients avoid — is a partial renovation that addresses cosmetics without addressing the underlying mechanical and structural issues that will surface later. A kitchen renovation in a house with 60-amp service and original galvanized plumbing is a renovation that will be torn apart again in ten years. If the house needs to be opened up, open it up properly and address the things behind the walls while they are accessible.

Conclusion: planning a Leaside renovation that delivers

Leaside bungalows are well-located, well-built for their era, and genuinely good candidates for transformation. The combination of generous lots, detached construction, and strong neighbourhood fundamentals makes the investment case compelling. But the projects succeed when they are planned honestly: with realistic budgets that include contingency, timelines that account for the full pre-construction phase, and a contractor who has done this work in this neighbourhood and can anticipate what the walls are going to reveal.
