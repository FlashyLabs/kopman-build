Design-build vs architect + GC in Toronto

Two fundamentally different ways to deliver a renovation or custom home — with different cost structures, timelines, risks, and points of accountability. Here is how to choose.

The question behind the question

When Toronto homeowners ask whether they should use a design-build firm or hire an architect and general contractor separately, they are usually asking a simpler underlying question: who is responsible when something goes wrong? The delivery model you choose determines the answer to that question, and everything else — cost, timeline, creative control, communication, risk — flows from it.

Neither approach is inherently superior. Each suits a different kind of project and a different kind of owner. What is genuinely harmful is choosing one without understanding what you are actually signing up for. After 25 years managing complex projects in Toronto, we have seen both models work beautifully and both models fail badly. The difference is almost never the model itself — it is the fit between the model and the project.

This guide explains how each approach actually works in practice, what each one costs in the Toronto market, and the specific scenarios where one clearly outperforms the other.

How the traditional architect + GC model works

The traditional delivery model separates design from construction. You hire an architect or design firm to develop your plans, then you take those plans to market and hire a general contractor to build them. The two are independent. The architect works for you; the contractor works for you. In theory, the architect acts as your advocate during construction, reviewing the contractor's work against the drawings and certifying payment when milestones are met.

In practice, the relationship is more complicated than that description suggests. The architect's engagement during construction is typically specified in their contract, and the scope varies considerably. A full-service engagement includes regular site visits, shop drawing review, response to contractor requests for information, and payment certification. A reduced-service engagement — which is more common than most homeowners realize — may stop at permit drawings, with the homeowner left to manage the contractor relationship independently.

The design phases and what they cost

Architectural fees in Toronto for residential renovation and custom home work typically run as a percentage of construction cost, or as a flat fee negotiated upfront. For a mid-size renovation or addition, expect:

  • Schematic design (concept through massing): roughly 25% of total architectural fee
  • Design development (detailed drawings, specifications): roughly 35% of total fee
  • Construction documents (permit-ready drawings): roughly 25% of total fee
  • Construction administration (site visits, RFI responses, payment review): roughly 15% of total fee

The design process itself takes time. From first meeting to permit application, a straightforward home addition in Toronto typically takes four to six months. A more complex custom home or significant renovation with heritage considerations can take nine to twelve months before a shovel touches the ground. This timeline exists regardless of how good the architect is — it is built into the process of proper design.

The contractor selection gap

One of the structural challenges of the traditional model is what happens between design completion and construction start. You have a completed set of drawings. Now you need to find a contractor. If you are fortunate, your architect has strong working relationships with contractors they trust and can facilitate introductions. If not — or if you want to run an open tender process to get competitive pricing — you are managing that search yourself.

Running a proper tender in Toronto takes four to eight weeks. Contractors need time to review the drawings, walk the site, prepare a detailed estimate, and ask clarifying questions. If you rush this process and award the job to a contractor who has not properly reviewed the scope, you will pay for it in change orders later.

Important: In the traditional model, the architect designs to a budget target but the contractor prices to the actual drawings. If the drawings specify finishes and systems that exceed your budget, you find out during tender — after months of design work. Misalignment between design intent and construction budget is one of the most common and most expensive problems in the traditional delivery model.

How design-build works

In a design-build delivery model, a single firm is responsible for both the design and the construction. You have one contract, one point of contact, and one entity accountable for the outcome. The design-build firm either employs designers and architects in-house, or operates with a closely integrated design partner with whom they have an established working relationship and shared accountability.

The practical difference is significant. Because the same organization is responsible for building what they design, cost and constructability are part of the design conversation from day one. A design-build firm does not produce drawings and then find out they are over budget. They design to a budget — in real time, with real knowledge of what things cost in the current Toronto market. When a client wants a specific finish or structural feature, the design-build team can immediately assess what it actually adds to the cost and whether the value is there.

The integrated process

A well-run design-build process typically looks like this:

  • Discovery and programming: Detailed conversations about how you live in the space, what you need, what you want, and what your budget actually is. This is where scope and priorities get established before a single line is drawn.
  • Concept design with cost modelling: Initial design directions developed alongside rough cost targets. If a design direction is running 20% over budget, you know immediately and can make informed choices about where to add or subtract.
  • Design development and specification: Detailed design with specific materials, systems, and finishes confirmed. By this stage, the project cost should be known to within 10–15% before detailed estimating.
  • Detailed estimating and contract: Full quantity takeoff and subcontractor pricing. The fixed price (or GMP, depending on the contract structure) is established here.
  • Construction: The same team that designed the project builds it. There is no translation gap between design intent and construction execution.

Side-by-side comparison

The table below summarizes how the two models compare across the dimensions that matter most to most Toronto homeowners.

Factor Architect + GC Design-Build
Single point of accountability No — design and construction are separate contracts Yes — one firm, one contract
Design independence High — architect has no financial interest in the build Lower — designer and builder are same entity
Budget certainty early in process Low — cost confirmed only at tender High — cost integrated throughout design
Total timeline (concept to occupancy) Longer — sequential process adds 3–6 months Shorter — overlapping phases compress schedule
Design fees as % of project cost 8–15% (architectural fees alone) Typically included or 5–10% of project cost
Competitive pricing Yes — can tender to multiple contractors No — price is proprietary to design-build firm
Change order risk Higher — drawings may miss field conditions Lower — builder knows the design intimately
Best for complex or bespoke design Yes — architect can push boundaries freely Depends on firm's design capability
Owner management burden Higher — two separate relationships to manage Lower — one relationship, one process

What things actually cost in Toronto

Numbers matter. Here is a realistic picture of what each delivery model costs on a mid-size Toronto renovation project — say, a whole-home renovation or a rear addition in the $700,000–$1,200,000 total project cost range.

Cost component Architect + GC model Design-Build model
Design / architecture fees $60,000–$140,000 (8–12% of construction) Included in project fee or $40,000–$90,000
Structural engineering $8,000–$20,000 (separate engagement) Often coordinated within design-build fee
Interior design (if separate) $15,000–$50,000+ Typically integrated
Construction cost Market rate, competitively tendered Market rate, sometimes with markup on subs
Owner's time (estimating, management) High — budget 40–80 hours of owner time Low — budget 15–30 hours of owner time
Contingency typically needed 15–20% recommended 10–15% recommended

These ranges reflect real Toronto market conditions in 2026. The total out-of-pocket cost between the two models on a $1,000,000 project is often closer than homeowners expect — the apparent cost savings of competitive tendering in the traditional model are frequently offset by higher design fees, longer timelines (which have real carrying costs on mortgages and temporary housing), and more change orders during construction.

Where the traditional model is clearly better

The architect + GC model has genuine advantages in specific situations. It is not simply the "old way" that design-build has superseded. There are good reasons to choose it:

  • You want design independence and creative ambition. An architect who is not also responsible for building has greater freedom to push the design in interesting directions. The best architectural work in Toronto — the kind that wins awards and ends up in architectural publications — typically comes from this model. If you have a complex site, significant heritage considerations, or a strong design vision that requires someone whose only job is to realize it, an independent architect is the right choice.
  • Your project is large enough to justify competitive tendering. On a $2M+ custom home, the ability to tender to three or four qualified contractors and capture competitive pricing can more than offset the coordination overhead of the traditional model. The larger the project, the more the math can favor competition.
  • You already have an architect relationship you trust. If you have worked with an architect before, if you have a strong recommendation for a specific designer, or if the architect has deep experience with a project type (say, heritage renovation in Rosedale or the Annex), the relationship advantage may outweigh the coordination cost.
  • You want independent oversight of the contractor. Having an architect who reviews the contractor's work and certifies payment provides a layer of independent quality control. This is particularly valuable on technically complex projects where construction quality is difficult for a non-expert owner to evaluate.

Where design-build is clearly better

Design-build earns its place in the Toronto market for a different set of reasons:

  • Budget certainty is your primary concern. If you cannot afford to discover at tender that the design is $200,000 over budget after six months of architectural work, design-build is the safer model. Cost is embedded in every design decision, not revealed at the end.
  • Speed matters. Design-build firms can typically compress overall project timelines by three to six months compared to the traditional model, because design and pre-construction activities overlap rather than proceeding sequentially. If you need to move home before a school year starts, or if you are selling and renovating on a tight window, this compression has real value.
  • You want a single throat to choke. This is blunt, but it is real. When a problem arises — a leak, a structural issue, a finish that does not match the specification — the design-build firm cannot point at the architect, and the architect cannot point at the contractor. One entity is accountable. For owners who do not want to become project managers, this matters enormously.
  • The project is mid-complexity: a renovation, addition, or well-defined custom home. Design-build is most efficient when the project type is recognizable enough that the firm has genuine prior-cost knowledge. A rear addition in Leslieville, a basement conversion in North York, a whole-home renovation in Etobicoke — these are projects that a strong design-build firm can price accurately and build efficiently because they have done it dozens of times.

The question to ask a design-build firm: "Can you show me three completed projects similar to mine, with the original budget estimate and the final cost?" A firm that cannot answer this question — or that cannot demonstrate budget discipline across their portfolio — does not actually offer the budget certainty that design-build promises. The model's main advantage disappears if cost management is poor.

The hybrid approach: what it looks like in practice

Many Toronto renovations end up somewhere between the two pure models. It is entirely reasonable to hire an architect to develop a design concept, produce permit drawings, and hand off to a general contractor who manages construction without full architectural administration. This can capture some of the design quality benefits of an independent architect while avoiding the full overhead of architect-administered construction.

The risk in this approach is the gap it creates. When an architect's engagement ends at permit issuance, the drawings they produce may not have the construction-level detail that a contractor needs to build without ambiguity. Drawings that are adequate for permit approval are often not adequate for detailed estimating and construction. Gaps get filled by the contractor, sometimes in ways the architect and owner would not have chosen. Change orders and disputes about what was "in scope" are more common when the design package is incomplete.

If you pursue this hybrid approach, invest in a more complete set of construction documents from the architect — specifications, reflected ceiling plans, millwork drawings, and detailed notes on finishes and systems. The additional architectural fee for this completeness will typically be recovered many times over in fewer change orders and less construction ambiguity.

Questions to ask before you decide

Before choosing a delivery model, get clear on your own situation. The following questions will help:

  • Is your design vision clearly defined, or do you need help finding it? If you know exactly what you want and need someone to execute it, the model matters less. If you need a design partner to help you understand what the project could become, the quality of that partner matters enormously — which may push you toward an architect with a strong specific design voice.
  • How much time can you personally invest in managing the project? The traditional model requires meaningful owner engagement. You are managing two separate professional relationships, coordinating between them when issues arise, and making decisions when the architect and contractor disagree. If this sounds like something you can do and want to do, the traditional model gives you more control. If it sounds like a second job you do not need, design-build is more appropriate.
  • How firm is your budget? If your $800,000 budget is truly $800,000 and not $850,000 if things get interesting, design-build is the safer model. If you have flexibility and a strong appetite for the design outcome, the traditional model with a rigorous architect can deliver something exceptional.
  • How complex is the project technically? Structural complexity, heritage designation, significant grade changes, high-performance building systems — these factors favor having an architect whose job is entirely focused on solving those problems well, rather than a design-build team whose attention is split across design and production.

How Kopman Build approaches this

We operate as a design-build firm for most residential projects, which means we take accountability for both the design process and the construction outcome. We have seen what happens when design and construction are managed separately without strong integration between them: misaligned expectations, budget surprises at tender, and finger-pointing when problems arise during construction. Our model eliminates those failure points.

Conclusion

The design-build vs architect + GC decision is not about which model is better. It is about which model is better for your project, your budget, and the amount of management you want to take on yourself.

The traditional model gives you design independence, the ability to tender competitively, and independent oversight of construction. It costs more time and requires more owner involvement. It is the right choice for ambitious design projects, large budgets, and owners who want meaningful control over the process.

Design-build gives you budget integration, a compressed timeline, and single-point accountability. It asks you to trust one firm with both the design and the build. It is the right choice for most mid-size Toronto renovations, for owners who want a managed process rather than a project management role, and for anyone for whom budget certainty is non-negotiable.

Either way, the quality of the firm you choose matters more than the model. A mediocre design-build firm and a mediocre architect-plus-GC pairing will both produce mediocre outcomes. The model only amplifies what the people involved bring to it. Choose carefully. We are always happy to talk through your project.
