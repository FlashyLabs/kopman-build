How much does a home addition cost in Toronto?

A realistic cost breakdown for Toronto home additions in 2026 — by type, what drives costs, and how to evaluate the investment.

Why adding on often beats moving in Toronto

Toronto's housing market has reset how homeowners calculate the value of space. The average detached or semi-detached home in established neighbourhoods now trades between $1.2 million and $1.5 million. When you factor in the full cost of a move — the selling agent's commission, the buyer's agent commission, Toronto's municipal land transfer tax, the provincial land transfer tax, legal fees, and the physical cost of moving — you're typically looking at 5–7% of the purchase price evaporating before you've unpacked a box. On a $1.4 million home, that's $70,000 to $98,000 in transaction friction alone, and that's before you account for the price premium of the larger home you're buying into.

A well-designed home addition bypasses most of that friction. You stay in your neighbourhood, your kids stay in their school, and you spend construction dollars that compound into property value rather than transaction costs that vanish entirely. For the right property and the right family situation, an addition is not a consolation prize for not being able to afford to move — it's frequently the smarter financial decision.

That said, additions aren't cheap, and the range of outcomes is wide. Here is what you can realistically expect to spend in Toronto in 2026.

2026 cost ranges by addition type

These figures represent all-in construction costs including design, permits, structural work, mechanical rough-ins, and a standard interior finish level. Luxury finishes, complex structural conditions, and heritage constraints will push costs toward the upper end and beyond.

What drives addition costs up

The spread between the low and high figures above is not arbitrary — specific conditions push projects toward the top of each range, and some push them beyond it.

Structural engineering and foundation work

Every addition that bears on or integrates with an existing structure requires a structural engineer. When the existing foundation is insufficient to support new load — common in older Toronto bungalows and many pre-war homes — underpinning or bench-pinning adds $40,000 to $120,000 before any living space is created. This is not optional; it's a code requirement and an engineering reality.

Matching the existing house

The invisible cost that catches many homeowners off guard is exterior integration. Matching original brick, recreating period trim profiles, sourcing appropriate window proportions, and tying in a roofline cleanly all take skill and money. A rear addition that reads as a thoughtless box appended to a fine Edwardian home destroys value rather than creating it. Do it properly, and the seam becomes invisible — which is the goal.

Heritage constraints

Properties designated under Part IV of the Ontario Heritage Act, or within a Heritage Conservation District, face additional approval layers. A Heritage Permit must be issued before a building permit can follow. Materials, proportions, and window placement may all be subject to Heritage Planning review. See our guide to heritage home renovations in Toronto for the full picture.

High-finish interior specifications

Construction costs are relatively similar across most contractors at a given quality tier. What varies enormously is interior specification: flooring, cabinetry, countertop materials, plumbing fixtures, tile, and trim. A second-storey addition with builder-grade finishes costs far less than the same footprint finished with white oak hardwood, custom millwork, and designer fixtures. Neither is wrong — they serve different households and different resale positions.

Separate HVAC and utility systems for suites

Laneway and garden suites require their own heating and cooling systems, separate electrical service (typically a new 100A panel), and in most cases a separate water and sewer connection to the municipal main. These utility connection costs — often $20,000 to $50,000 before the contractor touches the building — are a significant component of suite project costs and are sometimes underrepresented in initial estimates.

Ravine and Conservation Authority setbacks

Properties within the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority's regulated area — which includes much of the ravine system running through Don Valley, Humber Valley, and their tributaries — require a TRCA permit in addition to the City building permit. Construction within these setbacks may be restricted outright, or may require engineered stormwater management and specialized construction methods.

The permit process for Toronto additions

A building permit is required for any home addition in Toronto — no exceptions. The permit application requires architectural drawings stamped by a designer licensed under the Ontario Building Code Act (typically an architect or BCIN-qualified designer), and structural drawings stamped by a licensed professional engineer.

If the proposed addition exceeds the as-of-right zoning limits — most commonly because it pushes lot coverage above the permitted maximum, exceeds height limits, or encroaches on required setbacks — a minor variance from the Committee of Adjustment is required before the building permit can be issued. Committee of Adjustment hearings are scheduled roughly monthly, and approval decisions can be appealed to the Ontario Land Tribunal. In straightforward cases, the CoA process adds 3–5 months. Appealed cases can add significantly more.

A realistic permit timeline for a Toronto addition without a variance is 4–8 months from submission to issuance. Construction typically takes another 4–8 months depending on the scope. Plan for a 12–18 month timeline from first design meeting to occupancy — and sequence the design work to compress it where possible.

Laneway suites: what you need to know

Toronto passed its laneway suite bylaw in 2018, making it the first major Canadian city to legalize this housing form as-of-right across most of its residential zones. To be eligible, a property must abut a public lane, the lane must be at least 3.5 metres wide (6 metres in some circumstances), the lot must be at least 15 metres deep, and the main dwelling must have a rear main wall facing the lane.

Laneway suites are capped at one storey plus a rooftop terrace, or two storeys in some cases, with a maximum height of 6 metres. The suite cannot exceed 40% of the rear yard, and the rear yard must retain a minimum soft landscaped area. All-in costs typically run $280,000–$450,000, with the wide range driven primarily by utility connection costs, foundation soil conditions, and finish level. Rental income potential in established Toronto neighbourhoods makes the pro forma compelling for many properties.

Garden suites: the newer option

Following the 2022 amendment to Toronto's zoning bylaws, garden suites (also called accessory dwelling units) became permitted as-of-right on most residential lots in the city — including those without lane access. This significantly expanded the number of properties eligible for a secondary income-generating unit. Garden suites follow a similar design and construction process to laneway suites, with similar cost profiles ($260,000–$420,000 all-in), but are sited within the rear yard rather than at the lane. Setbacks and height limits apply, and a building permit is required, but the approval path is generally more straightforward than a laneway suite on properties without lane access complications.

Addition versus moving: the math

Consider a family in a 1,400 sq ft semi-detached home in East York or Leaside, currently worth $1.3 million. They need 600 more square feet. To buy a comparable home in the same neighbourhood with that additional space, they might pay $1.7 million — a $400,000 gross increase, plus $85,000–$120,000 in transaction costs on both sides of the move. Total capital deployed: roughly $500,000–$520,000 above their current position, and they leave the neighbourhood.

A well-designed two-storey rear addition adding 700 sq ft to the same home, with quality finishes and structural integration, might cost $380,000–$440,000 all-in. The family stays. The addition adds assessed value and market value that partially offsets the cost. The net capital at risk is meaningfully lower, with no moving disruption.

The math does not always work this way — it depends heavily on the specific property, the scope of the addition, and the family's circumstances. But it illustrates why additions deserve serious consideration as a first option rather than a fallback.

Working with the right contractor matters enormously

A home addition integrates new structure with old in ways that expose every weakness in workmanship and planning. The transition between old and new framing, the waterproofing of new roof-to-wall connections, the integration of mechanical systems — all of these require experience that goes beyond general renovation work.
