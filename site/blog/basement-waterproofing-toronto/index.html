Basement waterproofing in Toronto

Methods, costs, and what actually lasts — a straight account from contractors who have waterproofed basements in this city for 25 years.

Why Toronto basements leak

Toronto sits on a glacial till with a high clay content. Clay is dense, poorly draining, and expands when it absorbs water. It surrounds the foundations of virtually every house built before the 1980s — semi-detached Edwardians in Leslieville, brick bungalows in Etobicoke, red-brick detacheds in the Annex — and it creates a predictable problem: when it rains heavily or snow melts quickly, clay holds water against your foundation walls rather than letting it drain away. Eventually, that pressure finds a path in.

Older Toronto foundations compound the issue. Poured concrete walls crack over decades. Block foundations — common in homes built in the 1940s and 1950s — have mortar joints that deteriorate over time and become permeable. Original weeping tile systems installed before the 1970s were usually clay pipe, which collapses, roots into, and fails. The result is that a significant proportion of Toronto's existing housing stock has some degree of foundation moisture problem, and many homeowners only discover it when they decide to finish the basement or when a heavy summer storm reveals what was already there.

Understanding the source of water intrusion is the first step to solving it correctly. Water can enter a basement through hydrostatic pressure from a high water table, through surface water that migrates down the foundation wall, through cracks in the foundation itself, or through a failed or absent weeping tile system. Each source has a different best solution, and conflating them leads to spending money on the wrong approach.

The two main approaches: exterior vs interior

Every waterproofing conversation comes down to this choice. Exterior waterproofing stops water from reaching the foundation. Interior waterproofing manages water that has already entered. Both have legitimate applications, and the right answer depends on the source of the problem, your budget, and what disruption you are willing to accept.

Exterior waterproofing

Exterior waterproofing is the more invasive and more expensive option, but it is also the more complete solution. It involves excavating the soil around the perimeter of the foundation down to the footing — typically 7 to 9 feet in Toronto — cleaning and repairing the foundation wall, applying a waterproofing membrane to the exterior face, installing new drainage board, replacing the weeping tile with perforated pipe in a properly graded gravel bed, and then backfilling with drainage-friendly material rather than the original clay.

When it is done properly, exterior waterproofing addresses the root cause directly: water is redirected before it can contact the foundation. The membrane and drainage system protect the wall from the outside, which is where the water actually is. Warranties of 25 years are common from reputable contractors, and the system genuinely works for that long when installed correctly.

The trade-off is cost and disruption. Excavating around a house in a dense Toronto neighbourhood requires removing landscaping, fencing, decks, walkways, or driveways that are in the way. Access can be limited in attached or semi-detached homes. Restoration of the exterior after the work is a significant cost item in its own right, and it needs to be budgeted explicitly rather than treated as a loose assumption.

Interior waterproofing

Interior waterproofing works by capturing water that enters the basement and routing it to a sump pit for removal, rather than blocking it at the wall. The typical system involves breaking out the concrete floor at the perimeter, installing a drainage channel and perforated pipe below the slab, routing that to a sump pit, and then reinstating the concrete floor over the drainage. A sump pump removes the collected water continuously during wet conditions.

The significant advantage is disruption: the work happens entirely inside the home, no landscaping is disturbed, and for attached or semi-detached houses where exterior excavation on one or both sides is impossible, it is often the only viable option. It is also meaningfully less expensive than exterior work.

The limitation is conceptual: interior waterproofing does not stop water from reaching the wall or entering the foundation. It manages the water after entry. This means the wall itself may continue to deteriorate over time if it is being actively wet. For homes with significant structural cracking or block foundations in poor condition, interior drainage alone is not the full answer. A thorough assessment needs to determine whether the structural condition of the wall is acceptable before committing to an interior-only approach.

The honest answer on which approach to choose: In many Toronto renovation scenarios, the right answer is a combination. Exterior waterproofing on the accessible sides of the house, interior drainage on the attached or constrained sides. We always start with a thorough inspection to understand where water is actually entering and through what mechanism before recommending either approach.

Weeping tile: the most misunderstood part

The term "weeping tile" causes more confusion than almost anything else in basement waterproofing. It does not refer to tile in the flooring sense. It is the drainage pipe system that runs along the base of the foundation footing, collecting groundwater and directing it away from the building.

In homes built before approximately 1970, weeping tile was clay pipe — cylindrical sections with small gaps between them that allowed water to enter while routing it away from the foundation. Over 50 years, clay pipe crushes under soil pressure, collapses, becomes infiltrated by tree roots, and fails. In many older Toronto homes, the original weeping tile is either partially or completely non-functional, and this is the primary cause of chronic wet basement problems that homeowners have been fighting for decades with sump pumps and damp-proofing that never quite solves the issue.

Modern weeping tile is perforated polyethylene or PVC pipe, installed in a gravel bed and wrapped in filter fabric to prevent soil migration. It is far more durable, root-resistant, and hydraulically efficient than the original clay systems it replaces.

When we do exterior waterproofing, replacing failed weeping tile is standard. On interior drainage systems, the new interior perimeter channel effectively functions as weeping tile inside the slab. Understanding which system is in place — and whether it is functioning — is a critical diagnostic step before any waterproofing scope is finalized.

Sump pumps: sizing, placement, and backup

A sump pump is not optional in most Toronto basement waterproofing systems. Whether you are doing exterior or interior drainage, you need a pump to remove collected water. What many homeowners do not fully appreciate is that the pump itself introduces a failure point: it runs on electricity, it has moving parts that wear out, and if it fails during a major storm, your basement can flood regardless of how good the drainage system around it is.

Primary pump sizing

Pump capacity is rated in gallons per minute (GPM) or litres per minute and needs to be matched to the drainage volume the system is expected to handle. For most residential Toronto applications with a perimeter drainage system, a 1/3 horsepower pump handling 30–40 GPM is a baseline. Homes on lots with heavy clay, high water tables, or large drainage areas need more capacity. Undersizing a pump is a common mistake that reveals itself during the first significant storm.

Battery backup and redundancy

The worst storms — the ones most likely to send water into your basement — are also the ones most likely to knock out your power. A battery backup sump pump is not a luxury; it is a basic component of a reliable system. Battery backups typically activate automatically when the primary pump fails or when power is lost, and modern systems will send you an alert to your phone. For added security, a water-powered backup pump (which uses municipal water pressure rather than electricity) can be added as a tertiary layer in high-risk situations.

We also strongly recommend a sump alarm: a simple float-activated alarm in the pit that warns you if the water level rises abnormally, indicating the primary pump has failed. They cost very little and have prevented catastrophic flooding for many of our clients.

Toronto waterproofing costs in 2026

Cost ranges in waterproofing vary significantly based on the extent of the work, the access constraints, the condition of the existing foundation, and the soil conditions. The figures below reflect typical Toronto-area projects in 2026, including labour, materials, and basic site restoration but excluding major landscape or hardscape reconstruction.

Scope Typical Range (CAD) Notes
Interior drainage system, full perimeter (1,000 sq ft basement) $12,000 – $20,000 Includes perimeter channel, sump pit, primary pump, concrete reinstatement
Battery backup sump pump (add-on) $800 – $1,800 Price varies by brand and battery capacity
Exterior waterproofing, one wall (approx. 30 linear feet) $15,000 – $25,000 Includes excavation, membrane, new weeping tile, drainage board, backfill
Exterior waterproofing, full perimeter (detached home) $45,000 – $80,000+ Site access, soil conditions, and restoration costs drive the range significantly
Crack injection (polyurethane or epoxy, per crack) $400 – $900 Appropriate for isolated cracks; not a substitute for drainage system
Window well installation (per well) $1,200 – $2,500 Includes drainage connection to weeping tile where accessible
Exterior grading and swale correction $2,000 – $6,000 Often the highest-ROI first step if surface drainage is the primary issue

These numbers assume straightforward access. Attached houses in dense Toronto neighbourhoods where the problem wall faces a shared lot line add cost and complexity, because exterior work on that wall may not be feasible at all. In those cases, interior drainage is the primary tool and the system needs to be designed to handle what the exterior cannot.

On warranty claims: Basement waterproofing warranties vary enormously in what they actually cover. A 25-year warranty that excludes acts of God, damage from settlement, or sump pump failure is very different from a transferable warranty that covers water entry through the treated area under normal conditions. Read the exclusions carefully and ask the contractor to explain what scenarios are and are not covered before signing.

What a proper inspection should cover

A reputable waterproofing contractor should spend meaningful time diagnosing before recommending a solution. The inspection should include the following:

  • Interior assessment: Looking at the full basement perimeter for water staining, efflorescence (the white mineral deposit left by evaporating groundwater), active seepage, crack patterns, and evidence of where water is entering and at what elevation — floor level vs. wall vs. wall-floor joint.
  • Exterior grade check: Evaluating whether the ground slopes away from the foundation as it should, or whether soil settlement has created a negative grade that directs surface water toward the house. This is a common and often overlooked contributor to basement moisture.
  • Downspout and eaves assessment: Checking whether downspouts discharge to daylight away from the foundation or are connected to the original clay weeping tile system (which was sometimes used as a storm drain). Disconnection of downspouts from a failed weeping tile system is frequently the single most impactful thing we do.
  • Foundation type and condition: Identifying whether the foundation is poured concrete, concrete block, or stone rubble (found in some older Toronto homes pre-1920), as the treatment approach differs significantly.
  • Evidence of prior attempts: Looking for previous waterproofing work, crack repairs, or water control measures that may need to be accounted for or may have masked the underlying condition.

Common mistakes homeowners make

After two and a half decades of being called in after someone else's waterproofing failed, we have a clear view of what goes wrong. The patterns repeat with depressing regularity.

  • Treating the symptom with paint: Hydraulic cement and waterproofing paint are sold at every hardware store in Toronto. They stop visible seepage temporarily. They do not address the drainage or pressure situation causing it, and they eventually fail — sometimes more dramatically than the original problem because pressure has been building behind the treatment. We see this regularly in older homes where previous owners applied layer after layer of DryLock and hydraulic cement over twenty years, and the wall is now in much worse condition than it needed to be.
  • Choosing interior drainage only when the wall is structurally compromised: If a block foundation wall is bowing inward, spalling, or losing mortar at scale, interior drainage manages the water but does not stop the wall from continuing to deteriorate. In these cases, structural repair — carbon fibre straps, wall anchors, or in serious cases partial reconstruction — needs to be done as well. A contractor who quotes interior drainage on a wall in poor structural condition without addressing the structure is not giving you a complete solution.
  • Not addressing the sump pump discharge: The pump needs to discharge water away from the foundation and far enough that it cannot cycle back. We have seen sump pump discharges that terminated two feet from the house, effectively recycling the water back into the drainage system. The discharge needs to daylight at least 5–6 feet from the foundation and ideally be directed to a drainage swale or storm connection.
  • Ignoring the window wells: Basement window wells fill with debris, fail to drain, and become reservoirs that push water directly through the window frame into the basement. Window wells need proper drainage to the weeping tile system or to daylight, and they need to be covered or maintained regularly.
  • Finishing the basement before waterproofing is resolved: This is the most expensive mistake. Framing, insulation, drywall, and flooring installed in a basement that still has an unresolved moisture problem will need to be torn out when the issue eventually surfaces. We cannot finish a basement over an active moisture problem without disclosing it, and reputable contractors won't.

Permits and City of Toronto requirements

Interior drainage systems and sump pump installations in Toronto generally do not require a building permit when they are standalone waterproofing measures and no other structural or drainage-to-sewer work is involved. Exterior excavation projects typically do not require a permit either, though they may require a right-of-way permit if machinery needs to be staged on the road allowance or if work affects the municipal boulevard.

Where permits are required is when the work involves new connections to the municipal storm or sanitary sewer system, or when the project is part of a larger basement renovation involving structural changes, electrical upgrades, or new plumbing. In those cases, a building permit is required through the City of Toronto Building Division, and the scope needs to be submitted with drawings prepared or reviewed by a qualified professional.

Some Toronto homeowners are eligible for a basement flooding protection subsidy through the City of Toronto's Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy Program. As of 2026, the program provides up to $3,400 in subsidies for qualifying measures including backwater valve installation, sump pump installation, and disconnection of downspouts from the foundation drain. The program has income eligibility criteria and requires work to be completed by a licensed contractor. It is worth checking eligibility before starting any work.

How waterproofing fits into a broader basement renovation

If you are planning to finish your basement — add living space, a home office, a bedroom, or a rec room — waterproofing is not a separate project. It is the first phase of the renovation, and everything that follows depends on it being done correctly.

The sequence matters. Waterproofing is addressed before framing begins. Any structural concerns with the foundation are resolved before insulation goes in. Vapour control is designed as part of the wall assembly, not as an afterthought. The sump pit location is coordinated with the layout so it ends up in a mechanical room or utility area rather than in the middle of a finished space.

At Kopman Build, when we take on a full basement renovation, waterproofing and structural assessment are part of the initial scope review. We do not start framing until we have confirmed that the envelope is sound. It adds time upfront but it is the only way to stand behind the finished product.

Homeowners sometimes ask whether they should waterproof first and renovate later, or do it all at once. The answer is almost always all at once, if the budget allows. Coordinating a waterproofing contractor to come back and tear out finished walls to address a problem that should have been caught in the original scope is expensive and avoidable. If you are planning the renovation anyway, do the diagnostic work at the beginning.

Questions to ask a waterproofing contractor

Not all waterproofing contractors in Toronto are equal, and this is a space where low-quality work can fail silently for years before the problem resurfaces. When you are evaluating contractors, these questions separate the thorough ones from the box-checkers:

  • What is causing the water entry, specifically? A good contractor can tell you whether the problem is hydrostatic pressure, surface infiltration, a failed weeping tile system, or a specific crack. If they recommend a solution before explaining the diagnosis, that is a problem.
  • What does your warranty cover, and what does it exclude? Get this in writing and read the exclusions carefully before signing.
  • What happens to the sump pump discharge? Ask them to show you on a site sketch where water exits and how far it is routed from the foundation.
  • Do you recommend addressing the sump pump backup power? A contractor who does not bring up battery backup on their own is telling you something about their attention to the system as a whole.
  • Can I see a project similar to mine that you completed in the last two years? References for waterproofing are particularly important because the system needs time to prove itself through wet seasons.

Conclusion

A dry basement in Toronto is entirely achievable, even in a century-old house sitting on dense clay with original weeping tile that failed decades ago. But it requires diagnosing the actual problem rather than treating the symptoms, choosing the right method for the specific conditions, and having the work done by someone who understands both the waterproofing system and how it integrates with the foundation and the soil around it.

The temptation to apply a coat of waterproofing paint and hope for the best is understandable, especially when the problem only appears a few times a year. But deferred waterproofing in Toronto consistently costs more than early intervention — in remediation costs, in damage to structure and finishes, and in the disruption of having to redo finished spaces that should have been dry from the start.
