Basement finishing costs in Toronto for 2026

What it actually costs to finish a basement in Toronto — by project type, what drives costs, and how to read a contractor quote.

The best-ROI renovation most Toronto homeowners are sitting on

In a city where an extra bedroom adds six figures to a listing price and rental demand stays persistently ahead of supply, your unfinished basement is likely the most underused square footage you own. A typical Toronto semi or detached home carries 600 to 900 square feet of basement floor plate — space that currently generates zero value but costs you in heating bills and lost potential every single month.

Finishing that space is routinely one of the highest-return renovation investments available to Toronto homeowners. A well-executed legal secondary suite can yield $2,000 to $3,000 per month in rental income, often paying back the full renovation cost within five to seven years. Even a non-income-generating family room or home office finish typically adds more to appraised value than it costs to build — particularly in the $1M+ detached market where buyers expect livable below-grade space as a baseline.

But the numbers only work if you go in with clear eyes about what things actually cost. Toronto basement renovations span an enormous range — from a basic drywall-and-carpet finish to a fully permitted, underpinned, fire-separated legal suite — and conflating those scopes is where budget surprises come from. Here is a clear-eyed breakdown of 2026 costs.

Per-square-foot context: Standard basement finishes in Toronto run $100–$200 per square foot of finished area. A legal secondary suite — with its fire separation, dedicated mechanical, and full kitchen — typically lands at $150–$250 per square foot, or higher when underpinning is involved.

What drives costs up

Most basement renovation budgets that go sideways do so because one or more of the following factors was underestimated or discovered mid-project. Understanding them before you receive quotes puts you in a far stronger position.

Underpinning ($30,000 – $80,000+)

The single largest cost variable in Toronto basement work. Homes built before roughly 1970 — particularly in the pre-war and post-war housing stock of Leslieville, the Annex, Roncesvalles, and East York — routinely have basement ceiling heights of 6'0" to 6'8". A legal secondary suite requires a minimum clear height of 1.95 m (6'5") under the Ontario Building Code, and most buyers and tenants expect closer to 7'0" or more. When the existing slab doesn't get you there, the foundation must be underpinned — a process where the footing is excavated in stages and the floor is lowered. Cost depends heavily on linear footage of perimeter walls, soil type, and whether the project requires a shoring engineer. Budget $30,000 as a realistic floor; complex situations in clay-heavy soil with limited access can reach $80,000 or more.

Adding a bathroom ($20,000 – $40,000)

A basement bathroom requires breaking the concrete slab to run new drain lines below the existing floor grade. In a standard 3-piece bathroom — toilet, vanity, and shower — expect rough-in plumbing, slab cutting and patching, waterproofing, tile, fixtures, and ventilation to total $20,000 to $30,000 at a mid-range finish level. A full 4-piece with a soaker tub or a higher-end fixture specification pushes toward $35,000 to $40,000.

Egress windows ($3,000 – $8,000 each)

Any basement bedroom — whether for a legal suite or personal use — requires a window opening large enough to serve as an emergency exit under the Ontario Building Code. Most existing basement windows are too small. Installing a proper egress window involves cutting through the foundation wall, installing a window well, waterproofing the opening, and finishing the interior. Per window, expect $3,000 to $5,000 for a standard installation; larger openings in stone or block foundations, or those requiring extensive excavation on the exterior, can reach $6,000 to $8,000.

Separate HVAC for a secondary suite

A legal secondary suite must have its own dedicated heating system. Splitting an existing forced-air furnace is generally not compliant — the suite needs independent temperature control and, in most cases, its own heating appliance. A ductless mini-split heat pump is the most common solution, typically running $5,000 to $10,000 installed. If the suite also needs fresh air ventilation (HRV/ERV), add another $3,000 to $5,000.

Fire separation assemblies

The Ontario Building Code requires a minimum 30-minute fire separation between a secondary suite and the rest of the house. This means Type X drywall on the ceiling and any shared walls, fire-rated doors with closers at the suite entrance, and sealed penetrations for any mechanical, electrical, or plumbing elements that pass through the separation. When the separation is done properly by a contractor who understands the code, the materials cost is modest — but the detailing is labour-intensive and cannot be shortcut if you want the permit to close.

Permit fees

Toronto permit fees for basement finishing are calculated based on the value of the construction. For a $150,000 basement project, expect permit fees in the range of $1,500 to $2,500. This is a relatively small line item, but it is not optional — and any contractor who suggests skipping the permit on a legal suite is giving you advice that will create serious problems at the time of sale or with your insurer.

What a standard basement quote includes and excludes

Not all contractor quotes cover the same scope, and the gaps are where apples-to-apples comparisons break down. Here is what a complete, reputable basement quote should include — and what to ask about if it is not explicitly listed.

Typically included

  • Framing of all partition walls and ceiling furring
  • Insulation (batt or rigid foam to meet code minimum)
  • Drywall supply, hang, tape, and finish
  • Paint (one colour, standard finish)
  • Flooring (LVP or carpet — confirm which)
  • Pot lights and basic electrical to panel
  • Permit application and inspection scheduling
  • Debris removal and site cleanup

Common exclusions to ask about

  • Plumbing rough-in and bathroom fixtures
  • Egress window supply and installation
  • Underpinning or structural work
  • Waterproofing or drainage corrections
  • HVAC for the finished space
  • Kitchen cabinets and appliances (in suite projects)
  • Upgrade finishes beyond the stated allowances

A quote that looks significantly cheaper than its competitors is almost always covering less scope. Always compare the inclusions list, not just the bottom line.

Legal secondary suite requirements in Toronto

A legal secondary suite is not simply a finished basement with a kitchen — it is a regulated dwelling unit that must meet specific Ontario Building Code requirements and be registered with the City of Toronto. The key requirements as of 2026 are:

  • Minimum ceiling height of 1.95 m (6'5") clear throughout the main habitable areas. This is the floor-to-ceiling measurement after all finishes are in place — not the rough framing height.
  • Egress window in every bedroom. The opening must meet minimum dimensions for emergency exit. A bedroom without an egress window cannot legally be counted as a bedroom in the suite.
  • 30-minute fire separation between the suite and the rest of the house, including a fire-rated door with a self-closing mechanism at the point of separation.
  • Separate entrance that provides direct access to the suite without passing through the main unit. This can be a side entrance, rear entrance, or a dedicated exterior stairwell — but it must be independent.
  • Dedicated heating system capable of maintaining a minimum temperature without relying on the main unit's HVAC.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms interconnected between units as required by the Fire Code.

Completing these requirements and obtaining a final inspection sign-off is what makes the suite legal — which matters significantly for insurance coverage, rental licensing, and resale value. The City of Toronto also offers a forgivable loan program for homeowners creating legal secondary suites; it is worth checking current eligibility thresholds with the City before your project starts.

Getting the numbers right

The single best thing you can do before approaching contractors is to establish your ceiling height with a tape measure and determine whether your existing basement windows meet egress dimensions. Those two data points will immediately tell you whether underpinning and window replacement are in your future — which in turn lets you frame a realistic budget before you receive a single quote.

If your clear height is already at or above 1.95 m and you have a viable location for a separate entrance, a legal suite is a straightforward project. If you are sitting at 6'2" with hopper windows throughout, you are looking at a significantly more involved scope — but still one that pencils out for many Toronto homeowners given current rental rates.
