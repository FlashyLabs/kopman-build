Rear addition costs in Toronto

What a rear addition actually costs in 2026 — with real numbers, a full cost breakdown, and the process details that determine whether your project comes in on budget.

Why rear additions are the most common addition type in Toronto

The rear addition is the workhorse of Toronto residential construction. In a city where lots are narrow, lot severances are rare, and second-storey additions require far more structural intervention than most homeowners anticipate, building out the back of the house is often the most practical way to add meaningful square footage. You are extending the footprint, typically on one or two storeys, into the rear yard you already own.

The demand has not slowed. Detached and semi-detached homes in neighbourhoods like The Junction, Roncesvalles, Leslieville, and North York were built with compact floor plans that no longer match how families actually use their homes. Open-concept main floors, larger kitchens with island seating, primary suites with proper ensuites, mudrooms connected to the rear yard — none of these fit comfortably in a 1940s or 1950s bungalow without an addition.

What we build most often is a single-storey rear extension that bumps out the kitchen and creates a proper family room, or a two-storey rear addition that adds living space at grade and a primary bedroom above. Both project types are well-understood in Toronto's building department and relatively predictable to price — but "relatively predictable" still means a wide range depending on your specific lot, your home's existing structure, and the finishes you choose. This guide breaks down where that money goes.

What does a rear addition actually cost?

In 2026, construction costs in Toronto for rear additions run from approximately $350 to $650 per square foot of gross addition area, all-in, including permits, structural work, mechanical, and finishes. That range is wide because the variables that drive cost are significant. A simple single-storey slab-on-grade extension with standard finishes sits toward the lower end. A two-storey addition with a basement underpinning, radiant heated floors, and high-end kitchen millwork sits toward the upper end.

To put concrete numbers on it: a 200 sq ft single-storey rear addition that extends the kitchen and creates a breakfast nook will typically cost $90,000 to $140,000 all-in. A 400 sq ft two-storey addition — new family room at grade, new bedroom and bathroom above — typically runs $200,000 to $280,000. Those figures assume mid-range finishes, a straightforward lot, and no major surprises in the existing structure.

On the cost per square foot metric: Cost per square foot is a useful benchmark for comparing projects, but it can mislead if used in isolation. A 150 sq ft addition costs more per square foot than a 400 sq ft addition because fixed costs — permits, engineering, mobilization, foundation work, connection to existing structure — are spread over fewer square feet. Smaller additions are not cheaper in absolute terms than you might expect.

The factors that move the number most

Two projects that look similar on paper can come in at very different costs. These are the variables that matter most in Toronto specifically.

Lot constraints and zoning

Toronto's residential zoning bylaws set rear yard setback requirements, lot coverage maximums, and in some neighbourhoods, height restrictions. Many Toronto properties can accommodate a rear addition of meaningful size without triggering a variance. But if you are on a narrower lot, in a heritage conservation district, or trying to extend closer to the rear property line than the bylaw allows, you will need a minor variance application to the Committee of Adjustment. That process costs $3,000 to $8,000 in fees and professional preparation, takes three to four months, and introduces approval risk. Budget for it if your property is in any doubt.

What the existing structure can take

Opening up the rear wall of a Toronto home to create an open-plan connection between the existing house and the new addition almost always requires installing a flush beam or a dropped beam to carry the load formerly held by that wall. The size of that beam — and the columns or posts that carry it — depends on what is above it and how far it needs to span. In a one-and-a-half storey or two-storey home, removing a rear bearing wall requires a beam that may cost $8,000 to $18,000 in materials and labour alone, before the finish work around it. Engineers will need to specify it, and it needs to be built to that specification.

Older Toronto homes — especially pre-war construction in areas like Leslieville, the Annex, and High Park — sometimes have foundations that need remediation before a new load can be applied next to them. If you are planning an addition with a basement component, a structural engineer's assessment of the existing foundation is essential before you finalize your budget.

The foundation choice

Whether to build the addition on a slab, a crawl space, or a full basement is the single largest swing factor in foundation cost. Slab-on-grade with proper insulation and a frost wall is the most cost-effective and typically appropriate for a single-storey family room extension. A full basement under the addition — with underpinning of the adjacent existing foundation to match depth — adds $30,000 to $60,000 but delivers usable square footage below grade. A crawl space is sometimes used but is generally the worst of both worlds in a Toronto climate: not warm enough without careful insulation detailing, and not usable as living space.

Mechanical system capacity

Adding 400 square feet to a house draws more from the HVAC system, the electrical service, and sometimes the domestic water supply. In many older Toronto homes, the electrical panel is already at or near capacity. A 100-amp service that was adequate for a 1,400 sq ft house is not adequate once you have added a new kitchen, a new bathroom, and additional lighting circuits. Upgrading from 100 to 200 amp service typically costs $3,500 to $6,000 by itself. If your HVAC system cannot handle the additional load, you may need a supplemental mini-split or an upgrade to the existing forced-air system. These are real costs that some contractors leave out of early estimates and that show up mid-construction as change orders.

What to watch for in a low quote: When you receive a quote that looks significantly lower than the others, check what foundation type it assumes, whether it includes the structural beam work at the connection to the existing house, whether mechanical upgrades are included, and what allowances it sets for finishes. Low quotes often omit or undersize these categories and recover the margin through change orders once the job is underway.

The permit process for a rear addition in Toronto

Every rear addition in the City of Toronto requires a building permit, without exception. Any contractor who suggests proceeding without one is exposing you to significant legal and financial risk: unpermitted additions must be disclosed when you sell, and they can require costly removal or legalization after the fact. Insurance claims related to damage caused by unpermitted work can be denied. The permit process adds time to your project, but it also means your work is inspected at key stages — foundation, rough-in, insulation, and final — which protects you.

The typical permit timeline for a rear addition in Toronto in 2026 is eight to fourteen weeks from submission to permit issuance, assuming no zoning issues. If a minor variance is required, add three to four months for the Committee of Adjustment process before you can even submit for the building permit. Factor this into your project schedule: if you want to start construction in spring, your design and permit submission need to be underway by late autumn of the previous year.

What the permit submission requires

  • Architectural drawings: Prepared and stamped by a registered architect or designer. These show the existing and proposed floor plans, elevations, sections, and site plan with dimensions to property lines.
  • Structural drawings: Prepared and stamped by a licensed structural engineer. These specify all new structural elements including beam sizing, column locations, foundation design, and connections to the existing structure.
  • Energy compliance documentation: Ontario requires compliance with the Ontario Building Code's energy efficiency requirements. This is typically demonstrated through SB-12 compliance forms prepared by the designer.
  • Site plan: Showing the existing lot, existing house footprint, proposed addition footprint, and all setbacks to property lines and adjacent structures.

A realistic project timeline

From the day you first engage a designer to the day you move furniture into the new space, a rear addition in Toronto typically takes twelve to twenty months. That is not a construction delay — that is the actual full process when the permit timeline is included honestly. The construction phase itself, for a two-storey 400 sq ft addition, typically runs four to six months.

What is and isn't worth spending more on

After building many rear additions across Toronto, there are consistent patterns in where extra spend pays off and where it does not.

Worth spending more on

  • Windows and glazing. A rear addition that connects to a garden or outdoor space is transformed by generous glazing — larger windows, a sliding or folding door system to the deck, or a skylight over the kitchen extension. The quality of natural light in the space will matter every single day for as long as you live there. Do not cut here.
  • Insulation and thermal performance. Going beyond code minimum on insulation pays dividends in comfort and energy costs for decades. Exterior rigid insulation on the addition walls, an air-sealed ceiling assembly, and high-performance windows all add cost at construction but reduce operating costs and improve livability year-round.
  • Structural details done right. The beam installation, the waterproofing at the foundation, the flashing details where the new roof meets the existing wall — these are invisible once the finishes go in, but they are what determines whether you have problems ten years from now. Cutting corners here to save money is a choice you may regret.
  • Mechanical system sizing. Undersizing the heating and cooling for the addition creates a space that is uncomfortable in summer and cold in winter. Get the HVAC system sized properly for the new total square footage, not patched onto an undersized existing system.

Where mid-range finishes perform well

  • Flooring. Quality engineered hardwood in the $8 to $14 per sq ft range (supply and install) performs nearly as well as solid hardwood and is better suited to the temperature fluctuations common near exterior walls. The visual difference from high-end alternatives is modest in everyday use.
  • Interior doors and hardware. Solid core doors are worth the upgrade over hollow core for sound performance, but the difference between mid-range and high-end hardware is largely aesthetic.
  • Paint. Premium paint from Benjamin Moore or Sherwin-Williams covers and holds better than budget alternatives, but you are not gaining much from the luxury tier above that.

How to choose the right contractor for a rear addition

A rear addition is a more complex project than most interior renovations. You are building new structure, connecting to existing structure, integrating mechanical systems, and managing multiple inspections and subcontractor trades over several months. The contractor you choose needs to have done this before — specifically, they need experience with the structural and sequencing challenges that additions present, not just interior renovation experience.

Ask specifically: how many rear additions have you built in Toronto, and can I visit one or speak with those clients? Ask how they handle the transition from existing to new at the foundation level, how they manage the structural opening through the existing rear wall, and how they sequence the work to minimize disruption to the occupied home during the months when the rear wall is opened up. A contractor who can answer those questions in concrete terms has done it. One who gives you vague assurances has not.

Get a detailed, itemized quote — not a single lump-sum number. Each major category should be broken out so you can see what is and is not included, compare quotes on equal terms, and understand where contingency may be needed. A quote that presents a single number for a complex addition project is a quote that will generate change orders.

Conclusion: budget honestly, plan early

A rear addition is one of the most impactful investments a Toronto homeowner can make — it adds square footage, transforms how the home functions, and in the right neighbourhood, returns strong value when the home is eventually sold. But it is a significant construction project, not a straightforward renovation, and it needs to be approached with honest cost expectations and a realistic schedule.

The key takeaways from this guide:

  • Budget $350 to $650 per square foot all-in, depending on scope, finishes, and lot complexity. Smaller additions cost more per square foot than larger ones due to fixed costs.
  • The full project timeline from design start to move-in is twelve to twenty months. The permit process alone takes eight to fourteen weeks, and longer if a variance is required.
  • The biggest cost variables are the foundation type, the structural work at the connection to the existing house, and the mechanical system upgrades your existing home requires.
  • Permits are non-negotiable. Any contractor who suggests skipping them is telling you something important about how they operate.
  • A detailed, itemized quote is the only kind that gives you meaningful protection. A lump sum from an under-experienced contractor is how projects end up 40% over budget.

If you are in the early planning stages, the best first step is a conversation with a contractor who has built additions like yours before. Get a realistic number early — before you fall in love with a design that your budget cannot support — and plan your schedule accordingly.
