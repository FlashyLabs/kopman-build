Primary ensuite vs main bathroom: cost comparison for Toronto

Two very different renovations, two very different budgets — and the decision about which to do first matters more than most homeowners realize.

Why this comparison matters

When Toronto homeowners approach us about a bathroom renovation, one of the first questions is almost always the same: should we do the ensuite first, or the main bathroom? The answer depends on your household, your timeline, and your motivations — but it also depends on understanding that these are genuinely different projects with meaningfully different cost profiles, scopes, and complexities.

A primary ensuite renovation in a midtown Toronto semi is not the same job as a main bathroom renovation in a 1960s Etobicoke bungalow, even if both rooms are roughly the same square footage. The expectations are different, the fixtures are different, the level of finish is different, and the trade coordination required is different. Getting clear on those differences before you budget is the foundation of a renovation that delivers what you actually wanted.

This guide is based on projects we have completed across Toronto and the GTA over 25 years. The cost ranges reflect current 2026 labour and material pricing in the Toronto market, and they assume professional full-service contracting — not owner-supply, owner-managed, or hybrid approaches, which introduce their own risks and savings.

How the two rooms differ in scope

Before getting into numbers, it helps to be precise about what we mean by each room, because the terminology varies widely. For the purposes of this guide:

  • Primary ensuite: A private bathroom directly accessed from the primary bedroom, used exclusively (or primarily) by the homeowners. Typically 60–120 sq ft in Toronto homes, though luxury ensuites run larger. The expectation is a spa-like or hotel-quality finish — heated floors, large-format tile, a freestanding tub or oversized walk-in shower (or both), double vanity, and premium fixtures throughout.
  • Main bathroom: The shared family bathroom, typically accessed from the hallway and used by all household members including children. Usually 45–75 sq ft. Functional efficiency matters more than luxury; the room needs to work hard for multiple users. Tub-shower combinations are standard; vanity storage is a priority.

These differences in expectation drive meaningful differences in cost, even when the footprint is similar. The primary ensuite is where homeowners spend more on finishes, on plumbing fixtures, and on features like heated floors and custom millwork. The main bathroom is where the emphasis shifts to durability, storage, and utility.

Cost ranges: what to expect in Toronto

The ranges below reflect our current project experience in Toronto and the GTA. They assume full gut renovation — removing everything down to studs and subfloor, upgrading waterproofing and rough-in as needed, and rebuilding to current code. Cosmetic refreshes (retiling over existing tile, replacing fixtures in place) cost significantly less but are not the same project.

Scope / Tier Primary Ensuite Main Bathroom
Mid-range renovation $35,000 – $55,000 $22,000 – $38,000
Upper-mid renovation $55,000 – $85,000 $38,000 – $58,000
High-end / luxury $85,000 – $150,000+ $58,000 – $90,000
Typical Toronto project $60,000 – $90,000 $30,000 – $50,000

The "typical Toronto project" row represents the most common budget we see from homeowners in established midtown and east-end neighbourhoods who want a thorough, lasting result without going to the absolute top of the market. These are not budget renovations, but they are also not the projects where we are sourcing custom Italian stone or importing a freestanding tub from a European manufacturer.

Why Toronto costs more than national averages: Renovation labour rates in Toronto run 20–35% above the national average, reflecting both the cost of living and the density of skilled trade demand in the city. If you see bathroom renovation figures from a national source quoting $15,000–$25,000 for a full renovation, those numbers do not apply here. Plan accordingly.

Where the money goes: line by line

Primary ensuite cost breakdown

A mid-to-upper-end primary ensuite in Toronto at roughly $70,000 total typically allocates roughly as follows:

  • Demolition and disposal: $1,800–$3,500. Includes full gut, haul-away, and any asbestos testing or abatement if the home is pre-1990 (common in older Toronto neighbourhoods).
  • Rough-in plumbing: $4,500–$9,000. Repositioning drains or supply lines for a walk-in shower, freestanding tub, or double vanity adds significant cost. If the layout stays where it is, rough-in is cheaper.
  • Electrical: $2,500–$5,000. Heated floor wiring, exhaust fan, additional circuits for heated towel bars, and GFCI upgrades throughout.
  • Waterproofing and substrate: $1,500–$3,000. A proper shower membrane and drain assembly is not optional — it is what determines whether you are back in three years dealing with water damage behind the walls.
  • Tile and installation: $8,000–$18,000. Large-format tiles, book-matched stone, and elaborate shower niches are all labour-intensive. Material cost alone on premium tile can be $25–$60 per sq ft.
  • Heated floor system: $2,000–$4,500. Electric in-floor heating including thermostat. Hydronic (hot water) systems are more expensive and rarely done in bathroom renovations.
  • Vanity and millwork: $6,000–$16,000. A custom double vanity with integrated storage, solid surfaces, and hardware in the $8,000–$12,000 range is common. Semi-custom is available at the lower end.
  • Plumbing fixtures: $5,000–$14,000. Two sinks, two faucets, a rain shower with separate hand shower, body jets if included, a freestanding or built-in tub with filler, and all trim. Fixture costs vary enormously by brand.
  • Glass shower enclosure: $3,500–$7,500. Frameless glass at 10mm thickness with a quality system (Coastal, Fleurco, or custom) is standard at this level.
  • Lighting and mirrors: $1,500–$4,000. Recessed lighting, backlit mirrors, and sconce-level vanity lighting are typical expectations.
  • General contractor overhead and project management: Included in labour rates above, but accounts for scheduling trades, pulling permits, coordinating inspections, and warranty coverage.

Main bathroom cost breakdown

A well-executed main bathroom renovation in the $38,000–$50,000 range allocates differently:

  • Demolition and disposal: $1,500–$2,800. Similar process, slightly lower cost if the room is smaller.
  • Rough-in plumbing: $2,500–$6,000. Main bathrooms usually retain their existing layout, keeping plumbing costs lower. Adding a second sink is the most common scope addition.
  • Electrical: $1,800–$3,500. Exhaust fan, lighting circuits, GFCI outlets. Heated floor is less common but not unusual.
  • Waterproofing and substrate: $1,200–$2,500. Same quality standard required for the tub surround and shower area.
  • Tile and installation: $5,000–$11,000. More practical tile choices at mid-range price points; the tub surround and floor tile are the focal points.
  • Vanity and millwork: $3,500–$9,000. Single or double vanity; storage is the priority. Semi-custom and stock cabinetry with quality countertops performs well at this budget level.
  • Tub and shower fixtures: $2,500–$6,500. A quality alcove tub, shower system, and trim at mid-range price points from brands like Kohler, American Standard, or Riobel.
  • Toilet: $600–$1,800 supplied and installed. Wall-hung toilets cost more; floor-mounted comfort-height models are standard.
  • Accessories, mirrors, lighting: $1,200–$3,000. Simpler package than the ensuite but still worth budgeting properly.

Timeline differences

Both rooms share the same general construction sequence: demo, rough-in, board and waterproof, tile, fixtures, and finish. But the ensuite almost always takes longer because the scope is more complex and the tolerance for error is lower. A homeowner who has invested $80,000 in their ensuite will notice every imperfect grout joint; the same homeowner in the main bathroom is more focused on function.

Phase Primary Ensuite Main Bathroom
Design and planning 4–8 weeks 2–4 weeks
Permit approval (City of Toronto) 4–10 weeks 4–10 weeks
Demolition 2–3 days 1–2 days
Rough-in (plumbing, electrical) 5–10 days 3–6 days
Board, waterproof, tile 10–18 days 6–12 days
Fixtures, millwork, finish 5–10 days 4–7 days
Total on-site construction 4–7 weeks 3–5 weeks

Note that permit approval timelines are the same for both projects because both require a building permit in Toronto for plumbing and electrical work. The permit is pulled once approvals are obtained, and inspections happen at rough-in and completion. Plan for the full permit timeline before your target start date — not after you have signed a contract.

What drives cost up in either room

Several factors push bathroom renovation costs above the midpoints of the ranges above, in both room types:

  • Layout changes: Moving the toilet, relocating the shower, or adding a second sink where plumbing does not currently exist requires opening walls and floors to reroute drain lines. In a two-storey Toronto home, that may also mean working in the ceiling of the room below. Budget an additional $3,000–$8,000 depending on scope.
  • Structural surprises: Older Toronto homes — particularly pre-1960 stock in neighbourhoods like the Annex, Rosedale, or Riverdale — routinely reveal deteriorated subfloor, undersized joists, galvanized plumbing that must be replaced, or knob-and-tube wiring that cannot be left in place. Discovering any of these once the demo is done means change orders. A good contractor will flag the risk upfront; the contingency is typically 10–15% of budget.
  • Luxury fixture selections: Moving from mid-range to premium fixtures can add $8,000–$20,000 to a budget without changing the footprint at all. Brands like Watermark, Fantini, THG, or Phylrich charge a significant premium over quality production-grade options. There is a real quality difference at the top end, but it is worth understanding what you are paying for.
  • Custom millwork: A fully custom vanity built by a local millwork shop will outperform a semi-custom cabinet in quality and longevity, but the cost difference is real — typically $3,000–$8,000 more for a comparable unit.
  • Steam or spa features: A steam shower adds $4,000–$9,000 (generator, specialized door sealing, ceiling slope, controls). Chromotherapy lighting, built-in sound, or digital shower systems each add $2,000–$5,000.

On permits for bathroom renovations in Toronto: Any work involving new plumbing rough-in, electrical panel changes, or structural modifications requires a permit through the City of Toronto Building Division. Cosmetic work — replacing a vanity in place, swapping a toilet, retiling without moving plumbing — generally does not. If your contractor suggests skipping the permit on a full gut renovation to save time or money, that is a serious red flag. Unpermitted plumbing and electrical work creates liability when you sell and may void your home insurance in the event of a related claim.

Which one should you do first?

The honest answer is: it depends on your situation. But there are patterns that consistently hold across our projects.

Do the ensuite first if...

  • The primary bathroom is the one you use every day and it is materially affecting your quality of life. A 40-year-old ensuite with a cramped shower and no storage is a daily inconvenience in a way that the main bathroom, which you use less personally, simply is not.
  • You are planning to sell within 3–5 years and want the renovation to serve both your use and the resale. A beautifully renovated primary ensuite is one of the most compelling features for buyers in Toronto's $1.5M–$3M market. It photographs well and it signals that the home has been maintained to a high standard.
  • Your children are young. The main bathroom will take abuse for the next decade regardless of what you put in it. Renovating it now and having it beaten up by a rotating cast of kids, guests, and teenage use means you are replacing it again in ten years. Wait until the household use pattern stabilizes.

Do the main bathroom first if...

  • The main bathroom has a functional problem — an inefficient layout, inadequate ventilation causing mould, or a tub-only configuration that does not serve your household — that the ensuite does not. Functional failures take priority over luxury upgrades.
  • You have guests regularly and the main bathroom is what visitors see. In Toronto homes that do a lot of entertaining, the main bathroom is a more visible reflection of the home's overall standard than the ensuite, which most guests will never enter.
  • Budget is a constraint. The main bathroom renovation is almost always the less expensive project. If you cannot comfortably fund a high-quality ensuite renovation right now, doing the main bathroom well and deferring the ensuite is better than cutting corners in the ensuite to hit a budget that is too tight for the scope.

Doing both at once

Occasionally homeowners ask whether renovating both bathrooms simultaneously makes sense. The answer is: sometimes, but not always. The advantages are real — you are only disrupting the household once, the general contractor mobilization cost is spread across two projects, and tile and fixture orders can sometimes be consolidated. The disadvantage is equally real — you are without a functioning bathroom in the home for the duration of the project, which means either living through a more disruptive situation or temporarily relocating. For Toronto families with children, simultaneous bathroom renovation is often more disruptive than it is worth. For couples in a large home with a powder room to fall back on, it can make excellent practical and financial sense.

ROI: does the investment come back?

The ROI question on bathroom renovations in Toronto is nuanced. The primary ensuite, done well, consistently ranks among the highest-returning renovation investments for homes in the $1.2M–$3M range. Buyers at this price point expect a well-finished ensuite; a dated or cramped one is a negotiating point that can cost you more at sale than the renovation would have.

The main bathroom returns value more modestly, but a renovated main bathroom in a dated home can meaningfully accelerate a sale by reducing the scope of "work to be done" that buyers factor into their offers. In competitive Toronto neighbourhoods, a move-in-ready home with both bathrooms renovated commands a premium over comparable homes where buyers are pricing in the renovation themselves.

Neither bathroom renovation is a simple dollar-for-dollar return investment, and you should not budget one expecting to recover 100% of the cost at sale. The realistic return on a well-executed ensuite renovation in Toronto's current market is roughly 60–80% of the invested cost, reflected in sale price. The value of using a beautifully finished bathroom for the years you remain in the home is real but harder to quantify.

Conclusion: budget precisely, decide deliberately

The most common mistake we see Toronto homeowners make on bathroom renovations is not choosing the wrong room — it is underfunding the room they did choose. A primary ensuite renovation budgeted at $35,000 in the current Toronto market is going to produce a result that disappoints, because the scope that budget allows cannot deliver the expectation that comes with the word "ensuite." Better to do the main bathroom well at $40,000 than to attempt a luxury ensuite at a budget that forces constant compromise.

Get a clear scope defined before you set a budget. Know what layout changes you want to make, what your fixture expectations are, and whether you are willing to live with mid-range tile or want to spend on premium stone. A contractor who walks your space, asks those questions, and gives you a detailed written estimate — not a back-of-napkin range — is the starting point for a renovation that lands where you intend it to.
