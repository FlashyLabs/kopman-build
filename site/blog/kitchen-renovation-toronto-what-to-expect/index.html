Kitchen renovation in Toronto: what to actually expect

A realistic look at the kitchen renovation process — phases, timeline, hidden surprises, and how to avoid the most common mistakes.

A kitchen renovation is one of the highest-value investments you can make in a Toronto home. It's also one of the most disruptive. After 25 years of building and renovating kitchens across Toronto, the GTA, and Muskoka, we've seen every version of how this goes — the smooth projects and the ones derailed by surprises nobody planned for. This guide gives you the unvarnished version: what happens in what order, how long it actually takes, what hides inside the walls of older Toronto homes, and what decisions you must have locked in before a single cabinet comes down.

The phases of a kitchen renovation, in order

Every kitchen renovation moves through the same sequence. Skipping or compressing steps is where projects go sideways. Here is the order as we run it:

1. Design finalization

Before anything physical happens, every decision that affects structure, plumbing, or electrical must be settled. Cabinet layout, appliance locations, island dimensions, range hood routing, sink placement — all of it. Changes made after demolition begins cost two to three times what they would have cost on paper.

2. Permits

Toronto Building requires permits for structural work (removing walls, adding beams), electrical panel upgrades, and plumbing relocations. A straightforward kitchen refresh with no layout changes typically does not require a permit. An open-concept conversion or a full gut renovation almost always does. Plan two to four weeks for permit approval in the City of Toronto; timelines vary by ward and season.

3. Cabinet ordering

This is the step that surprises most homeowners. Semi-custom and custom cabinetry carries a lead time of six to twelve weeks from order to delivery. Cabinets must be ordered before demolition starts — not after. If you wait until the walls are stripped to finalize your cabinet selections, you will be living in a gutted kitchen for months. Order first, demolish second.

4. Demolition

Demo day is fast and loud. A full kitchen strip-out — cabinets, countertops, flooring, drywall — typically takes one to two days. What is discovered behind those walls determines how the rest of the project unfolds. More on that below.

5. Rough-in plumbing and electrical

With walls open, plumbers relocate drain lines and supply pipes, and electricians run new circuits for the refrigerator, dishwasher, range, microwave, and dedicated small-appliance circuits required by current code. This is the right time to upgrade an undersized panel if the home needs it. Both trades require inspections before walls close.

6. Drywall

Once inspections pass, new drywall goes up. Skim-coat plaster finishing follows for a smooth substrate. Allow several days for compound to dry between coats — this step cannot be rushed without visible consequences later.

7. Cabinetry installation

Cabinet installation takes two to four days for a typical Toronto kitchen. Precision matters here: cabinets must be level and plumb even when floors and walls are not — and in most older Toronto homes, they are not. Shimming, scribing, and careful measurement are what separate a clean install from one that looks off for the next 20 years.

8. Countertop template and installation

Countertops — whether quartz, granite, or marble — cannot be templated until cabinets are fully installed and confirmed square. The fabricator comes to template, then returns the slab to their shop for cutting and edge profiling. Expect a minimum of two weeks between template day and countertop installation. This lag is built into every properly scheduled kitchen renovation; it should not be a surprise.

9. Backsplash tile

Tile goes in after countertops are set, so the installer can scribe to the actual stone rather than an assumed height. Grouting and curing add another day or two before the area can be used.

10. Appliance delivery and installation

Built-in appliances — wall ovens, column refrigerators, integrated dishwashers — are coordinated with cabinet installation. Freestanding ranges and refrigerators go in near the end. Confirm delivery windows early; some appliances carry their own multi-week lead times.

11. Lighting, trim, and hardware

Pot lights, pendants, and under-cabinet lighting are wired during rough-in but fixtures install at the end. Crown moulding, toe-kicks, filler panels, and cabinet hardware follow. These finishing details are what give a kitchen its polished, complete feel — and they take longer than clients expect.

12. Punch list

A final walkthrough to identify anything that needs adjustment, touch-up, or replacement. A thorough punch list review before final payment is standard practice and protects both parties.

How long does a Toronto kitchen renovation actually take?

For a full kitchen renovation — gut to finish — budget eight to fourteen weeks of on-site construction time, plus the pre-construction period for design, permits, and cabinet lead time. When you add it up, a homeowner who starts the planning process today should expect to be cooking in their new kitchen in four to six months. Projects that feel like they drag on forever are almost always ones where decisions were deferred or cabinets were ordered late.

The single biggest schedule killer we see: homeowners who want to "see the space first" before ordering cabinets. By the time demo reveals anything meaningful, you have already lost six to eight weeks of cabinet lead time.

Hidden surprises in Toronto homes

Toronto's housing stock is old. A large share of the city's detached and semi-detached homes were built between the 1920s and the early 1980s, and their bones reflect it. Here is what we find regularly once walls open:

  • Load-bearing walls. Open-concept kitchens are among the most requested renovations in the city, and a surprising number of the walls separating kitchens from dining rooms are load-bearing. Removing them requires an engineer-stamped beam design, a permit, and temporary shoring during construction. Budget $5,000–$15,000 for the structural work alone, depending on span.
  • Knob-and-tube wiring. Homes built before 1950 — and some from the 1960s — frequently still have knob-and-tube wiring in portions of the house. It is ungrounded, cannot support modern circuit loads, and most insurance providers will not cover a home with active knob-and-tube. Discovering it during a kitchen reno means rewiring that portion of the house. The cost is real, but so is the safety improvement.
  • Asbestos. Homes built before 1985 may contain asbestos in vinyl floor tiles, floor tile adhesive (the black "cutback" mastic), textured ceiling finishes, pipe insulation, and drywall joint compound. Any suspect material must be tested before disturbance, and abatement must be performed by a licensed contractor in accordance with Ontario Regulation 278/05. This adds time and cost but is not optional.
  • Plumbing that needs relocation. Drain lines in older Toronto homes were often run in ways that made sense at the time but conflict with a modern kitchen layout. If your new island includes a sink, or you are moving the main sink to a different wall, expect the plumber to open the subfloor to re-route the drain stack connection.
  • Subfloor damage. The area under and around the original sink almost always shows some degree of water damage from decades of minor leaks. In severe cases, the subfloor needs to be cut out and replaced before new flooring can go down. We factor this possibility into every kitchen estimate.

How to live through it

Eight to fourteen weeks without a functional kitchen is genuinely hard. Homeowners who plan for it fare much better than those who don't. A few practical measures that make a meaningful difference:

  • Set up a temporary kitchen in another room before demo starts. A folding table, a microwave, a toaster oven, an electric kettle, and a mini-fridge covers most daily needs. Keep it stocked before day one.
  • Ask your contractor to install plastic dust barriers at all doorways adjoining the work zone. Construction dust is fine and travels farther than you think. Barriers protect your furniture, your HVAC filters, and your sanity.
  • Protect flooring in adjacent rooms and hallways. Contractors move heavy materials repeatedly throughout the project. Ram board or similar temporary floor protection in high-traffic paths prevents damage to hardwood and tile you are not replacing.
  • Establish a daily check-in rhythm with your contractor so you know what is happening each day. Surprises feel less surprising when communication is consistent.

What does a Toronto kitchen renovation cost?

Cost ranges vary widely depending on scope. Here is an honest breakdown of what you should expect in the current Toronto market:

ScopeTypical RangeWhat's Included
Cosmetic refresh $15,000 – $35,000 New countertops, cabinet hardware, paint, faucet, lighting fixtures. Existing cabinet boxes stay in place.
Mid-range renovation $60,000 – $110,000 Full cabinet replacement, new countertops, backsplash, flooring, appliances, updated electrical and plumbing within existing layout.
Full custom renovation $120,000 – $250,000+ Layout changes, structural work, custom millwork, high-end appliances, designer tile, integrated lighting design, full electrical and plumbing re-work.

These ranges assume a standard Toronto kitchen of 150–250 square feet. Prices shift based on material selections, structural complexity, and what is discovered during demolition. Any contractor quoting a firm fixed price before the walls are open is either pricing in a large contingency or underestimating the unknown — ask which.

Decisions that must be finalized before demo starts

Indecision mid-project is expensive. The following decisions need to be locked in before demolition begins — not during the project, and not after cabinets arrive:

  • Cabinet brand, door style, finish, and complete layout including all fillers and panels
  • Countertop material and edge profile
  • Sink model and faucet (these determine the cutout in the countertop)
  • Appliance models — especially anything built-in, which affects cabinet sizing
  • Range hood type and venting path (exterior venting requires a penetration through an exterior wall or roof)
  • Flooring material and whether it runs under the toe-kicks or stops at them
  • Backsplash tile selection and pattern
  • Lighting fixture locations and types

A change order mid-renovation — say, swapping a 36-inch range for a 48-inch range after cabinets have been ordered — can cost $3,000 to $8,000 in cabinet modifications, restocking fees, and lost schedule time. Decide once, decide early.
