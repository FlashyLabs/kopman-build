Underpinning and lowering a basement in Toronto

Costs, process, permits, and the real-world considerations that determine whether a basement lowering project delivers the value homeowners expect — from contractors who have done it hundreds of times.

Why Toronto homeowners underpin

Toronto's housing stock is old by North American standards. A significant proportion of the semi-detached and detached homes in the older core neighbourhoods — Leslieville, the Annex, Roncesvalles, East York, Leaside, Forest Hill — were built between 1900 and 1960, when a six-foot basement ceiling was considered adequate. Nobody was finishing those spaces. They were for coal, the furnace, and storage.

Today, a basement with a finished ceiling height of under seven feet does not function as livable space in any meaningful way. You cannot rent it. You cannot use it as a bedroom, a proper family room, or a home office without feeling like the walls are pressing in. And in a Toronto market where a legal basement apartment can add $1,800 to $2,800 per month in rental income, or where adding a functional bedroom and bathroom can make the difference between selling and not selling, an unlivable basement represents a significant unrealized asset.

Underpinning is the structural engineering solution that makes the floor lower while keeping the house standing. Done properly, it transforms a space that was otherwise unusable into one of the most valuable square feet in the home.

What underpinning actually is

Underpinning is the process of extending the existing foundation of a building deeper into the ground. Your house sits on footings — concrete pads that transfer the load of the structure down into the soil. In a typical Toronto century home, those footings may be at a depth that leaves only six feet between the basement floor and the floor joists above. To get eight feet of clear ceiling height (the minimum for a comfortable finished basement, and a requirement for legal secondary suites), you need to lower the floor by roughly two feet.

You cannot simply excavate the floor and dig down. If you remove the soil beneath the existing footings without replacing the structural support, the footing — and the wall above it — will move. The solution is to excavate and pour new concrete in sections, one pin at a time, so the load is always supported while the work progresses. That process is called pit underpinning, and it is what the vast majority of Toronto basement lowering projects use.

Pit underpinning versus bench footing

There are two primary approaches to lowering a basement in Toronto. The right one for your home depends on your foundation type, lot size, soil conditions, and budget.

Pit underpinning extends the existing footings straight down. Each pin is excavated in alternating sections (typically 3-foot widths), concrete is poured and cured, and the adjacent section is then excavated. The sequence prevents any section of wall from losing its support simultaneously. Pit underpinning is the more structurally rigorous approach and is suitable for virtually any home, but it costs more per linear foot and produces slightly less net headroom gain per dollar because the new footing takes up vertical space.

Bench footing (sometimes called bench underpinning) does not extend the footings downward. Instead, new concrete is poured at the base of the existing wall like a ledge, and the floor is excavated down in the interior of the space only. The bench itself takes up floor area — typically 12 to 16 inches of width along each wall — but the cost per square foot is lower and the structural complexity is reduced. It is appropriate for homes with poured concrete foundations and good soil conditions, but it reduces usable floor area and is sometimes not permitted depending on the foundation type and the engineer's assessment.

Which method is right for your home? The decision between pit underpinning and bench footing is not yours to make alone — it requires a structural engineer's assessment. The engineer inspects the existing foundation, reviews soil reports, and specifies the method and sequence. Any contractor who recommends an approach before an engineer has looked at your specific foundation is getting ahead of the process.

The underpinning process, step by step

A basement lowering in Toronto follows a defined sequence. Understanding the stages helps homeowners plan around disruption, set realistic timeline expectations, and ask better questions of their contractor.

Stage 1: Engineering and permits

Every basement underpinning project in Toronto requires a building permit from the City of Toronto Building Division, and every permit application must be supported by drawings and specifications prepared and sealed by a licensed structural engineer. The engineer specifies the underpinning sequence, the pin dimensions, the concrete mix and reinforcement requirements, and the waterproofing approach. Without these drawings, no permit will be issued, and no legitimate contractor should proceed without one.

Permit processing times through the City of Toronto have improved but remain variable. Expect four to eight weeks for a standard permit, though projects in older neighbourhoods with heritage designations or properties near ravines may face additional review. Factor this into your timeline before committing to any start date.

Stage 2: Site preparation and interior demolition

Before excavation begins, any existing concrete floor must be broken up and removed (typically with a jackhammer and handwork in the narrower areas near the footings). Interior partitions in the basement are demolished if they interfere with the work zone. A temporary support system may be installed beneath the floor joists above if the structural engineer requires it. This stage takes approximately one to two weeks for a typical semi-detached or detached home.

Stage 3: Underpinning excavation and concrete pours

This is the core of the work and the most time-consuming stage. Each pin is excavated by hand — mechanical equipment cannot reach under an existing footing safely — and then formed, reinforced with rebar, and poured with concrete. The concrete must cure before the adjacent pin can be excavated. The sequence continues around the entire perimeter of the foundation.

For a typical 1,000-square-foot semi-detached basement in Toronto, expect 20 to 30 individual pins depending on the pin width specified by the engineer. At two to three pours per week (limited by curing time), the excavation and concrete phase alone can run six to ten weeks.

Stage 4: Interior excavation and new floor pour

Once all pins are poured and cured, the interior soil can be excavated to the new finished grade. Soil is removed through the basement windows or via a chute to a bin on the street. A new drainage membrane and weeping tile system is installed, followed by a layer of compacted granular fill and rigid insulation, and finally the new concrete floor slab is poured. Depending on basement size, this stage takes one to two weeks.

Stage 5: Waterproofing

When the foundation walls are exposed during underpinning, it is the right time to address any existing waterproofing deficiencies. The exterior of the foundation should ideally be waterproofed during construction, but interior waterproofing systems — drainage membranes, weeping tile connected to a sump pump — are standard practice for Toronto underpinning projects where exterior access is limited by adjacent structures. Do not skip this step. Water infiltration after underpinning is expensive and disruptive to repair once the space is finished.

Stage 6: Inspections and completion

City of Toronto building inspectors review the work at defined intervals: typically after excavation, after rebar installation, and after concrete pours. Final inspection is required before the project closes out. A diligent contractor coordinates inspections proactively rather than letting them become a bottleneck.

How much does underpinning cost in Toronto?

Underpinning costs vary significantly based on the size of the foundation perimeter, the method used, soil conditions, the existing state of the foundation, and what additional work is done alongside the structural work. The numbers below reflect 2026 pricing for the Toronto market.

Item Typical Range (CAD) Notes
Structural engineer (drawings + inspections) $4,000 – $9,000 Required for permit. Larger or more complex foundations cost more.
City of Toronto building permit $1,500 – $3,500 Fee is calculated on project value; includes admin and inspection fees.
Pit underpinning (labour + materials) $350 – $500 per linear foot A 120 linear-foot perimeter runs $42,000 – $60,000 for underpinning alone.
Bench footing (labour + materials) $200 – $320 per linear foot Lower cost per foot but reduces usable floor area by 12–16 in. on each wall.
Interior excavation and soil removal $8,000 – $18,000 Depends on volume of soil and site access for removal.
New concrete floor slab $8,000 – $15,000 Includes drainage membrane, granular fill, insulation, and 4-in. slab.
Interior waterproofing system $6,000 – $14,000 Drainage membrane, weeping tile, sump pit, and sump pump.
Window wells (egress, if required) $2,500 – $5,000 each Required for bedroom use and legal secondary suites.
Total (structural work only, no finishing) $70,000 – $130,000 For a typical Toronto semi-detached or detached home, 900–1,200 sq ft.

These figures cover the structural work only — the underpinning, excavation, slab, and waterproofing. They do not include basement finishing (framing, insulation, drywall, electrical, plumbing, flooring, or a bathroom). A full underpinning plus finished basement project on a typical Toronto home will run $120,000 to $220,000 depending on the scope and finish level.

On low quotes: Underpinning is one of the categories in Toronto renovation where low quotes are a serious warning sign. The labour is intensive, the concrete and engineering costs are fixed, and the permit process is not optional. A quote that is 30% or 40% below the others is almost always missing scope, planning to use non-union or unlicensed labour, or omitting the engineer and permit. The consequences of a failed or non-compliant underpinning are structural — not cosmetic. This is not an area to optimize on price.

What affects your cost most

Several factors can move a Toronto underpinning quote meaningfully in either direction. Being aware of them helps you budget accurately and understand why quotes vary between properties.

  • Foundation perimeter length. Underpinning is priced per linear foot, so a detached home with a larger footprint costs more than a semi-detached on the same street. A typical Toronto semi-detached has a perimeter of 100 to 130 linear feet; a detached may be 140 to 180 feet or more.
  • Existing foundation condition. Stone rubble foundations, common in homes built before 1920, are more complex to underpin than poured concrete or concrete block. They may require additional stabilization work and a more conservative pin sequence, both of which add cost and time.
  • Soil conditions. Clay-heavy soil, a high water table, or soft ground conditions require slower excavation and curing sequences and may require dewatering. Toronto has a mix of soil profiles across its neighbourhoods, and the east end tends to have more variable conditions than the more stable glacial till in parts of North York and Etobicoke.
  • Site access. Row houses with no side yard access require all soil removal to pass through the interior of the home or through basement windows. This slows excavation considerably compared to a detached home with a driveway or side yard where a conveyor or chute can be deployed efficiently.
  • How much height you are gaining. Lowering by 12 inches versus 24 inches involves meaningfully different amounts of excavation and pins. The engineering is also more conservative when you are going deeper, particularly in clay soils.
  • Whether waterproofing is included. Some contractors quote the structural work and waterproofing as a single package; others present them separately. Make sure you are comparing apples to apples when reviewing quotes.

Underpinning timeline: what to expect

From the time you engage a contractor and begin the permitting process to the completion of the structural work (before basement finishing begins), a typical Toronto underpinning project runs as follows:

Phase Duration
Structural engineering drawings and permit application 3 – 5 weeks
City permit processing 4 – 8 weeks
Interior demolition and site setup 1 – 2 weeks
Underpinning excavation and concrete pours 6 – 12 weeks
Interior excavation and soil removal 1 – 2 weeks
Waterproofing and drainage installation 1 – 2 weeks
New concrete floor slab pour and cure 1 – 2 weeks
Final inspection and permit close-out 1 – 2 weeks
Total (structural work only) 18 – 35 weeks from contractor engagement

Permitting is consistently the longest variable. Projects started in the fall can often get permits and begin work through the winter months (underpinning itself is interior work and not weather-dependent). Projects that begin the permitting process in spring often face summer backlogs at the City. Factor this into your planning, particularly if you are working toward a rental income target or a sale date.

Permits: what is required and who pulls them

Basement underpinning in Toronto requires a building permit without exception. The permit application must include:

  • Structural drawings and specifications sealed by a Professional Engineer (P.Eng.) licensed in Ontario
  • A site plan showing the property, the building footprint, and any relevant setbacks
  • Elevation drawings indicating existing and proposed basement heights
  • A description of the underpinning sequence and method
  • Details of the new slab construction, drainage, and waterproofing

Your contractor should pull the permit in their own name as the registered contractor. If a contractor suggests you pull the permit as the homeowner — sometimes framed as saving time or fees — decline. When you pull the permit yourself, you take on the liability as the general contractor. If something goes wrong structurally or if a worker is injured on site, that liability lands on you, not on the contractor.

Inspections are mandatory at several stages. Your contractor must coordinate these and pass before proceeding to the next phase. A contractor who does not coordinate inspections diligently is creating risk for you: uninspected structural work can require destructive investigation if problems are later suspected, and unpermitted work must be disclosed on sale.

Living in the house during underpinning

Homeowners frequently ask whether they can stay in the house while the work is underway. The honest answer is: usually yes, but with significant disruption, and it depends on your household situation.

Underpinning involves jackhammering, concrete breaking, hand digging, and multiple concrete pours. The noise during demolition and excavation phases is substantial. Dust management is possible with proper containment but never perfect — concrete dust penetrates gaps and spreads. The main floor of the house remains accessible throughout, but the basement is completely inaccessible for the duration of the structural work.

Households with young children or anyone with respiratory sensitivities often choose to vacate for at least the demolition and active excavation phases. Households without those constraints often manage the disruption by treating the basement as simply not part of the home for a few months. There is no structural reason you cannot stay in the upper floors; the work is designed to maintain the structural integrity of the home throughout.

Is underpinning worth it in Toronto?

The question of return on investment depends heavily on what you intend to do with the lowered space. We see three common scenarios in Toronto:

  • Creating a legal basement apartment. In neighbourhoods where a two-bedroom lower-level suite commands $2,200 to $2,800 per month in rent, the combined cost of underpinning and finishing ($140,000 to $200,000 all-in) can yield a payback period of six to nine years on the rental income alone — in addition to the value added to the property. For many owner-occupied Toronto homes, this is the strongest financial case for underpinning.
  • Improving livability for personal use. Converting an unusable 6-foot basement into a proper family room, guest suite, or home office is harder to quantify on a spreadsheet, but it meaningfully increases the functional square footage of the home. In the Toronto resale market, buyers discount unfinished or low-ceiling basements significantly. A properly finished, eight-foot-ceiling basement typically returns 60 to 80 cents on the dollar at sale.
  • Underpinning alongside an addition or full renovation. When underpinning is part of a larger project — a rear addition, a full gut renovation, or a whole-home upgrade — the mobilization costs are shared and the disruption is consolidated. This is usually the most cost-efficient context in which to underpin.

The weakest case for underpinning is when it is done in isolation purely as a speculative improvement without a clear plan for the finished space. In that scenario, you carry the capital cost and the disruption without a clear return until a future project completes the vision.

Choosing the right contractor for underpinning

Underpinning is structural work. The margin for error is smaller than in finish renovation, and the consequences of mistakes are larger. There are a few things to look for specifically when evaluating contractors for a basement lowering:

  • Direct underpinning experience. Ask how many underpinning projects the contractor has completed in Toronto specifically. Soil conditions, foundation types, and permit processes in Toronto are particular. A contractor with extensive experience in a different market is not the same as one who has done 50 projects in the Annex and East York.
  • Their engineer relationship. The best underpinning contractors have established relationships with structural engineers and can facilitate that engagement efficiently. Be cautious of contractors who ask you to find your own engineer, or who seem unfamiliar with what the engineer's drawings need to contain.
  • References from comparable projects. Ask for references from projects that involved the same method (pit underpinning or bench footing) on a similar foundation type. Walk through a completed project if you can. You want to see the quality of the new floor pour, the sump pit installation, and the transitions at the existing wall.
  • Their inspection record. Ask the contractor whether their projects pass inspections on first review as a general rule. A contractor who is consistently requiring re-inspection or who does not coordinate inspections proactively is creating risk and delay for your project.
  • Transparency about the sequence. A competent underpinning contractor should be able to walk you through the pin sequence, explain how they will manage the concrete curing schedule, and describe how they will handle soil removal for your specific site. Vague answers to specific questions are a signal.

Conclusion: underpinning done right is a lasting investment

Basement lowering is one of the more expensive and disruptive projects a Toronto homeowner can undertake, but it is also one of the most structurally permanent improvements you can make to a property. A properly designed, permitted, and executed underpinning will last the life of the building. It expands what is possible in your home — legally, functionally, and financially.

The key variables are straightforward: get the engineering done properly, get the permit, choose a contractor with documented experience in Toronto underpinning, and budget realistically for the full scope including waterproofing and the eventual finish. Shortcuts in any of those areas create problems that are expensive and disruptive to remedy.
