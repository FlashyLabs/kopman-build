Renovating a heritage home in Toronto: what it takes

Heritage-designated properties in Toronto come with specific rules. Here's what they mean in practice and how to navigate them.

What heritage designation actually means

Toronto has one of Canada's largest concentrations of heritage-protected residential properties — but "heritage" is not a single uniform status. There are three distinct levels of protection in the city, each carrying different obligations and different implications for what you can and cannot do without approval.

Listed on the Heritage Register (non-designated)

Properties on Toronto's Heritage Register without a formal designation designation are "listed" properties. Listing provides some procedural protections — notably, the City must be given 60 days notice before demolition — but it does not require a Heritage Permit for alterations. Renovations can generally proceed with a standard building permit, though the property may come under more scrutiny if a demolition is ever proposed. Listing does not restrict what you do to the interior, and it does not require material-matching for exterior work, though Heritage Planning staff may comment during building permit review.

Individually designated under Part IV of the Ontario Heritage Act

A Part IV individual designation is the strongest form of heritage protection for a single property. City Council passes a designation bylaw identifying the property's specific "heritage attributes" — typically the street-facing facades, roofline, window and door openings, original materials, and significant architectural details. Any alteration to a designated heritage attribute requires a Heritage Permit, which must be issued before a building permit can follow. Demolition of a Part IV designated property requires City Council approval, and appeals can delay demolition effectively indefinitely. Part IV properties are subject to the highest level of scrutiny from Heritage Planning staff.

Within a Heritage Conservation District under Part V

Heritage Conservation Districts (HCDs) apply heritage rules at the neighbourhood scale rather than the individual property level. Toronto has over 20 designated HCDs, each governed by an HCD Plan that specifies what work requires a Heritage Permit, what materials are acceptable, and what design guidelines apply. An individual property within an HCD may not be individually designated, but it is still subject to HCD rules for exterior work that affects the character of the district. In some HCDs, even fence replacement or front yard landscaping changes require Heritage Planning review.

Toronto neighbourhoods with significant heritage stock

Heritage-protected properties and HCDs are concentrated in some of Toronto's most desirable residential neighbourhoods. If you own or are considering buying in any of these areas, understand the heritage status of the specific property before you plan any exterior work:

  • Rosedale — mix of Part IV designated properties and the South Rosedale HCD
  • Forest Hill — significant stock of 1920s–1940s houses, several Part IV designations
  • The Annex — large concentration of late-Victorian and Edwardian homes; many Part IV designated
  • Cabbagetown — one of Canada's largest concentrations of preserved Victorian housing; within the Cabbagetown HCD
  • Wychwood Park — a designated HCD with some of Toronto's most significant Arts and Crafts architecture
  • Parkdale — Victorian and Edwardian row housing; increasing designation activity
  • Old Town / St. Lawrence — some of the city's oldest surviving residential fabric
  • Bickford Park and Palmerston–Little Italy — dense Edwardian stock, many recently listed or designated

What requires a Heritage Permit

On a Part IV designated property, a Heritage Permit is required for any alteration to the heritage attributes listed in the designation bylaw. In practice, this typically means:

  • Any change to the street-facing facades, including window or door replacement, addition of openings, or removal of original details
  • Changes to the roofline visible from the public realm, including dormers, skylights, and chimney alterations
  • New additions or structures that are visible from a public street or lane
  • Replacement of original cladding materials (brick, stone, wood siding) with different materials
  • Demolition of any part of the designated property, even partial demolition

Within an HCD, the specific Heritage Permit trigger list varies by district — you need to read the HCD Plan for the specific neighbourhood. Some HCDs require Heritage Permits for rear additions, some only for front facade changes. Heritage Planning staff can advise.

The critical procedural point: the Heritage Permit must be issued before the building permit can be issued. You cannot short-circuit the sequence by starting construction under a building permit and obtaining heritage approval later. Work without a required Heritage Permit is illegal and the consequences are serious.

What typically does not require Heritage approval

Not everything on a heritage property triggers the Heritage Permit process. In most cases, the following work can proceed with a standard building permit or no permit at all:

  • Interior renovations of any scope — kitchens, bathrooms, basement finishing, structural work inside the envelope — do not require Heritage Permits
  • Like-for-like repairs using matching materials: replacing rotted wood siding with identical wood siding, repointing brick with matching mortar, replacing a window with an identical window in the same opening
  • Rear yard work not visible from any public street or lane (on many, though not all, Part IV properties)
  • Mechanical systems upgrades — HVAC, electrical panel upgrades, plumbing — that do not alter heritage attributes

That said, the definition of "like-for-like" is sometimes contested, and it is always better to confirm with Heritage Planning staff before assuming work is exempt. A pre-application consultation costs nothing and can save significant time and money.

Before starting any work on a heritage property in Toronto, search the Heritage Register at toronto.ca/heritage. Work on a Part IV designated property without the required Heritage Permit can result in a stop-work order and mandatory restoration at the owner's expense — which can be substantial. Restoration orders have required owners to rebuild demolished features using original materials at costs far exceeding what the original renovation would have cost.

Working with Toronto Heritage Planning

Heritage Planning staff at the City of Toronto are not adversaries. Their mandate is to ensure that significant heritage attributes are preserved while allowing property owners to maintain and update their buildings. Early, proactive engagement with Heritage Planning almost always produces better outcomes than trying to navigate approval after designs are finalized.

Pre-application consultation is free and available to any property owner or their representative. Bring your preliminary design concept and a clear description of the work you want to do. Heritage Planning staff will tell you what's likely approvable, what materials they'd expect, and what the approval process will look like. This conversation early in the design process is far more valuable than the same conversation after you've paid for a full set of drawings.

Heritage Permit review timelines vary: straightforward applications for routine work may be approved by Heritage Planning staff under delegated authority in 30–45 days. Applications requiring a report to Toronto City Council Heritage Committee — typically for significant alterations, additions, or properties with complex heritage status — can take 90–120 days or longer. Factor this into your project schedule.

When Heritage Planning issues a Heritage Permit, it typically includes conditions specifying acceptable materials, methods, and in some cases details like window profiles and mortar composition. These conditions are binding. Your contractor must be able to work within them.

The physical realities of older Toronto homes

Heritage-designated homes in Toronto are almost always old homes — typically pre-1940, often pre-1920. The designation protects the architectural character, but it does not freeze the building systems in amber. Inside the heritage envelope, virtually every project involves confronting the accumulated deferred maintenance and obsolescence of a century or more of occupation.

Knob-and-tube wiring

Pre-1940 homes in Toronto almost universally have knob-and-tube wiring as their original electrical system. Most home insurers will not provide coverage — or will not renew coverage — for properties with active knob-and-tube, and replacement is effectively mandatory for any renovation that touches the walls. A full rewire of a large Toronto Victorian or Edwardian home costs $18,000–$40,000 and should be treated as a baseline assumption, not an unexpected add.

Galvanized steel plumbing

Galvanized steel supply piping corrodes from the inside out over decades, progressively restricting flow until water pressure at fixtures becomes problematic and the pipe eventually fails. Homes built before approximately 1960 often have galvanized supply piping. Replacement with copper or PEX is straightforward but requires opening walls — which, in a heritage renovation, must be done with care to avoid unnecessary damage to original plaster and lath.

Horse-hair plaster

Original horse-hair plaster over wood lath is both a treasure and a challenge. It is acoustically superior to modern drywall, has excellent fire resistance, and when intact contributes to the character of a heritage interior. But it is also fragile — vibration from adjacent work, moisture incursion, or failing lath keys causes it to crack and detach. Specialist patching with lime-based plaster compounds is available and appropriate for repairs. Large-area replacement of failed plaster with drywall is often the more practical solution in areas that are being opened for mechanical work, but it changes the wall profile and texture. Your designer and contractor should have a clear approach to plaster before work begins.

Old-growth timber framing

Pre-1940 Toronto homes were framed with old-growth Douglas fir, hemlock, or pine — timber far denser and more dimensionally stable than modern lumber. This framing is often in excellent condition after a century of service, and it can carry loads that would be impossible to achieve with equivalent-dimension modern lumber. Structural engineers familiar with heritage construction know how to work with it. It requires respect and careful cutting rather than wholesale replacement.

Lead paint

Lead-based paint was used in residential construction until it was banned in Canada in 1976. Any home built before 1978 should be assumed to have lead paint until tested otherwise. For renovations that disturb painted surfaces — sanding, cutting, demolition of painted components — lead abatement procedures are required to protect workers and occupants. In a heritage renovation where original paint may be a heritage attribute, consult both Heritage Planning and an environmental consultant before proceeding.

What to look for in a contractor

The phrase "I've worked on old houses" is not a qualification for heritage renovation work. What you need is a contractor who has specifically submitted Heritage Permit applications and constructed approved alterations on designated properties — someone who knows how to read a Heritage Permit's conditions of approval and translate them into construction practices, and who has relationships with the specialty trades (plaster specialists, heritage masonry contractors, custom millwork shops) that old-house work requires.

Ask for examples of completed heritage projects. Ask whether the contractor has worked with Toronto Heritage Planning on applications, and what the outcomes were. Ask who does their masonry pointing, their plaster repairs, and their custom window replication if required. The answers will tell you quickly whether you're dealing with someone who understands this category of work.
