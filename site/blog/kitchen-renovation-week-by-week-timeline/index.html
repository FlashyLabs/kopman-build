Kitchen renovation timeline week by week

A Toronto contractor's honest guide to what happens when, how long each phase actually takes, and where projects lose time — from permit application to final walkthrough.

Why the timeline question is harder than it looks

Every client asks some version of the same question: how long will this take? The honest answer is that a kitchen renovation timeline depends on three things that are almost never the same across two projects: the scope of work, the lead times on your selected materials, and what your contractor finds once demolition begins. In 25 years of doing this in Toronto, we have never had two kitchen renovations that ran on identical schedules.

That said, there are real patterns. A cosmetic kitchen refresh — new cabinet doors, countertops, backsplash, and paint, with no changes to plumbing or electrical layout — can be done in two to three weeks of active construction. A mid-range full gut renovation with new cabinets, relocated plumbing and electrical, new flooring, and a modest layout adjustment typically runs six to nine weeks of active construction. A high-end renovation that involves structural changes, custom millwork, appliance relocation, and premium long-lead materials can easily run twelve to sixteen weeks, with several additional weeks of pre-construction planning and permit processing before a single wall comes down.

The biggest source of timeline confusion is the difference between elapsed time and active construction time. A renovation that takes eight weeks to build can take five months from the day you sign a contract to the day you are cooking in the finished kitchen. Material procurement, permit processing, and scheduling of subcontractors all happen in between. This guide breaks down both: what happens when the crew is on site, and what the realistic elapsed schedule looks like from start to finish.

Before construction begins: the pre-construction phase

Most of the decisions that determine whether your kitchen renovation goes smoothly are made before anyone picks up a sledgehammer. The pre-construction phase is where planning, design, permitting, and material procurement happen — and it is the phase homeowners most often underestimate in both time and importance.

Design and scope finalization (Weeks 1–3)

If you are working with a contractor who offers design-build services, design and scope development happen together. If you are bringing in an independent kitchen designer, this step may begin independently before you hire a contractor. Either way, you need a finalized floor plan, an agreed scope of work, and specified materials before a permit application can be submitted and before accurate cabinet orders can be placed.

Rushing this phase is a mistake that costs you later. Every change to the design after cabinets are ordered, or after a permit is issued, creates delays and often additional costs. We spend more time here than clients sometimes expect, because the cost of a good decision before construction is a few hours; the cost of the same decision during construction can be thousands of dollars and weeks of delay.

Permit application and approval (Weeks 2–6)

In Toronto, a kitchen renovation that involves relocating plumbing, moving gas lines, or making changes to electrical panels requires permits through the City of Toronto Building Division. A project that is purely cosmetic — replacing surfaces without changing the location of fixtures or services — generally does not. Your contractor should be able to tell you clearly what permits your project requires and pull them in their own name.

The City of Toronto's building permit processing time for residential renovation projects currently runs two to four weeks for straightforward applications submitted online. Projects that require zoning review, heritage review, or that fall under the jurisdiction of the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority can take considerably longer. Budget four to six weeks from application submission to permit issuance for a typical full kitchen renovation, and start the application as early in the design process as possible.

Cabinet and material procurement (Weeks 3–10)

This is where many kitchen renovation timelines get extended, and where the quality of pre-construction planning shows. Stock cabinets from a big-box retailer can be available within days. Semi-custom cabinets from a Canadian manufacturer typically run four to eight weeks. Fully custom cabinetry from a local Toronto millwork shop runs eight to fourteen weeks and sometimes longer for complex projects. Stone countertops require template fabrication after cabinets are installed, then typically two to three weeks for fabrication and delivery.

Other materials with meaningful lead times that need to be confirmed before you set a construction start date:

  • Specialty tile and backsplash materials: In-stock tile is available immediately; imported or specialty tile can take four to ten weeks depending on origin
  • Appliances: Standard in-stock appliances can be delivered within a week; specific models, panel-ready appliances for integrated looks, or commercial-grade ranges can take six to sixteen weeks
  • Plumbing fixtures: Most standard fixtures are available within one to two weeks; designer or European brands may require four to eight weeks
  • Windows and skylights: If your kitchen renovation includes new or enlarged windows, budget six to ten weeks for custom-sized units

The right time to finalize all material selections is before construction begins — not during it. A contractor who starts demolition before cabinets are confirmed is setting up a delay.

The rule we use on every project: We do not set a construction start date until cabinets are confirmed with a delivery date, appliances are ordered, and permits are either in hand or approved with a known issuance date. Skipping this discipline costs more time than it saves.

The timeline below reflects a mid-range full kitchen renovation in a Toronto home: full gut with new layout, relocated electrical and plumbing, semi-custom cabinetry, stone countertops, and new flooring. The scope assumes a kitchen in the range of 120–200 square feet. Simpler scopes will compress some phases; more complex work will extend them.

What renovation scope does to the schedule

The table above represents a full gut renovation. Not every project touches every phase. Understanding how scope changes the timeline helps you make informed decisions about what to include and what to defer.

Renovation scope Active construction time Typical total elapsed time Approximate cost range (Toronto)
Cosmetic refresh (surfaces only, no layout changes) 2–3 weeks 6–10 weeks $25,000–$55,000
Mid-range full renovation (new cabinets, same layout) 6–8 weeks 14–20 weeks $60,000–$110,000
Full gut with layout changes (plumbing & electrical relocation) 8–10 weeks 16–24 weeks $100,000–$175,000

All cost figures above are in Canadian dollars and reflect Toronto market pricing as of 2026. Costs vary meaningfully based on the specific finishes chosen, the age and condition of the home, and whether structural or heritage considerations apply.

The phases where Toronto projects lose the most time

After running hundreds of kitchen renovations across Toronto and the GTA, the delays we see most consistently fall into a predictable set of categories. None of them are inevitable — good planning eliminates most of them — but they are common enough to plan around.

Material decisions made too late

The most frequent cause of avoidable delay is a client who has not finalized material selections when construction begins. If your contractor is installing cabinets and you have not yet chosen your countertop material, you cannot template until you decide, you cannot fabricate until you template, and your kitchen sits unusable for three weeks while you deliberate over quartz versus quartzite. All material selections should be finalized, ideally with deposits placed, before your construction start date.

Discoveries during demolition

Toronto's housing stock includes a large proportion of homes built between 1900 and 1970, and those homes regularly contain surprises behind the walls. The most common issues we encounter during kitchen demolitions:

  • Moisture damage and mould: Particularly around sinks, dishwashers, and exterior walls. Remediation is not optional and adds one to three weeks depending on extent
  • Asbestos-containing materials: Floor tile, drywall compound, and pipe insulation in homes built before 1980 may require abatement, adding one to two weeks and $2,000–$8,000 in cost
  • Knob-and-tube or aluminum wiring: Many Toronto homes built before 1960 have wiring that must be remediated before new circuits can be added; full remediation can add one to two weeks
  • Undersized drain lines: Older cast-iron drain stacks and branch lines may be corroded or undersized for a relocated island sink; re-routing adds three to five days
  • Out-of-level floors and out-of-plumb walls: Cosmetic issues in most cases, but extreme deviations require correction before tile or flooring can be installed properly

A contingency budget of 10–15% of your total project cost is standard practice for this reason. A contractor who does not mention contingency in their proposal is not accounting for the reality of older construction.

Inspection scheduling

The City of Toronto's building inspection process is sequential: rough-in must be inspected and approved before walls can close, and the final inspection must happen before the permit can be closed. When inspectors are busy — as they often are during periods of high construction activity — wait times for inspection appointments can run three to seven business days. Planning your construction schedule to minimize the impact of these waits is part of good project management: rough-in work should be completed on a Thursday or Friday so that an early-week inspection does not leave the crew idle.

Countertop fabrication

Stone countertops are templated after cabinets are installed, then fabricated, then delivered and installed. This is a sequential process that cannot be compressed: you cannot template before cabinets are in, and fabrication takes what it takes. Most Toronto stone fabricators are running two to three week backlogs in spring and summer. If your countertop is on the critical path — and it almost always is — get on the fabricator's schedule the moment cabinet installation is confirmed.

On living through a kitchen renovation: For a full gut renovation, plan to be without a functional kitchen for the full construction period. Set up a temporary kitchen in another room — a microwave, a hot plate, a mini-fridge, and a kettle cover most needs. Budget for eating out more than usual. Most clients underestimate how disruptive kitchen loss is until they are three weeks in.

How to read a contractor's proposed schedule

When you receive a project schedule from a contractor, there are several things to look for that distinguish a realistic plan from an optimistic one.

  • Does the schedule account for permit processing time? If a contractor is promising a start date before a permit has been applied for, they are either planning to start work without a permit or they are being unrealistic about approval timelines. Either is a concern.
  • Are material lead times baked into the schedule? A schedule that shows cabinet installation beginning in week four but does not show when cabinets were ordered is missing critical information. Ask when all major materials will be ordered and what the confirmed lead times are.
  • Is there float built in for inspections? Any honest schedule allows for inspection waiting time at the rough-in and final stages. A schedule with no buffer around inspection milestones is optimistic.
  • Is there a contingency note? A schedule that shows no allowance for the unexpected is not a realistic schedule for a renovation in an older Toronto home. Ask what the plan is if demolition reveals a problem that adds a week.
  • When is substantial completion defined, and how does it differ from final completion? Substantial completion is the point at which you can use the kitchen for its intended purpose; final completion includes all deficiencies resolved. These are not the same date and should not be treated as such in the payment schedule.

The full elapsed timeline: what to plan for

Combining pre-construction planning, permit processing, material procurement, active construction, and post-construction punch list work, here is what a realistic elapsed timeline looks like for a full kitchen renovation in Toronto, from initial contractor meeting to project close-out:

  • Weeks 1–3: Scope and design finalization; contractor agreement signed
  • Weeks 2–4: Permit application submitted; cabinet order placed; appliances ordered
  • Weeks 4–8: Permit approval; specialty materials procured; cabinet delivery confirmed
  • Weeks 8–18: Active construction (10 weeks for a full gut renovation as outlined above)
  • Weeks 18–20: Punch list, final inspection, permit close-out, statutory holdback release

That is a five-month elapsed timeline from first meeting to project completion for a mid-to-high-range full kitchen renovation. Clients who are planning around a holiday gathering, a home sale, or another fixed deadline need to work backward from that date and plan accordingly. We typically recommend starting the contractor selection process at least six months before you want to be cooking in a finished kitchen.

What you can do to keep your project on schedule

The client has more influence over the schedule than most people realize. The decisions and behaviours that keep projects on track are consistent across every project we have run:

  • Make all material selections before construction begins. Every undecided item on your specification list is a potential delay. If you cannot decide between two tile options, decide before the crew is on site — not while they are waiting to start the backsplash.
  • Respond to contractor communications quickly. Contractors manage many active decisions during construction. A question that requires your approval — a change order, a substitution for an out-of-stock item, a structural option — that sits unanswered for three days can translate directly into a schedule slip.
  • Do not change the scope once construction has begun. Scope changes during active construction are the single largest cause of cost overruns and schedule extensions. If you want to add the island, add it during design. Adding it during cabinet installation is a different conversation entirely.
  • Visit the site regularly, not sporadically. Regular walkthroughs during construction — weekly is ideal — allow you to catch issues before they are buried in the wall or compounded by subsequent work. Discovering a concern at substantial completion is always more expensive than discovering it at rough-in.
  • Have contingency budget available and accessible. When discoveries happen that require extra work, the fastest path through them is an owner who can approve a change order promptly. Projects where the owner needs two weeks to deliberate over a $4,000 change lose more time than the change order itself requires.

Conclusion: plan the timeline, not just the design

A kitchen renovation is one of the most complex projects in residential construction — it involves more trades, more sequential dependencies, and more long-lead materials than almost any other room. The homeowners who have the best experiences are the ones who understand the timeline before they start, build their planning around it, and give themselves enough time to make good decisions without pressure.

The homeowners who have difficult experiences are almost always the ones who started the process too late for their target date, rushed material selections, or hired a contractor who started work before the plan was solid. The stress of a kitchen renovation is not inherent to the process; it is almost always the result of timeline compression.
