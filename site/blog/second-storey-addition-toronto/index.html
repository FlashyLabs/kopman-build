Second storey addition in Toronto: the complete guide

How to double your living space without moving — costs, process, and what to expect when you add a second storey in Toronto.

Why second-storey additions are Toronto's most powerful renovation

Toronto has a vast stock of bungalows built during the postwar housing boom of the 1940s through 1960s. Drive through North York, Etobicoke, East York, and Scarborough and you'll see them on nearly every block: compact, well-sited homes on generous lots, designed for a different era's idea of how much space a family needed. Many are now occupied by families who need three or four bedrooms and two full bathrooms where the original design provided two bedrooms and one.

The arithmetic of adding a second storey to a Toronto bungalow is compelling. A 900 sq ft bungalow becomes a 1,700–1,800 sq ft two-storey home without purchasing additional land — which in Toronto costs far more per square foot than construction does. The result is a home that can accommodate a modern family's life and commands a substantially higher market value than the original bungalow.

Moving to a larger home in the same neighbourhood involves paying a significant premium for that larger footprint — plus the transaction costs of the sale and purchase, which run 5–7% of the purchase price. A second-storey addition sidesteps all of that. Done well, it is one of the highest-return investments a Toronto homeowner can make.

Feasibility: what must be assessed before committing

A second-storey addition is not appropriate for every bungalow. Before a design is developed, a structural engineer must assess four critical factors:

Foundation capacity

The foundation of a 1950s Toronto bungalow was designed to support one storey of structure above it. Adding a second storey doubles (or more than doubles) the gravity load on the foundation walls and footings. Many bungalow foundations in Toronto cannot support this additional load without intervention. The structural engineer's assessment will determine whether underpinning — excavating beneath the existing footings and extending them to competent bearing material — is required, or whether the less invasive technique of bench-pinning is sufficient. Do not proceed with a second-storey project without this assessment. It is not possible to estimate the project accurately without knowing whether underpinning is required, as it can add $40,000–$120,000 to the cost.

Ground floor load-bearing wall layout

The second storey floor structure needs to bear its load on walls that transfer it to the foundation. The layout of load-bearing walls on the ground floor determines what the second floor plan can look like. If the existing bungalow has open-plan ground floor spaces with long spans unsupported by interior walls, the structural solution for the second floor may require beams that affect ceiling heights below. The structural engineer will resolve this, but homeowners should understand that the existing layout constrains — and in some cases enables — the design options above.

Zoning height limits and lot coverage

Most Toronto residential zones permit buildings up to 9 or 10 metres in height measured from grade to the mid-point of the roof. A two-storey structure on a typical Toronto bungalow footprint will usually meet this limit, but the specific property must be checked. If the proposed addition exceeds the as-of-right height limit, a minor variance from the Committee of Adjustment is required before the building permit can be issued — adding 4–6 months to the front end of the project. A zoning review is a day's work for an experienced designer and should happen before detailed design begins.

Heritage constraints

If the property is listed on Toronto's Heritage Register, individually designated under Part IV of the Ontario Heritage Act, or within a Heritage Conservation District, additional approval requirements apply. Exterior alterations — including the addition of a second storey — on designated properties require a Heritage Permit before a building permit can follow. See our detailed guide on heritage home renovations in Toronto for the full picture.

Luxury finish specifications, complex structural conditions, or heritage constraints will push costs beyond these ranges.

Total cost and per-square-foot benchmarks

All-in cost for a full second-storey addition on a Toronto bungalow typically runs $350,000–$650,000, depending on the scope of structural work and the finish level chosen. On a per-square-foot basis, expect $350–$550 per square foot for the addition itself, not including foundation underpinning if required. If underpinning is needed, add $40,000–$120,000 to any estimate.

The reason for the wide range is real, not evasive. A project without underpinning, with new vinyl siding on the exterior and standard builder finishes inside, lands near the low end. A project requiring full perimeter underpinning, with brick-matching exterior and a primary bathroom with radiant floors, bespoke tile, and custom vanity, lands at or above the high end. Both are legitimate outcomes of a second-storey addition — they serve different households and different resale strategies.

The permit process

A building permit from the City of Toronto Building Department is required for every second-storey addition. The application must include architectural drawings prepared by a licensed designer (architect or BCIN holder), structural drawings stamped by a licensed professional engineer, and energy compliance documentation demonstrating that the new construction meets the Ontario Building Code's thermal envelope requirements.

If the proposed structure exceeds the as-of-right height limit for the zoning classification — most often because the ridge height pushes above 9 or 10 metres — a minor variance from the Committee of Adjustment is required. CoA applications are heard roughly monthly and decisions can be appealed to the Ontario Land Tribunal. Budget 4–6 months for a straightforward CoA approval; appeals can add significantly more.

Without a variance requirement, permit processing at the City of Toronto Building Department currently runs 4–8 months from a complete application submission to permit issuance. Submitting a complete, well-organized application reduces the risk of revision requests that extend this timeline.

Construction timeline and sequencing

A second-storey addition has a specific construction logic that differs from most other renovation types. Understanding the sequence helps homeowners prepare for what they will experience.

  • Structural assessment and design: 2–3 months. The structural engineer assesses the foundation and designs the new floor and roof structure. This work happens during the permit application period and does not extend the overall timeline if it starts promptly.
  • Permit: 4–8 months. Design and permit phases overlap with the structural assessment. The key is starting design and permit submission as early as possible.
  • Structural preparation and any underpinning: 4–10 weeks. Foundation underpinning, if required, happens before the roof is removed. It is disruptive but the home remains habitable.
  • Roof removal and new second-floor framing: 3–4 weeks. The most dramatic phase. The existing roof is removed, the second-floor structure is installed, and the new roof goes on. Weather protection is installed the same day the existing roof comes off. Speed matters here — this is the most weather-sensitive phase of the entire project.
  • Rough-in mechanical, electrical, and plumbing: 3–4 weeks. Once the structure is weather-tight, the rough-in trades work through the framing. Inspection signoffs at multiple stages are required before proceeding.
  • Exterior: 3–5 weeks. Cladding, windows, and exterior trim are installed while rough-ins proceed inside.
  • Interior finishes: 8–14 weeks. Drywall, flooring, tile, trim, cabinetry, fixtures, and painting. This phase has the most variability — complex bathrooms and custom millwork extend it.
  • Total construction: 5–8 months from structural preparation to occupancy.

End-to-end from first design meeting to moving into the completed home: plan for 14–20 months. Projects that start design and permit early, have clear scope, and have no CoA requirement come in at the lower end of this range.

Living through a second-storey addition

Most families stay in their home through the majority of a second-storey addition. The structural preparation phase, including underpinning, is noisy and dusty but the home is livable. The rough-in and finishing phases are similarly manageable with basic dust containment measures in place.

The exception is the roof removal phase, which typically runs 1–2 weeks. During this window, the home is not habitable — weather protection covers the structure but the interior is exposed to dust, noise, and variable temperature. Families almost universally rent short-term accommodation (an Airbnb, a family member's home) for this window. The cost is real but manageable: budget $2,000–$6,000 for 2–4 weeks of alternative accommodation.

Once the new structure is weather-tight — typically 2–3 weeks after roof removal begins — most families return to the ground floor while the second-floor construction continues above them. The balance of the project is loud at times and generates significant dust, but it is livable with proper containment and a cooperative family.

The question every homeowner should ask their contractor

Second-storey additions on Toronto bungalows are genuinely specialist work. The structural integration of old and new, the management of the weather-critical roof removal sequence, and the coordination of the multiple trades involved require a contractor who has done this specific type of project — not just one who has done "lots of renovations."

Ask any contractor you're considering: Have you completed second-storey additions specifically on Toronto bungalows? Can you show me completed projects, including before-and-after photographs? Can I speak with one or two past clients whose projects were similar to mine? A contractor with real experience in this category will answer these questions without hesitation and with specifics. One who cannot should be disqualified regardless of how competitive their price is.
