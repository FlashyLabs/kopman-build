Bathroom tile selection guide for Toronto renovations

Material types, cost ranges, layout patterns, and the installation details that actually determine how long your tile lasts — from a contractor who has tiled a few hundred bathrooms in this city.

Why tile decisions matter more than they look

Bathroom tile is one of those renovation decisions where the showroom experience and the lived experience can diverge dramatically. In the showroom, everything is pristine, well-lit, and beautifully laid. In your bathroom five years from now, what matters is whether the grout is cracking along the shower curb, whether the floor tile has chipped at the threshold, and whether water has been silently working its way behind the wall because the waterproofing was compromised before the tile went down.

The tile itself is only part of the story. The substrate it sits on, the membrane system that protects the structure behind it, the grout type, and the skill of the installer all matter as much as which tile you picked from the sample board. We have seen $80-per-square-foot Italian porcelain fail within three years because the installation was wrong, and we have seen $12-per-square-foot ceramic hold up perfectly for twenty years because the prep work was done properly.

That said, tile selection is genuinely consequential. Different materials have meaningfully different performance characteristics, maintenance requirements, and cost profiles. The purpose of this guide is to give you an honest picture of the options, so that you can make decisions that hold up — not just decisions that look good on the day the bathroom is handed over.

Understanding tile material types

The Toronto market has access to an enormous range of tile materials. These are the ones we work with regularly, with an honest assessment of where each makes sense.

Ceramic tile

Ceramic is the workhorse of bathroom renovation. It is made from clay fired at relatively low temperatures, which makes it less dense than porcelain but also easier to cut and handle on site. In a bathroom context, ceramic is well-suited to wall applications: shower surrounds, tub decks, and accent walls. It is generally not recommended for high-traffic floors because its surface is softer and will show wear over time, particularly in a family bathroom used daily.

The cost advantage is real. Quality ceramic wall tile in Toronto typically runs $4 to $18 per square foot for the material, making it the most accessible option for budget-conscious renovations without requiring a sacrifice in appearance. The range of formats, colours, and textures available in ceramic is extensive, and domestic and Italian options are both well-represented at Toronto tile suppliers including Centura, Olympia, and Paris Ceramics.

Porcelain tile

Porcelain is fired at higher temperatures than ceramic, which produces a denser, less porous tile that is harder, more water-resistant, and more durable under foot traffic. For bathroom floors, porcelain is the standard choice among contractors who prioritize longevity over upfront cost. Its hardness also makes it a better choice for heated floor systems, where thermal cycling over years can stress softer materials.

Modern large-format porcelain — 24x24, 24x48, and even larger slabs — has become increasingly popular in Toronto bathroom renovations, particularly in high-end projects where homeowners want a seamless, low-grout-line look. These formats require a perfectly flat substrate and are unforgiving of deflection in the floor structure, so they demand more preparation work and sometimes floor reinforcement in older homes.

Porcelain tile runs $8 to $45 per square foot for standard formats, with large-format slabs and specialty finishes running higher. Labour costs for large-format tile are also meaningfully higher than for standard sizes due to the handling, substrate requirements, and cutting complexity involved.

Natural stone: marble, travertine, and slate

Natural stone tile brings a warmth and character that is genuinely difficult to replicate with manufactured products. Marble, in particular, has had a sustained moment in Toronto's higher-end renovation market, showing up in primary bathrooms across Rosedale, Forest Hill, and the Annex. It photographs beautifully and ages gracefully when properly maintained.

The trade-offs are real, though. Marble is porous and requires sealing on installation and periodically thereafter. In a shower, acidic products like shampoo and body wash will etch an unsealed marble surface over time. Travertine, which has a distinctive pitted texture, requires filling and sealing. Slate is more durable and slip-resistant but limited in colour range. None of these materials are maintenance-free in the way that porcelain is.

Natural stone tile generally runs $15 to $80 per square foot for material, with installation costs that are higher than ceramic or standard porcelain due to the care required in cutting, sealing, and handling. Budget for ongoing maintenance as well.

Glass tile

Glass tile is almost exclusively a wall and accent material in residential bathrooms. It reflects light beautifully, is non-porous, and does not stain or harbour mildew in the way that grout-heavy surfaces can. The limitations are also well-known: glass tile chips if struck, cannot be used on floors, and requires careful installation because any adhesive inconsistency or air pocket behind the tile will be visible through the face.

Glass tile is most effective as an accent strip, a niche liner, or a feature wall in a shower rather than as a primary wall covering. Material costs run $20 to $60 per square foot for quality glass tile, and skilled installation is essential — a rushed glass tile job is immediately apparent.

On the porcelain-that-looks-like-stone trend: Rectified porcelain tiles that convincingly replicate marble, wood grain, or concrete have improved dramatically in the last five years. For clients who want the aesthetic of natural stone without the maintenance overhead, these have become our most-recommended option in primary bathrooms. The visual difference from a good vein-matched porcelain slab versus actual marble is minimal to most observers, and the long-term performance is far better in a wet environment.

Tile formats, patterns, and layout

The format of the tile — its size and shape — and the pattern in which it is laid have as much visual impact as the material itself. A few principles that hold up in practice:

Size and proportion

Larger format tiles make a small bathroom feel larger by reducing the number of grout lines in the visual field. A 12x24 or 24x24 tile on the floor of a 5x8 bathroom will read very differently than 4x4 mosaic, even if the material and colour are similar. This is a reliable principle, but it has a limit: a single very large tile in a tiny bathroom can look awkward. The sweet spot for most Toronto bathroom floors is 12x24 or 18x18.

For shower walls, 4x16 subway format (the modern elongated version of the classic 3x6) remains the most popular choice in our projects and shows no sign of declining. It is proportionally versatile, works in both contemporary and traditional settings, and is available in a wide price range. 12x24 wall tiles are also common in more contemporary bathrooms.

Layout patterns

The pattern affects both the visual outcome and the installation labour cost. Common patterns and their practical implications:

  • Running bond (offset / brick pattern): The classic subway tile pattern. A 1/3 offset is the most stable for elongated tiles and avoids the lippage problems that a 50% offset can create with larger formats. For tiles 15 inches or longer, most manufacturers recommend a maximum 33% offset. Simple to explain, straightforward to install.
  • Stack bond (straight set): Tiles aligned in both directions with continuous grout lines. Very contemporary in feel, particularly with a minimal grout joint. Requires precise substrate prep because any variation in flatness is immediately visible with perfectly aligned joints.
  • Herringbone: An angled pattern that adds visual energy to a feature wall or a small floor area. More cutting waste (factor in 15% overage rather than the standard 10%), and more installation time. Works best with smaller format tiles — 3x12 or 2x8 — rather than large slabs.
  • Diagonal (45-degree set): Turns a square tile to 45 degrees. Makes a floor feel larger and adds visual interest to an otherwise plain layout. More cutting waste at walls and thresholds; budget 15% overage.
  • Basketweave and encaustic patterns: Decorative mosaic patterns popular in period renovations in older Toronto neighbourhoods like Cabbagetown, Leslieville, and The Annex. Typically used for floors in small bathrooms or as inset feature areas. Installation is labour-intensive; budget accordingly.

Grout: the detail everyone underestimates

Grout selection is treated as an afterthought by most homeowners and as a critical decision by experienced contractors. The wrong grout choice — or the wrong grout maintenance plan — is responsible for more bathroom tile failures than any other single factor.

Sanded versus unsanded grout

Sanded grout contains fine aggregate and is used for joints wider than 3mm (approximately 1/8 inch). Unsanded grout is used for narrower joints, particularly with polished stone where sanded grout would scratch the surface. For most bathroom floor applications with a standard 3mm joint, sanded grout is appropriate. For tightly-jointed wall applications, unsanded. Using the wrong type — which happens more often than it should with inexperienced tile setters — leads to cracking or poor adhesion.

Epoxy grout

Epoxy grout is a two-component product that cures chemically rather than through drying. It is significantly harder, more stain-resistant, and more water-resistant than cement-based grout. In shower floors and areas subject to regular cleaning with bleach-based products, epoxy grout performs dramatically better over time. It is also more expensive to purchase and more difficult and time-consuming to install — expect to pay a premium in labour. For shower floors, we recommend it. For dry areas and simple wall work, standard cement grout with proper sealing is sufficient.

Grout colour

Grout colour is a design decision with practical consequences. Light grout joints in a shower floor will require more maintenance to keep clean. Dark grout in a light wall tile application can look striking initially but may show efflorescence (white mineral deposits) as the grout cures. A grout colour that closely matches the tile is generally the lowest-maintenance choice and also tends to make a space feel larger by making grout lines visually recede.

On grout sealing: Cement-based grout should be sealed after installation and resealed every one to three years depending on shower use. This is not optional maintenance if you want the grout to stay clean and intact. Many homeowners are never told this. We include sealing in our standard tile scope and give clients written maintenance instructions at handover. If your contractor does not do the same, ask them to.

What happens before the tile goes down

The substrate and waterproofing system beneath the tile is invisible once the job is complete, which is exactly why it is where corners get cut. In our experience, the single biggest cause of premature tile failure in Toronto bathrooms is inadequate substrate preparation and waterproofing — not a bad tile selection, not grout failure, but what happened before the tile ever went down.

Substrate requirements

Tile requires a substrate that is flat (within 3mm over 3 metres for standard tile, tighter tolerances for large format), stable, and structurally sound. In most Toronto bathrooms, that means cement board (HardieBacker or equivalent) for wet areas, or a poured mortar bed for shower floors where drainage slope is required. Drywall — even moisture-resistant greenboard — is not an appropriate substrate in wet areas and should not be used in a shower surround regardless of what anyone tells you about waterproofing paint or membranes applied over it.

In older Toronto homes, existing floors often have some degree of deflection or unevenness from decades of settlement. Before large-format tile goes down, we often sister the joists, apply a self-levelling compound, or do both. This adds cost to the project, but it prevents the more expensive outcome of cracked tiles and failed grout joints two years later.

Waterproofing systems

The Toronto market has largely moved to sheet membrane or liquid-applied membrane systems for shower waterproofing over the past decade. Products in the Schluter KERDI system, the Laticrete Hydro Ban family, and similar membranes have replaced traditional hot-mop and tar paper approaches in most quality bathroom renovations. These systems provide a continuous, bonded waterproofing layer behind the tile and are tested to specific performance standards.

For a shower to perform over the long term, the waterproofing must be continuous: floor, walls up to a minimum height above the finished shower pan, all inside corners, and all penetrations including the drain and any niches or recesses. Niches that are not properly waterproofed are a common failure point. A niche behind a shower wall that receives inadequate waterproofing is a water-infiltration path directly into the wall cavity — and the damage can take years to become visible while it is progressing.

Tile selection tips for specific Toronto contexts

After working in Toronto homes for 25 years, a few observations about context-specific selection decisions:

  • Victorian and Edwardian semi-detached homes (Leslieville, Riverdale, Roncesvalles, Trinity-Bellwoods): Period-appropriate choices like encaustic cement tile, small-format hexagon mosaic, or classic subway tile integrate well with the architecture and tend to appeal to buyers if you are planning to sell. Large-format contemporary porcelain can look out of place in a 1910 bathroom even when executed beautifully.
  • Post-war bungalows (Etobicoke, East York, North York): These bathrooms are often being expanded as part of a main-floor renovation. Simpler, larger-format tile selections work well here. Heated floor systems are popular in these projects and pair effectively with porcelain, which tolerates thermal cycling better than stone.
  • Condo bathroom renovations (downtown, midtown): Floor penetrations for in-slab drainage are typically not permitted, which affects drain placement. Tile weight is also a consideration — heavy stone tile in large quantities can be problematic in high-rise construction. Consult your building's guidelines before selecting very heavy materials.
  • Primary bathrooms in Rosedale, Forest Hill, and Bridle Path: Expectations for material quality and installation precision in these neighbourhoods are high. Budget for premium materials, tighter installation tolerances, and the additional time required to achieve clean reveal lines and perfectly executed niches and built-ins.

How to evaluate tile quotes

When comparing tile installation quotes from contractors, the line items to pay attention to are not just the material and labour rates — they are the things that often get omitted from lower bids:

  • Substrate preparation: Is cement board or membrane substrate included, or is the quote assuming the existing surface is usable? In an older Toronto home, assume it probably is not.
  • Waterproofing scope: What waterproofing system is specified, and does it cover the full required area including niches and curbs? A quote that does not specify a waterproofing product is a quote that may not include adequate waterproofing.
  • Material overage: A properly constructed quote will include 10% overage for standard patterns and 15% for diagonal and herringbone. If no overage is included, you will be buying extra tile mid-project, potentially from a different production run that does not match perfectly.
  • Grout sealing: Is grout sealing included in the tile scope, or is it an add-on? It should be standard.
  • Transitions and trim: Schluter RENO profiles, threshold transitions, and tile edge trim are often quoted separately or not at all. These details matter both functionally and aesthetically.

A practical timeline for bathroom tile work

Understanding the sequencing of tile work helps you manage expectations during a bathroom renovation. The tile phase of a bathroom project typically unfolds over two to three weeks within a broader renovation timeline:

  • Days 1–2: Substrate installation (cement board, membrane system, self-levelling compound if required). The substrate must be fully cured before tile adhesive is applied.
  • Days 3–5: Waterproofing membrane installation, including all seams, corners, and penetrations. Inspection and approval before tile proceeds.
  • Days 6–10: Field tile installation — floor first, then walls. Large-format or pattern tile takes longer than standard formats. A typical primary bathroom tile scope takes three to five days for an experienced tile setter working steadily.
  • Day 11: Cure time. Thin-set adhesive requires 24 to 48 hours of cure time before grout is applied. Do not rush this step.
  • Days 12–13: Grouting, cleaning, and initial sealing.
  • Days 14–21: Full cure period before the shower is put into service. Most manufacturers specify a 28-day full cure, though controlled first-use is typically acceptable after 72 hours.

The bottom line

Bathroom tile selection in Toronto is a decision that spans aesthetics, material science, neighbourhood context, budget, and installation quality. The material you choose matters. The installer matters more. And the system beneath the tile — the substrate, the waterproofing, the thin-set, the grout — determines whether your beautiful selection is still beautiful in fifteen years or becomes a remediation project in four.

The most reliable approach we have found over 25 years: select a tile that suits the character of the home and your budget, specify the right substrate and waterproofing system in your contract, and hire an installer whose previous work you have seen in person. The sample board in the showroom is the easiest part of the decision. The rest of it is what this guide is for.
