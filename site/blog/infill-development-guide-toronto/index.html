Infill development in Toronto: a practical guide

What you actually need to know before buying an infill lot, navigating the approvals process, and building a custom home in an established Toronto neighbourhood.

What infill development actually means in Toronto

Infill development refers to the construction of new buildings on vacant or underutilized land within an existing neighbourhood — land that has already been serviced with roads, water, sewer, and utilities. In Toronto, that typically means one of three scenarios: a vacant lot sitting between two existing homes, a deep lot where the rear portion has been severed from a larger property, or a lot where a small or deteriorated house has been demolished to make way for a new build.

This is different from greenfield development, where you are building in a new subdivision on land that has never been developed. Infill lots exist within the fabric of established streets, surrounded by neighbours with opinions about what gets built next door, subject to existing city infrastructure that may or may not have capacity, and governed by zoning rules written decades before the city started looking at intensification as a policy priority.

Interest in infill development has accelerated in Toronto over the past several years, driven by a combination of factors: land supply constraints, the provincial push for intensification under the More Homes Built Faster Act, a rising preference among buyers for newer construction in established neighbourhoods rather than in remote subdivisions, and the straightforward economics of land that is already serviced and centrally located. For the right buyer, an infill lot in Leaside, Bloor West Village, or the Upper Beaches is a compelling opportunity. But the path from lot acquisition to occupancy is more complicated than many first-timers anticipate.

This guide walks through each stage of that path — from assessing a lot before you buy it, through the planning and approvals process, into construction and completion.

Assessing an infill lot before you buy

The single most important thing you can do before purchasing an infill lot is understand what you are actually allowed to build on it. A lot that looks promising on paper may have zoning constraints, service limitations, or site conditions that significantly affect buildable area or cost. A thorough pre-purchase assessment takes no more than two to three weeks and can save you from a very expensive mistake.

Zoning and lot coverage

Pull the zoning for the specific property through the City of Toronto's online zoning map. Most residential infill in Toronto falls under an R, RD, or RS zone designation. Each zone specifies minimum lot frontage, minimum lot area, maximum lot coverage, maximum building height, and setback requirements from the front, rear, and side property lines. These parameters directly determine how large a home you can build and how it must sit on the lot.

In many established Toronto neighbourhoods, the zoning was written to reflect the character of the existing housing stock — which may be significantly smaller and lower than what you want to build. A 30-foot lot in a neighbourhood zoned for a 0.35 floor space index (FSI), for example, will yield a meaningfully smaller footprint than a comparable lot in a zone that permits 0.6 FSI. Understanding these numbers before you negotiate the purchase price is essential.

Lot dimensions and shape

Standard Toronto infill lots vary enormously. A lot severed from a deep property in North York may be 30 feet wide and 120 feet deep; an end-of-row lot in Leslieville might be 18 feet wide and 100 feet deep. Width is particularly constrained by side-yard setback requirements — on a narrow lot, the setbacks can consume enough of your frontage that the buildable envelope becomes very tight.

Irregular lot shapes — pie-shaped lots, flag lots, lots with notched corners — add complexity to design and sometimes to zoning calculations. A surveyor's report should be commissioned before closing on any infill lot so that you know exact dimensions and any encroachments or easements that affect the buildable area.

Services and utilities

A lot within the existing city boundary is generally assumed to have access to water and sewer, but the capacity and condition of those services needs to be confirmed. Some older Toronto streets are served by combined sewers (storm and sanitary in the same pipe), which the city is progressively separating. If your lot requires a service upgrade as a condition of building, that cost falls to you — and it can range from $15,000 to $50,000 or more depending on the length of the service run and whether road cutting is involved.

Also confirm hydro and gas service entry points, any existing easements for utilities crossing the lot, and the location of the municipal water shutoff. These details affect both design and budget.

Site conditions

A geotechnical assessment is worth commissioning on any infill lot where the soil conditions are unknown. Ravine-adjacent lots, lots in low-lying areas, and lots near former industrial land all carry particular risks. Toronto has significant areas of fill material from past development activity, and building on fill requires engineered foundations that add cost. The Toronto and Region Conservation Authority (TRCA) has jurisdiction over lots within regulated areas near ravines, valleys, and watercourses — a TRCA permit may be required before you can build, and TRCA setback requirements can substantially reduce your buildable envelope.

Before you make an offer: Have an architect or experienced builder review the zoning, survey, and any available service records before you finalize a purchase price for an infill lot. The cost is typically $1,500–$3,000 for a preliminary feasibility review and can save you from overpaying for a lot that will not accommodate the home you want to build.

Navigating Toronto's planning and approvals process

For many infill lots, zoning compliance is achievable as-of-right — meaning the home you want to build meets the zoning requirements without needing any special approvals beyond the standard building permit. But a significant proportion of infill projects in Toronto require some form of planning approval, either because the proposed building exceeds the as-of-right permissions or because the lot itself was created through a severance that requires approval.

Committee of Adjustment

The most common planning approval for single-lot infill is a minor variance application to the Committee of Adjustment. Minor variances are used when your proposed building complies with the general intent of the zoning but deviates from one or more specific numeric requirements — a side-yard setback that is 1.2 metres instead of the required 1.5 metres, for example, or a building height that is 9.5 metres in a zone that permits 9 metres.

The Committee of Adjustment meets regularly and decisions are generally issued within 60–90 days of application. Applications require drawings prepared by an architect or designer, a fee to the City (currently $1,500–$4,000 depending on the number of variances), notice to adjacent property owners, and a hearing at which neighbours can speak in support or opposition. The Committee applies a four-part test: is the variance minor, desirable for the land, in keeping with the general intent of the Official Plan, and in keeping with the general intent of the zoning? Well-designed applications with good neighbourhood support typically succeed. Contentious applications — particularly those involving significant massing increases or neighbourhood opposition — may be refused or sent back for revision.

Lot severances

If you are creating a new infill lot by severing the rear portion of an existing property, you need a consent application (commonly called a severance) through the Committee of Adjustment as well. Severances are assessed based on whether the new lot meets zoning requirements, whether the existing lot after severance continues to meet zoning requirements, and whether the severance is consistent with the character of the neighbourhood. The process typically takes 3–6 months and involves conditions — such as registering the title transfer within a set period — that must be fulfilled before the severance is final.

Building permits

Once planning approvals are in place, a building permit application is filed with the City of Toronto Building Division. For a new custom home, this requires architectural drawings, structural drawings, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing drawings, and an energy compliance submission under the Ontario Building Code. Permit review times in Toronto currently run 6–10 weeks for residential new builds, though projects in heritage conservation districts or that involve complex structural conditions can take longer.

The building permit fee is calculated based on the value of construction — for a new home valued at $1.5 million in construction cost, expect a permit fee in the range of $20,000–$30,000. This is not negotiable and must be paid before the permit is issued.

Infill development costs in Toronto: what to budget

The total cost of an infill build in Toronto is the sum of land acquisition, soft costs (design, engineering, approvals), hard construction costs, and carrying costs during the development period. Each category is significant and each has a meaningful range depending on the complexity of the project.

A realistic all-in budget for a 2,500 sq ft custom infill home in Toronto — exclusive of land — ranges from $1.8 million to $2.8 million depending on specification level, site complexity, and whether planning approvals are required. High-specification builds on complex sites in premium neighbourhoods can go higher. These numbers are as of mid-2026 and reflect current labour and material pricing in the Toronto market.

The infill development timeline

One of the most consistent surprises for first-time infill developers is how long the process takes from lot purchase to occupancy. The approvals and design phases take more time than most people expect, and construction timelines for custom homes are longer than they are for production builds.

A realistic timeline for a straightforward infill project — no major planning approvals, clean site conditions — looks like this:

  • Months 1–2: Pre-purchase feasibility, lot assessment, survey, geotechnical if required. Architect selection and engagement.
  • Months 2–5: Schematic design and design development. Client review and revision cycles. If a minor variance is required, application prepared and submitted during this phase.
  • Months 5–8: Committee of Adjustment hearing and decision (if applicable). Construction document preparation across all disciplines. Building permit application submission.
  • Months 8–10: Permit review period. Contractor tendering and selection. Demolition of existing structure (if applicable). Site preparation.
  • Months 10–22: Construction. A 2,500–3,500 sq ft custom home in Toronto typically takes 12–18 months to build from permit issuance to occupancy.
  • Month 22–24: Occupancy permit, deficiency resolution, final landscaping.

Projects that require a severance, a zoning by-law amendment, or face TRCA involvement should add 6–12 months to the planning phase. A realistic total timeline from lot purchase to move-in for a complex infill project can reach 30–36 months.

On carrying costs: Every month between lot purchase and occupancy is a month of mortgage interest, property tax, and insurance without generating occupancy value. At Toronto land prices, the carrying cost on a $1.2 million lot at current interest rates runs $5,000–$7,000 per month. A project that takes 24 months from purchase to occupancy accumulates $120,000–$168,000 in carrying costs that belong in your budget. Approvals delays that extend the timeline are not abstract — they have a direct dollar cost.

Key design and construction considerations for infill lots

Party walls and neighbour impact

Building on a tight urban infill lot means your construction activity is immediately adjacent to occupied homes. Excavation, shoring, hoarding, concrete pours, and heavy equipment movement all affect neighbours directly. A builder experienced in urban infill will manage this proactively — pre-construction surveys of adjacent properties to document existing conditions, shoring designs that protect neighbouring foundations, site hoarding that meets City standards, and proactive communication with neighbours throughout the project. This is not optional courtesy; it is risk management. Neighbour complaints can trigger stop-work orders that cost far more in delays than the cost of proper site management.

Grading and stormwater management

Toronto's infill approval process increasingly requires a grading and drainage plan prepared by a civil engineer. The City's Green Standards requirements mean that new infill homes must manage stormwater on-site rather than directing runoff to the municipal sewer — this typically means designing a combination of grading, soakaway pits, or green roof elements to meet the required retention targets. On tight lots where the basement sits close to the water table, this can be a meaningful engineering challenge that needs to be resolved during design rather than during construction.

Tree protection

Toronto's Private Tree By-law protects trees on private property with a trunk diameter of 30 cm or greater at breast height. The City's Street Tree By-law protects trees on city property adjacent to your lot. Before any site work begins, trees meeting those thresholds must be assessed, and removal or injury permits must be obtained where applicable. Removal of a large tree requires City approval and typically triggers replacement planting requirements. Budget $3,000–$8,000 per significant tree for assessment, permits, and replacement planting, and plan the design to protect as many existing trees as possible — a mature tree is an asset, not an obstacle.

Working within heritage conservation districts

Many of Toronto's most desirable infill neighbourhoods — Cabbagetown, Roncesvalles, parts of Leslieville, Riverdale, South Rosedale — fall within or adjacent to heritage conservation districts (HCDs). Building within an HCD requires heritage permit approval in addition to a standard building permit, and the City's heritage guidelines impose design requirements around massing, materials, rooflines, and the relationship between the new build and existing street character. This does not mean you cannot build something contemporary; many excellent modern homes have been approved in HCDs. But it does mean the design process requires more iteration and that City heritage staff must be engaged early.

Choosing the right builder for an infill project

Infill construction in Toronto is not like building in a suburban subdivision. A builder who has managed fifty production homes in Markham may not have the experience needed to excavate adjacent to a century-old brick Victorian in the Annex, manage a Committee of Adjustment process, coordinate a team of engineers around a tight urban site, and deliver a finished custom home that satisfies a discerning client. The skills are genuinely different.

When evaluating builders for an infill project, look for:

  • A portfolio of completed infill projects in Toronto, ideally in neighbourhoods similar to yours. Ask to visit completed projects and speak with the clients.
  • Experience with the City of Toronto planning and permits process, including relationships with the Building Division and familiarity with committee of adjustment applications.
  • An established network of consultants — architects, structural engineers, civil engineers, geotechnical firms — who have worked together on urban infill projects. The coordination between these disciplines is where a lot of projects run into trouble.
  • Transparent cost management, including detailed estimates broken down by trade, a clear change order process, and willingness to provide references who can speak to how the builder handled budget and schedule on their project.
  • Adequate insurance coverage, including general liability with limits appropriate for urban construction (typically $5 million or more) and confirmation of WSIB coverage for all site workers.

Conclusion: infill development rewards preparation

Infill development in Toronto is one of the most compelling ways to build a custom home in an established, walkable neighbourhood — but it is not a straightforward process. The lots are constrained, the regulatory environment is complex, the timelines are long, and the costs are significant. Every one of those challenges is manageable with the right preparation and the right team. Every one of them becomes a serious problem if it is discovered late.

The clients who have the best experience with infill projects are the ones who started with a realistic assessment of what they were buying, engaged their builder and architect early in the process, budgeted conservatively, and planned for the fact that Toronto's approvals machine moves on its own schedule regardless of how urgent your timeline feels. The result, when done well, is a home that sits in the neighbourhood you wanted to live in, built to the specification you actually wanted, with no compromises forced by a production builder's standard package.
