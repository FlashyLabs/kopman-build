Garden suite zoning in Toronto: what's allowed and where

The City legalized garden suites city-wide in 2022. But legalized does not mean simple — here is what the zoning rules actually say, and what they mean when you are standing in your backyard trying to figure out what you can build.

Garden suites versus laneway suites: the distinction matters

Before getting into the zoning rules, it helps to establish exactly what a garden suite is, because the term gets used interchangeably with "laneway suite" in casual conversation and they are not the same thing. The City of Toronto treats them differently under the zoning bylaw, and the distinction affects where each type is permitted and what constraints apply.

A laneway suite is a detached residential unit built at the rear of a property that faces and has direct access to a public laneway. Laneways are those narrow service roads that run through the midblock of many older Toronto neighbourhoods — the ones used historically for garbage collection and utility access. Laneway suites were legalized city-wide in 2018.

A garden suite is also a detached residential unit in the rear yard, but it does not require laneway access. It sits entirely within the property and reaches the street through a pedestrian path alongside the main house. Garden suites were legalized city-wide in September 2022, opening up the backyard-suite option to the large majority of Toronto lots that do not have laneway frontage.

This distinction is meaningful. If your lot backs onto a laneway, you have both options available. If it does not, garden suites are your path. The zoning rules, while similar in spirit, differ in the specifics — and confusing the two when planning your project can lead to a design that does not comply.

Where garden suites are permitted in Toronto

Garden suites are now permitted as-of-right on most residential lots in Toronto. "As-of-right" means you do not need to apply for a rezoning or a minor variance to build one, provided your design meets all the applicable zoning standards. You still need a building permit — that is not optional — but you are not asking the City for permission to do something outside the rules. You are working within them.

The permissions apply broadly across residential zones, including most RD (Residential Detached), RS (Residential Semi-Detached), and RM (Residential Multiple) zones. The major exclusions are:

  • Lots already containing a laneway suite. You cannot have both a laneway suite and a garden suite on the same property.
  • Lots in the Neighbourhood Heritage Conservation Districts where local heritage overlays restrict new construction in rear yards. Check with Heritage Preservation Services early if your property is in a Heritage Conservation District.
  • Lots in the Ontario Heritage Act listings or with individual property designations that restrict alterations — rear yard construction included.
  • Certain floodplain and Environmental Protection zones mapped by the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority (TRCA), where any new residential construction may be prohibited or require additional TRCA approval.

In practical terms, the vast majority of standard residential lots in neighbourhoods like the Annex, Roncesvalles, East York, Danforth Village, North York, and Etobicoke are eligible. Properties in the Bridle Path or in areas with large irregular lots in the greenbelt are more likely to encounter constraints.

The fastest way to confirm eligibility: Use the City of Toronto's Zoning By-law Map (available at toronto.ca/zoning) and enter your address. It will show you the applicable zone and overlay designations. From there, cross-reference with the garden suite zoning standards in By-law 2022-900. If anything in the map output is unclear — heritage overlay, flood susceptibility area, special study area — get a pre-application consultation with City Planning before spending money on design.

The lot requirements: minimum size and dimensions

Not every eligible-zone lot can physically accommodate a garden suite. The City has set minimum lot size thresholds, and many narrower or shallower Toronto lots will not meet them without a variance.

Minimum lot area

Your lot must have a minimum area of 200 square metres (approximately 2,150 square feet) to permit a garden suite. This threshold is measured as the total lot area, not the area of the rear yard alone. Most standard Toronto lots in older residential neighbourhoods exceed this, but small infill lots, irregular corner cuts, and some downtown properties may not.

Minimum lot frontage

There is no explicit minimum frontage specified in the garden suite bylaw, but the setback requirements effectively establish a practical minimum. On a very narrow lot — say, less than 6 metres of frontage — the combination of side yard setbacks on both sides will eliminate any buildable area in the rear. On typical Toronto residential lots of 7.5 to 9 metres of frontage, a garden suite is feasible but the side yard setbacks will tightly constrain the footprint.

Rear yard depth

You need enough depth in the rear yard to satisfy both the setback from the rear property line and still have a footprint to build on. The minimum rear yard setback for a garden suite is 7.5 metres from the rear lot line on lots where the rear yard does not face a laneway. Given that most garden suites are designed at 40 to 60 square metres of floor area, you generally need a rear yard depth of at least 15 to 18 metres to have a workable building and some outdoor space remaining. Shallower rear yards are the single most common deal-breaker we encounter at Kopman Build.

Setbacks, height limits, and GFA rules

Assuming your lot clears the minimum size thresholds, the zoning envelope for a garden suite is defined by four sets of rules: setbacks, maximum height, maximum gross floor area, and lot coverage. Understanding each of these before you engage a designer will save you from wasted drawing fees.

Setbacks

  • Rear lot line setback: 7.5 metres minimum. This is the most consequential constraint on most lots.
  • Side lot line setbacks: 1.5 metres on each side for a one-storey garden suite. For a two-storey or loft suite, the side setback increases to 1.8 metres from each side lot line above the first storey. On a 6-metre-wide lot, a two-storey suite is therefore physically impossible unless you use a minor variance.
  • Separation from the main house: The garden suite must be set back at least 5 metres from the rear wall of the principal dwelling. This is separate from the rear lot line setback and catches many homeowners by surprise. On a typical 30-metre deep lot, the buildable zone for a garden suite is roughly between the 5-metre separation from the house and the 7.5-metre setback from the rear line — a roughly 17-metre band to work within.

Maximum height

Garden suites are capped at a maximum height of 6 metres, measured from established grade to the top of the roof. For a sloped or peaked roof, the measurement is to the mid-point of the slope. This effectively limits you to a two-storey structure with modest ceiling heights or a single-storey with a loft. A flat-roof design has more usable volume within the 6-metre envelope than a pitched-roof design — a consideration worth discussing with your architect early.

Maximum gross floor area (GFA)

The gross floor area of a garden suite is capped at the lesser of 60 square metres or 40% of the rear yard area. For most standard 30-foot Toronto lots, the 60-square-metre cap is the binding constraint. On larger lots with deeper rear yards, the 40% rear yard rule may be more restrictive. Either way, you are building a compact unit. A well-designed 55-square-metre garden suite with a loft can be a genuinely comfortable one-bedroom, but if your goal is a spacious two-bedroom unit, the zoning envelope will be a limiting factor.

Lot coverage

The garden suite's footprint contributes to your overall lot coverage calculation. In most residential zones, the combined lot coverage of the main house, any existing ancillary structures, and the new garden suite cannot exceed 33% of the lot area. On lots where the main house already occupies a significant portion of the lot — common in denser neighbourhoods where coverage was historically applied more liberally — the residual coverage available for a garden suite can be quite small. Run the numbers before you get attached to a particular footprint size.

Cost breakdown: what garden suites actually cost to build

We build garden and laneway suites regularly at Kopman Build, and the honest answer on cost is that the range is wide. A simple single-storey slab-on-grade suite built with standard finishes is a very different project from a two-storey poured-concrete suite with radiant heating, high-end millwork, and custom windows. That said, here are realistic ranges based on what we are actually tendering and building in Toronto as of mid-2026:

Item Typical Range (CAD) Notes
Design & drawings (architect/designer) $15,000 – $30,000 More for complex sites or heritage adjacency requiring additional submissions
City permit fees $3,000 – $8,000 Scales with GFA; includes development charges where applicable
Site servicing (water, sewer, gas, electrical) $20,000 – $55,000 Largest variable; depends on distance from street and whether new service lateral required
Foundation (slab-on-grade) $18,000 – $32,000 Slab is most common; grade beam and piers for poor soil conditions adds cost
Structural framing & envelope $45,000 – $80,000 Includes framing, insulation, windows, roofing, exterior cladding
Mechanical (HVAC, plumbing, electrical) $40,000 – $65,000 Mini-split systems typical for HVAC; separate electrical panel and meter required
Interior finishes $35,000 – $75,000 Kitchens and bathrooms drive most of the variance in this category
Landscaping & site restoration $8,000 – $20,000 Includes access path, fence repairs, grading, and soft landscaping
Total all-in (typical range) $280,000 – $450,000+ Mid-complexity, good-quality finishes; simple suites closer to $250K; high-spec suites can exceed $500K

A few cost factors that consistently surprise clients: servicing is expensive and unpredictable. If the City requires a new water service lateral from the main under the street, that alone can add $30,000 to $50,000 to the project. Development charges in Toronto have also increased substantially and now typically run $10,000 to $25,000 for a new residential unit, depending on size. Understanding these costs upfront prevents very unpleasant mid-project surprises.

The approval and permit process, step by step

Even though garden suites are as-of-right under the zoning bylaw, the path from idea to occupancy involves a sequence of approvals and inspections. Here is how the process works in practice:

Step 1: Feasibility assessment (weeks 1–4)

Before engaging a designer, establish that your lot actually qualifies. Confirm the zone, check for overlay designations, measure your rear yard depth, calculate available lot coverage, and determine whether a separate servicing connection is feasible given where your current utilities enter the property. A good contractor or designer can help you with this quickly; the City also offers pre-application consultations through City Planning for more complex situations.

Step 2: Design and drawings (months 2–5)

Engage a licensed architect or experienced building designer to produce permit-ready drawings. For a garden suite, drawings typically include a site plan showing all setbacks and coverages, floor plans, elevations, a section, and structural drawings signed and sealed by an engineer. If your design is pushing against any of the zoning limits, the designer will identify whether a minor variance application is required before permit submission. Do not skip this step or try to work from rough sketches — the permit review will identify deficiencies and the revision cycle will cost more time than doing it right the first round.

Step 3: Permit submission and review (months 4–8)

Building permit applications for new residential structures typically take 3 to 6 months for a complete review at the City of Toronto Building Division. First-time submissions that require revisions can extend this timeline. Toronto has consistently had permit backlogs, and garden suite applications are not expedited. A complete and well-prepared submission is the best tool you have to minimize review time. Engage a designer who has successfully permitted garden suites before and understands what the City reviewers are looking for.

Step 4: Construction (months 8–18)

With permit in hand, construction on a typical garden suite takes 6 to 9 months from groundbreaking to substantial completion, depending on scope and site conditions. Access is often a constraint — materials need to come through the side of the house or through a gate, and in dense neighbourhoods, staging space is limited. A well-organized site plan matters more for a garden suite than for most other project types because the logistics are genuinely complicated.

Step 5: Inspections and occupancy (months 16–19)

City inspectors will visit at multiple stages of construction: after excavation and foundation forming, after rough-in framing, after rough mechanical installations, and at final completion. Electrical work is inspected and approved by the Electrical Safety Authority (ESA), not the City. Once all inspections are passed, you receive an Occupancy Permit. A separate unit registration may be required with the City's Rental Housing Licensing and Standards office depending on how the unit is used.

On development charges: The Province of Ontario passed legislation in 2022 exempting garden suites and laneway suites from development charges in many cases. However, the exemption has conditions and was subject to municipal implementation. As of 2026, confirm with the City at time of permit application what development charges apply to your specific project — this can meaningfully affect your total budget and should not be assumed to be zero.

Practical design considerations within the zoning envelope

The zoning rules create a tight envelope. Working intelligently within it is where good design makes the difference between a suite that feels cramped and one that feels considered and liveable. After building a number of these, a few design principles consistently produce better outcomes:

  • Prioritize ceiling height in the primary living space. A 55-square-metre suite with 9-foot ceilings reads very differently than the same area with 8-foot ceilings. If the 6-metre height limit allows it, spending height on the main living area pays back in perceived spaciousness far more than spending it on a roof pitch for curb appeal that nobody sees from the street.
  • Consider a loft rather than a full second storey. A single-storey suite with a sleeping loft can get you close to the same usable area as a two-storey design while keeping the overall massing lower, reducing the visual impact on neighbours, and simplifying the structure. The side setback rules are also more permissive for single-storey structures.
  • Treat the access path as part of the design. The pedestrian path from the front of the property to the garden suite typically runs along one side of the main house. It needs to be at least 0.9 metres wide and should be integrated into the landscape design from the outset rather than treated as an afterthought. A well-designed approach creates privacy and a sense of arrival for the suite occupant.
  • Get the mechanical and electrical right from the start. Garden suites require their own electrical panel, separate utility metering if being rented, and an independent HVAC system. Mini-split heat pumps are the standard choice: efficient, compact, and capable of both heating and cooling. Plan the mechanical room or HVAC location during design, not during construction. Mechanical changes in a 55-square-metre building have cascading effects on everything else.
  • Think carefully about privacy for both units. The orientation of windows, the placement of the suite on the lot, and the design of outdoor areas all affect whether the main house and garden suite occupants can live comfortably without feeling like they are living on top of each other. This matters most for rental uses, but it matters for family accommodation too.

Garden suites versus other intensification options

Garden suites are one of several options Toronto homeowners now have for adding a secondary unit. Comparing them against the alternatives is worth doing before committing to a direction:

  • Garden suite vs. basement apartment: A basement apartment is typically less expensive to build ($80,000 to $180,000 for a well-done legal basement unit in Toronto) and does not require separate servicing. But it is attached to the main house, which limits flexibility and can complicate both living and selling. A garden suite is a fully detached unit with its own address potential — a fundamentally different asset class.
  • Garden suite vs. laneway suite: If you have laneway access, a laneway suite is operationally similar but benefits from that direct rear access for tenants, deliveries, and eventual future uses. If you do not have a laneway, a garden suite is the comparable option. Costs are similar; the main difference is access and the applicable zoning rules on setbacks.
  • Garden suite vs. addition to the main house: If your goal is additional space for family — a parents-in-law suite, a returning adult child, a home office — a rear addition to the main house can achieve a similar outcome at lower cost, with simpler permitting, and without the full separation that a detached suite requires. If your goal is rental income or a truly independent unit, the garden suite is the better path.

What Kopman Build recommends before you start

After 25 years building in Toronto, we see the same pattern repeatedly: homeowners who do thorough upfront work — confirming eligibility, understanding the cost landscape honestly, and choosing the right design team — have far better experiences than those who rush into design and permitting only to discover a constraint that changes the entire project.

The most useful things you can do before calling anyone are these:

  • Pull out a tape measure and measure your actual rear yard depth from the rear wall of the house to the rear lot line. If it is less than 20 metres, a garden suite is going to be tight and you should understand the constraints before getting excited.
  • Check the City's online zoning map at toronto.ca and enter your address. Note the zone designation and any overlay flags. Save a screenshot.
  • Walk the side yard of your house and visualize a pedestrian path 1 metre wide running from the front gate to the backyard. Is there room? Are there existing utilities, air conditioning units, or garden features that would need to move?
  • Have an honest conversation with yourself about the goal. Is this for rental income? Family accommodation? Future resale value? The goal affects the right size, finish level, and program for the suite, which in turn affects what you should budget and what timeline makes sense.
