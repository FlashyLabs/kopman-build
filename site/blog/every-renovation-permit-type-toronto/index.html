Every renovation permit type in Toronto explained

What each permit covers, what it costs, how long it takes to get, and exactly when your project triggers the requirement — from a team that has been pulling Toronto permits since 1999.

Why permits matter more than most people realize

The permit question comes up on virtually every project we start. Homeowners want to know: do I really need one for this? The honest answer is that the permit system exists to protect you, not to slow down your contractor. When a building inspector signs off on your structural work, your electrical rough-in, or your new plumbing stack, they are providing an independent verification that the work meets the Ontario Building Code. That matters when something goes wrong, when you sell, and when you file an insurance claim after a fire or flood.

After 25 years of renovating homes in Toronto, we have seen the consequences of skipped permits play out in predictable ways. A basement finished without a permit becomes a liability that surfaces in disclosure during a sale — and the buyers either walk or demand a price reduction that exceeds what the permit would have cost. A structural beam installed without inspection fails quietly for years before becoming a major problem. An unpermitted electrical panel becomes the reason an insurer denies a claim after a house fire. The permit fee is never the expensive part of the equation.

Toronto processes permits through the City of Toronto Building Division, which operates under the Ontario Building Code Act. The rules are not arbitrary — they are detailed, and they change periodically. What follows is a current overview as of mid-2026, but for anything project-specific, we strongly recommend confirming requirements directly with the Building Division or having your contractor do so on your behalf.

The building permit: the most common type

A building permit is the general-purpose permit that covers most structural, architectural, and envelope changes to a residential property. It is required whenever you are doing work that affects the structural integrity of the building, changes its footprint or height, adds new interior space, or alters fire separations and means of egress.

What triggers a building permit

The list of work that requires a building permit in Toronto is longer than most homeowners expect. Common triggers include:

  • Home additions: Any increase to the building's gross floor area, whether it is a rear addition, a side addition, a second-storey addition, or a garage conversion to living space. No exceptions — this always requires a permit.
  • Basement underpinning or benching: Lowering the basement floor to increase headroom is structural work that always requires a building permit, and typically an engineering report as well.
  • Interior structural changes: Removing or altering load-bearing walls, adding or moving beams, modifying the floor or roof structure.
  • Garage construction: Attached or detached garages, carports, and accessory structures over 10 square metres.
  • Decks over 600mm above grade: Any deck that exceeds 600mm (roughly 24 inches) in height above the adjacent grade requires a building permit. Low-level decks are exempt.
  • New windows or doors in new openings: Replacing a window in an existing opening without structural change is typically exempt. Creating a new opening in a wall is not.
  • Change of use: Converting a single-family home into a duplex or adding a legal secondary suite, including basement apartments.
  • Laneway houses and garden suites: These require a building permit along with zoning review. Toronto's laneway suite program has specific design requirements and approval steps that run concurrently with the permit process.
  • Demolition of more than 50% of exterior walls: Major gut renovations that retain the foundation but replace most of the structure above grade.

A building permit in Toronto is calculated as a percentage of the estimated construction value, currently set at approximately $20.53 per $1,000 of construction value for residential projects, subject to a minimum fee. A $200,000 rear addition will generate a permit fee in the range of $4,000 to $5,500, depending on how construction value is assessed and whether any zoning review fees apply separately.

The electrical permit: required more often than not

Electrical permits in Ontario are issued by the Electrical Safety Authority (ESA), not the City of Toronto Building Division. This is a distinction that confuses many homeowners who assume one permit covers everything. For any work involving your home's wiring, panel, or electrical systems, you need a separate ESA permit, and the work must be inspected by an ESA inspector once complete.

When an electrical permit is required

  • Panel upgrades and replacements: Upgrading from a 100-amp to a 200-amp service, or replacing an outdated Federal Pacific or Zinsco panel, always requires an ESA permit.
  • New circuits: Adding dedicated circuits for kitchen appliances, EV chargers, hot tubs, electric furnaces, or high-draw bathroom fixtures.
  • Rough-in for new spaces: Any new wiring as part of a basement finish, addition, or major renovation.
  • Knob-and-tube replacement: Older Toronto homes often have knob-and-tube wiring. Replacing it — which most insurers now require — requires a full ESA permit and inspection.
  • EV charging installation: Level 2 EV charger installation requires an ESA permit in Ontario regardless of whether the charger itself is hardwired or plug-in.

ESA permit fees for residential work are set provincially and are relatively modest. A standard rough-in permit for a basement finish runs approximately $200 to $400 depending on the number of outlets and circuits. A panel upgrade permit is in the $150 to $250 range. The ESA fee is separate from the electrician's labour and is typically included in the electrician's total quote.

Important: Only a licensed electrical contractor (EC) registered with the ESA can pull an electrical permit in Ontario. Homeowners can obtain an owner-builder electrical permit for their own principal residence, but the work must still be inspected. For any renovation involving a contractor, the electrical permit must be in the contractor's name or that of their licensed electrical subcontractor.

The plumbing permit: tied to the building permit but distinct

Plumbing permits in Toronto are issued by the City of Toronto Building Division and are often processed concurrently with the building permit, but they cover a distinct scope of work. Any change to the drain, waste, and vent (DWV) system or the potable water supply within the home requires a plumbing permit. Purely cosmetic fixture swaps — replacing a toilet with another toilet in the same location, for example — do not.

What triggers a plumbing permit

  • Adding a new bathroom, wet bar, or laundry rough-in in a basement or addition
  • Moving an existing kitchen sink or bathroom fixtures to a new location
  • Installing or replacing a backwater valve or sump pump with new drains
  • Replacing or extending the sanitary drain stack
  • Installing in-floor radiant heating that uses a hydronic (water-based) system
  • Any connection to the City's municipal water or sewer services, including new sewer laterals

Plumbing permit fees in Toronto are calculated per fixture and per connection. A typical basement bathroom rough-in (toilet, shower, sink) runs approximately $250 to $450 in permit fees. A more complex scope involving a new stack and multiple fixtures can reach $800 or more. These fees are modest relative to the cost of the plumbing labour itself.

The HVAC / mechanical permit

Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning work falls under the mechanical permit category in Toronto. This permit is required whenever new ductwork is added, a furnace or air handler is replaced, or a new ventilation system is installed. Like plumbing, mechanical permits are often rolled into the building permit application on larger projects.

For standalone HVAC replacements — swapping an old gas furnace for a new one in the same location — a mechanical permit is typically still required if the unit's capacity or fuel type is changing significantly. In practice, reputable HVAC contractors in Toronto include the permit as a standard part of any furnace or heat pump installation. If your HVAC quote does not mention a permit, ask about it explicitly.

The demolition permit

A demolition permit is required for the full or partial demolition of a structure in Toronto. Full demolition of a detached house, garage, or accessory structure always requires one. Partial demolition — removing more than 50% of the exterior walls while retaining the foundation — also triggers this requirement. Interior demolition (gutting finishes down to the studs) generally does not require a separate demolition permit, though it may be part of a broader building permit application.

Demolition permits have a flat fee structure and are processed relatively quickly. A full residential demolition permit runs approximately $400 to $600. However, the permit is only one element of a demolition project — Toronto also requires disconnection of all utilities (gas, water, hydro) before demolition begins, and there are specific requirements for asbestos and designated substances surveys on any pre-1980 structure.

Heritage properties: a separate approval layer

Toronto has one of the largest inventories of designated heritage properties in Canada. If your home is individually designated under the Ontario Heritage Act, or if it sits within a Heritage Conservation District (HCD) such as Cabbagetown, Rosedale, Wychwood, or Kensington Market, virtually any exterior alteration — including window replacement, cladding, roof material, porches, and additions — requires a Heritage Alteration Permit in addition to the standard building permit.

Heritage applications go to the City's Heritage Preservation Services division and, depending on the scope, may also require approval from the Toronto Preservation Board or City Council. On additions and significant alterations, the heritage review alone can add four to six months to the permitting timeline. This is not unusual — it is the reality of working on a designated property in Toronto, and it needs to be accounted for in project scheduling from the very beginning.

Many properties in established Toronto neighbourhoods are on the Heritage Register but are not individually designated — these are "listed" properties. Listed properties have some protections, particularly against demolition, but do not trigger the full heritage alteration permit process for interior renovations. Confirm your property's status before assuming either way.

Zoning variances and the Committee of Adjustment: A building permit only confirms that your project meets the Ontario Building Code — it does not address zoning compliance. If your proposed addition exceeds lot coverage limits, encroaches on setbacks, or exceeds height restrictions in your zoning category, you will need a Minor Variance from the Committee of Adjustment before the building permit can be issued. Minor variances add three to five months to the timeline and involve a public hearing. Factor this in early.

What happens if you skip a permit

The consequences of unpermitted work in Toronto are concrete, not theoretical. Here is what we have seen play out over 25 years of practice:

  • Order to comply: If a City inspector or bylaw enforcement officer discovers unpermitted work, they can issue an Order to Comply requiring you to obtain a permit retroactively, open up completed work for inspection (at your cost), and correct any code deficiencies found. Retroactive permits are more expensive and more difficult to obtain than permits pulled before work begins.
  • Stop-work orders: On active construction sites, discovery of unpermitted work results in a stop-work order. The project stops entirely until the permit situation is resolved. On a $500,000 renovation, a three-week delay while you sort out a permit costs real money in carrying costs, trade scheduling delays, and potential damage to exposed work.
  • Insurance implications: Most home insurance policies in Ontario exclude coverage for losses caused by or related to work that required a permit but didn't have one. A fire traced to an unpermitted electrical panel may result in a denied claim. This is not a hypothetical scenario — it happens.
  • Real estate disclosure: Ontario requires sellers to disclose material facts about a property, and unpermitted work is a material fact. If the buyer discovers unpermitted work during the inspection, they can renegotiate the price or rescind the offer. If they discover it after closing, they may have grounds for a legal claim against you.
  • Municipal fines: Under the Ontario Building Code Act, fines for proceeding without a permit can reach up to $50,000 for an individual. While maximum fines are rarely levied on residential homeowners for good-faith errors, fines in the $500 to $5,000 range are not uncommon for deliberate permit avoidance.

How the permit process actually works on a renovation project

For a typical Toronto renovation — say, a rear addition with a kitchen reconfiguration and new basement bathroom — the permit process unfolds in roughly the following sequence:

  1. Design and drawings: Before any permit application is submitted, you need permit-ready architectural drawings prepared by a registered architect or designer qualified under the OBC. For additions and structural changes, you will also need engineering drawings stamped by a Professional Engineer. This design phase typically takes four to eight weeks depending on complexity.
  2. Application submission: The permit application is submitted through the City's online Permit Portal, along with drawings, a construction value estimate, and applicable forms. Applications for residential additions and alterations are reviewed by a plans examiner, who checks for code compliance. Complex projects may go through multiple review cycles with comments.
  3. Permit issuance: Once the plans examiner approves the drawings, the permit is issued and construction can begin. Keep the permit and approved drawings on site for the duration of construction — inspectors will want to see them.
  4. Inspections: Building inspections are required at specific stages: typically after demolition/excavation, after structural framing, after rough-in of mechanical/plumbing/electrical (before walls are closed), and at substantial completion. You must call for each inspection and receive sign-off before proceeding to the next stage.
  5. Occupancy and final: For additions that add new living space, an occupancy permit is required before the new space can be used. The final inspection and permit close-out confirm that all work is complete and compliant.

What your contractor should handle

On any project we run at Kopman Build, we handle the full permit process on behalf of the client. That means coordinating with the architect and engineer on permit-ready drawings, submitting the application, responding to plans examiner comments, scheduling all required inspections, and managing the close-out. Clients should not have to navigate this process themselves.

There is a practice in the industry — more common among lower-cost operators — of asking the homeowner to pull permits as the owner-builder. This transfers legal responsibility for code compliance from the contractor to you. It also means that if the work is deficient and discovered later, you, not the contractor, are on the hook. We do not operate this way. If a contractor asks you to pull your own permits on work they are performing, treat it as a red flag.

The permit fees themselves are a pass-through on our projects — they are included in the project budget, paid directly by us, and reconciled in the final billing. There are no surprises. We have a working relationship with the City's Building Division and with the ESA inspectors who cover our neighbourhoods, and that relationship speeds up inspections and resolves plan review questions faster than a homeowner navigating the system for the first time.

Conclusion: the permit is the foundation of a well-built project

A renovation permit in Toronto is not red tape for its own sake. It is the mechanism by which independent, qualified inspectors verify that the work in your home is safe, structurally sound, and consistent with the code that protects occupants and neighbours alike. The cost of permits — even for a large addition — is typically less than one percent of the total project cost. The cost of not having them, when things go wrong, is almost always larger.
