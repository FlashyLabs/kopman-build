How to find a good renovation contractor in Toronto

A practical guide from 25 years in the industry — what to look for, what to ask, and which red flags are actually red flags.

The contractor matters more than the design

Most Toronto homeowners spend months choosing tile patterns, cabinet finishes, and lighting fixtures before they spend a single hour vetting the contractor who will actually build it. That is the wrong order of operations. A mediocre contractor with a beautiful design will produce a mediocre result. A great contractor with a simple design will produce something you are proud of for decades.

This is not a knock on design — design matters enormously. But a renovation lives or dies on execution: whether the rough-in is done right before the walls close up, whether the waterproofing in the shower will last, whether the contractor actually managed the subcontractors and the schedule or just hoped everything worked out. Those things are determined entirely by who you hire, not by what you chose from the sample book.

After 25 years doing this in Toronto, the pattern is consistent: clients who came to us after a difficult experience with another contractor almost always say the same thing. The pricing looked reasonable. The portfolio looked fine. But nobody asked the hard questions upfront, and by the time the problems showed up, tens of thousands of dollars were already spent.

This guide is designed to help you ask those questions before you sign anything.

Where to find candidates

The best source for a renovation contractor in Toronto is still a personal referral from someone whose project you have actually seen. Not a recommendation from someone who had work done five years ago and vaguely remembers it going well — a referral from someone whose finished basement or kitchen addition you can walk through, where you can see the quality of the trim work, open the cabinet doors, and ask direct questions about how the project actually went.

If you don't have that kind of referral in your immediate circle, expand it. Neighbours are underrated. If you have admired a renovation on your street, knock on the door. Most homeowners are happy to share their contractor's contact information, and they will tell you the truth about the experience in a way that no review platform can replicate.

Online platforms

HomeStars and Houzz are the two most useful platforms for finding Toronto renovation contractors. HomeStars in particular has a critical mass of local reviews and allows you to filter by trade, neighbourhood, and project type. Treat the ratings as a signal, not a verdict. A contractor with 50 reviews averaging 9.2 is meaningfully different from one with 6 reviews averaging 10 — the latter tells you very little.

Google reviews matter as well, and they are harder to game than some platform-specific review systems. Search the company name and look at both the average and the text of the reviews. One-star reviews that mention "wouldn't return calls" or "left the job unfinished" are more informative than five-star reviews that just say "great work."

A note on online leads: Platforms that connect you to multiple contractors simultaneously — where you submit a request and five contractors call you within the hour — tend to attract contractors who compete on price rather than quality. Use them if you must, but treat any contact they generate with extra scrutiny during vetting.

Reading a portfolio and checking references

A contractor's portfolio tells you what they have built. References tell you what it was like to work with them. Both matter, but references are more predictive of your experience.

When reviewing a portfolio, look for projects that are similar in scope and complexity to yours. A contractor who builds beautiful custom homes may not be the right fit for a targeted bathroom renovation, and vice versa. Look for consistency across projects — a portfolio with five exceptional photos and twenty mediocre ones tells you something different than one where every project looks carefully executed.

Questions to ask references

Always ask for references, and always call them. When you do, go beyond "were you happy with the work?" The most useful questions are:

  • Did the project come in on or near the original budget? Cost overruns happen, but large ones — 30% or more over estimate — often indicate a contractor who underbid to win the job or who has poor cost control. Ask how change orders were handled.
  • Did the project finish on or near the original schedule? Delays are common in renovation, but chronic lateness points to poor project management or a contractor who has taken on more work than they can handle.
  • Was the site kept reasonably clean and organized during the work? This is a proxy for professionalism. Contractors who run tight, organized job sites tend to produce better-quality work.
  • How did the contractor handle problems when they came up? Problems always come up. What you want to know is whether they were communicated clearly and resolved fairly, or whether you had to chase the contractor for answers.
  • Would you hire them again? This is the single most informative question. A qualified "yes, but..." tells you as much as a flat "yes."

What to ask before you sign

Once you have a shortlist of two or three candidates, there are several things you need to know before you can make a sound decision.

Insurance and WSIB

Any legitimate general contractor operating in Ontario must carry general liability insurance and must be registered with the Workplace Safety and Insurance Board (WSIB), or provide proof that all subcontractors they use are covered. Ask for certificates of insurance directly — not just confirmation that they have coverage. A reputable contractor will hand these over without hesitation. If there is any resistance or delay, that is your answer.

Liability coverage protects you if a worker is injured on your property or if a structural problem causes damage. Without it, you can be personally liable. This is not a box-ticking exercise; it is genuinely important.

How many projects are running concurrently?

A contractor who is managing ten active job sites simultaneously cannot give your project the attention it needs. Ask directly: how many projects are currently underway, and how is your time divided between them? There is no magic number, but you want to understand whether you will have consistent access to the person making decisions, or whether you will primarily be dealing with a crew that has limited autonomy to solve problems.

Who will supervise the site day-to-day?

Find out whether the contractor or owner will be on site regularly, or whether they will be managing the project remotely through a site supervisor. If there is a supervisor, ask to meet them before you sign. The person who physically runs your job site has an enormous influence on quality and scheduling. Knowing who that person is — and whether you trust them — matters.

How are change orders handled?

Scope changes are inevitable on renovation projects, particularly in older Toronto homes where you never fully know what is behind the walls. Ask the contractor to walk you through their change order process: how changes are documented, how pricing is determined, and how approval works before work proceeds. A contractor who handles changes informally — "we'll sort it out at the end" — is setting up a dispute. Changes should be documented in writing with agreed pricing before any additional work is done.

What is the payment schedule?

A reasonable payment schedule is tied to project milestones, not to arbitrary dates. A typical structure might look like: a deposit at signing, a draw at completion of demolition and rough-in, a draw at drywall and finishes, and a final payment at substantial completion with a holdback retained until all deficiencies are resolved. In Ontario, a statutory holdback of 10% is required under the Construction Act for larger projects. Even on smaller jobs, keeping a meaningful amount back until you are satisfied gives you leverage to ensure deficiencies are addressed promptly.

Red flags that are actually red flags

The renovation industry in Toronto, like any major city, has contractors who will take your money and underdeliver. Some warning signs are well-known but worth repeating, because they keep working on homeowners who haven't heard them before.

  • A large cash deposit upfront. A deposit of 10–15% is normal and reasonable. A demand for 30%, 40%, or 50% before work begins is not. Legitimate contractors have established relationships with suppliers and do not need your money to float their materials.
  • Suggesting you skip the permit. Renovation permits in Toronto exist for a reason, and skipping them creates real problems: your work is not inspected, your home insurance may be voided if something goes wrong, and you will have to disclose the unpermitted work when you sell. Any contractor who suggests bypassing permits as a way to save money or time is telling you something important about how they operate.
  • No written contract, or a vague one. "We've always worked on a handshake" is not a reassurance — it is a liability. A contract protects both parties. If a contractor is resistant to putting the details in writing, walk away.
  • Quotes that are significantly lower than the others. If you get three quotes and one is 40% below the other two, the low quote is almost certainly missing scope, planning to use inferior materials, or counting on change orders to make up the margin. A competitive price is reasonable; an inexplicably low price is a problem.
  • Pressure to decide quickly. Urgency tactics — "I have another client who wants this slot," "pricing goes up next week" — are designed to prevent you from doing proper due diligence. A contractor confident in their work and reputation does not need to rush you.
  • No physical address or established online presence. Not every excellent contractor has a polished website, but some form of verifiable history — reviews, a registered business address, a portfolio with identifiable projects — should exist. A brand-new business with no history is a risk.

What a good contract must include

A renovation contract that protects you is detailed, specific, and boring. It should not leave anything material to verbal agreement or good faith. At minimum, your contract should include:

  • A detailed scope of work, including specific materials, brands, and models where applicable
  • A project start date and estimated substantial completion date
  • A milestone-based payment schedule with specific holdback terms
  • A clear change order process: how changes are documented, priced, and approved in writing before proceeding
  • What is explicitly excluded from scope, to avoid disputes about what was assumed to be included
  • The contractor's WSIB account number and confirmation of liability insurance coverage amounts
  • A dispute resolution process
  • Warranty terms for both labour and materials

If the contractor provides a contract that is two paragraphs long, ask for a more detailed one. If they cannot or will not provide it, that tells you something about how the project will be managed when things get complicated.

On permits specifically: In Toronto, most structural work, additions, new plumbing, and electrical changes require a permit through the City of Toronto Building Division. Your contractor should be able to tell you exactly what permits are required for your project and should pull them in their own name — not ask you to pull them as the homeowner, which transfers liability back to you.

Finding someone you can actually work with

The technical and legal checklist matters. But so does fit. A renovation is a months-long working relationship conducted inside your home, often while you are living in it. You want a contractor who communicates the way you communicate: who returns calls and emails promptly, who tells you when something unexpected has come up rather than hoping you won't notice, and who treats your home with the same care they would bring to their own.

The best indicator of this is how a contractor behaves before you sign. Do they show up on time for the estimate? Do they ask good questions about how you use the space? Do they offer perspectives on the design that reflect real-world construction knowledge? Or do they just take measurements and disappear with a number? The pre-contract interaction is a preview of the relationship you will have once the work begins.
